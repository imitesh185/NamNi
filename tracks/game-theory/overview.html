Game Theory & Signalling

Game theory is the mathematics of situations where your best move depends on someone else's best move, and theirs depends on yours. The instant that circularity appears, ordinary optimisation stops working — there is no "best" action to compute, only a fixed point to find. Signalling is the branch that asks the harder question underneath: when nobody can verify what you claim to be, what makes a claim worth believing?

The one idea to carry. Rational players, each doing the best they can, routinely arrive somewhere all of them dislike. Bad outcomes do not require bad actors. They fall out of the structure of the payoffs — which means the lever is almost never persuasion, and almost always a change to the game.

00 Why this exists

Three claims that justify the field.

1 Interdependence breaks ordinary reasoning. Deciding what to do requires modelling what they will do, which requires modelling what they think you will do. Left unstructured this regresses forever. Equilibrium is the device that cuts the regress by asking for a set of expectations that are self-consistent.
2 Individually rational, collectively terrible. The Prisoner's Dilemma is not a puzzle about criminals. It is a proof that the sum of locally optimal choices can be globally worst — arms races, overfishing, price wars, unmaintained code. No villain is required.
3 Words are free, so words are ignored. Anything you can say costs nothing to say, therefore a liar can say it too, therefore it carries no information. What survives is what a liar could not afford to imitate. This single asymmetry generates credentials, warranties, peacock tails and hostage-taking.
The practical payoff Four questions you can answer before acting: (a) Who are the players, really — including the ones not in the room? (b) What is each one's payoff, as opposed to their stated position? (c) Is this played once or repeatedly, and does everyone know which? (d) What can I do that someone weaker than me could not do?

01 Anatomy of a game

Four components. Getting them wrong is the usual source of a wrong answer.

Players
Every party whose choices affect payoffs. The commonest modelling error is omitting one — the regulator, the other candidate, your future self.
Strategies
A complete plan of action, specifying what you do at every point you might be asked to move. Not a single choice — a full contingent plan.
Payoffs
Preferences over outcomes, on an ordinal or expected-utility scale. Payoffs are not money; reputation, spite and fairness sit inside them.
Information
Who knows what, and who knows that others know. Common knowledge means everyone knows, everyone knows everyone knows, ad infinitum — a far stronger condition than "everyone was told".

Two structural splits matter more than any other. Simultaneous games (you move without seeing their move — normal form, the payoff matrix) versus sequential games (you observe, then respond — extensive form, the tree). And complete information (payoffs known to all) versus incomplete information (types are private — this is where signalling lives).

Modelling discipline "Simultaneous" does not mean same instant. It means neither party observes the other's choice before committing. Sealed bids submitted a week apart are simultaneous. A reply sent one second later, having read theirs, is sequential.

02 Dominance and Nash equilibrium

The solution concept the whole field is built on, and the thing it does not promise.

A strategy is dominant if it beats your alternatives regardless of what anyone else does. If you have one, the game is easy — play it. The Prisoner's Dilemma's cruelty is that defection is dominant for both, so the reasoning is trivial and the outcome is bad.

They cooperateThey defect
You cooperate3, 30, 5
You defect5, 01, 1

Amber = the Nash equilibrium. Green = the outcome both players prefer. That they are different cells is the dilemma.

Most games have no dominant strategy. Nash equilibrium generalises: a profile of strategies where no player can improve by changing theirs alone. It is a mutual best-response, a fixed point of the "what should I do given what they do" map.

Nash's theorem (1950). Every finite game — any number of players, any finite strategy sets — has at least one equilibrium, provided mixed strategies are allowed. Proved on one and a half pages via a fixed-point theorem. Nobel 1994.

Nash, J. (1950). Equilibrium points in n-person games. PNAS 36(1), 48–49.

What equilibrium does not mean Not optimal. Not fair. Not unique. Not stable against coalitions. Not necessarily what people do. It means exactly one thing: no individual can profit by deviating on their own. Every popular misuse of "Nash equilibrium" comes from importing a guarantee that isn't there.

03 The five games worth memorising

Each isolates one difficulty. Almost every real situation is one of these wearing a costume.

G1Prisoner's Dilemma

Mutual defection is the unique equilibrium and is worse for everyone than mutual cooperation.

Difficulty
Cooperation is individually irrational even when collectively obvious.
In the wild
Arms races, price wars, overfishing, technical debt, doping.
The exit
Repetition, enforceable contracts, or changing the payoffs. Never exhortation.
G2Stag Hunt

Two equilibria: both hunt stag (higher payoff) or both hunt hare (safer). Stag pays more but fails if the other defects.

Difficulty
Not greed — trust. Payoff-dominance versus risk-dominance.
In the wild
Startup co-founders, standards adoption, bank runs, joint migrations.
The exit
Assurance. Anything that raises confidence the other will show up.
G3Chicken / Hawk–Dove

Both swerving is fine, one swerving is a win for the other, neither swerving is catastrophic.

Difficulty
The winner is whoever credibly cannot yield. Weakness of will is strength.
In the wild
Brinkmanship, strikes, deadline chicken, territorial disputes.
The exit
Visible commitment — see §06.
G4Battle of the Sexes

Both want to coordinate, but on different options. Any coordination beats none.

Difficulty
Coordination with conflicting preferences. Two equilibria, and a fight over which.
In the wild
Meeting venues, API standards, which team's tooling wins.
The exit
Focal points, turn-taking, or moving first.

Pure conflict. Whatever you do, they want the opposite. No equilibrium in pure strategies.

Difficulty
Forces mixed strategies: the only equilibrium is to randomise.
In the wild
Penalty kicks, auditing, security patrols, bluffing.
The lesson
Predictability is itself exploitable. Being readable is a payoff loss.

04 Repetition changes everything

The shadow of the future is the cheapest cooperation technology known.

Played once, defection is dominant. Played indefinitely, defection today costs you every future round of cooperation — and if the future matters enough, cooperation becomes an equilibrium. This is the folk theorem: with a high enough discount factor, almost any reasonable outcome can be sustained by the threat of future punishment.

Axelrod ran the question as a tournament. Researchers submitted strategies to play repeated Prisoner's Dilemma against each other. The winner, twice, was the shortest program entered — TIT FOR TAT: cooperate first, then copy the opponent's last move.

Why it won, in Axelrod's own decomposition:

Nice
Never defects first. Every high-scoring strategy in the tournament shared this.
Retaliatory
Punishes defection immediately, so it cannot be farmed.
Forgiving
Returns to cooperation the moment they do. Prevents death spirals.
Clear
Legible enough that the opponent can learn the rule. Unpredictability is worthless here — you want to be modelled correctly.

Axelrod, R. (1984). The Evolution of Cooperation. Basic Books. Tournament results first published in Journal of Conflict Resolution (1980).

Two honest caveats Mixed (1) Known finite horizon unravels it. If both know the last round, defection is optimal there; knowing that, the second-to-last unravels too, all the way back. Cooperation needs an open-ended or uncertain end. (2) TIT FOR TAT is not unbeatable. A Southampton team won the 2004 anniversary tournament by entering colluding programs that recognised each other and sacrificed themselves. Press & Dyson (2012, PNAS) later found "zero-determinant" strategies that can unilaterally set an opponent's payoff. The result was tournament-specific; the mechanism — future consequences make cooperation rational — is what generalises.

05 Sequential play and non-credible threats

Solve the tree backwards, and most threats evaporate.

When moves are observed, you solve by backward induction: start at the final decision, work out what that player does, replace the branch with its outcome, repeat. What survives is a subgame perfect equilibrium (Selten, Nobel 1994) — an equilibrium that remains sensible in every subgame, not just along the expected path.

Its main use is destructive. It deletes threats that would be irrational to carry out. "Match my price or I'll burn the market down" is a Nash equilibrium strategy but not subgame perfect, because when the moment arrives, burning the market hurts the threatener. A threat that is against your own interest to execute conveys nothing — unless you have removed your own ability to not execute it, which is the next section.

Where backward induction predicts badly Mixed In the centipede game the theory says take the money immediately; real players continue for many rounds and earn more. Backward induction assumes common knowledge of rationality through arbitrarily long chains — an assumption that breaks in humans well before round ten. Rosenthal (1981); McKelvey & Palfrey (1992), Econometrica.

06 Commitment — the strategic value of fewer options

Schelling's inversion, and the most counter-intuitive result in the field.

The power to constrain an adversary depends upon the power to bind oneself. — Thomas Schelling, The Strategy of Conflict (1960). Nobel 2005.

Ordinary reasoning says more options is better. In strategic interaction the reverse holds: options are a liability, because they can be used against you. If you are able to back down, they know you are able to back down, so they push. If you have burned the bridge, their push has nowhere to land.

Burn the bridge

Destroy the retreat physically. Cortés scuttling the ships. Deploying tripwire forces that cannot be withdrawn quietly.

Delegate to a rigid agent

"Policy forbids it." A negotiator with no authority to concede is stronger than one with full authority.

Make it public

Reputation cost converts a soft preference into a hard constraint. Announced positions are expensive to abandon.

Focal points

Schelling's other durable contribution. When players must coordinate without communicating, they converge on whatever is salient — not optimal, just conspicuous. Asked to meet a stranger in New York with no time or place agreed, a large majority named Grand Central at noon. Nothing in the payoff structure selects it. Shared culture does.

Why this matters outside game theory Focal points explain why arbitrary conventions are so stable: round numbers in pricing and salary, the status quo option, the incumbent, "whatever we did last time". Defaults win not because they are good but because they are obvious, and obviousness is what coordination runs on.

07 Asymmetric information and adverse selection

The market that destroys itself because quality is invisible.

Akerlof's used-car argument. Sellers know whether a car is good or a lemon; buyers do not. Buyers can only pay the average value. That price is below what a good car is worth, so owners of good cars withdraw. The average quality drops, so the price drops, so more good cars leave. The market can unravel to nothing while every participant behaves sensibly.

Akerlof, G. (1970). The market for "lemons": Quality uncertainty and the market mechanism. Quarterly Journal of Economics 84(3), 488–500. Nobel 2001, shared with Spence and Stiglitz. Foundational

The generalisation Wherever quality is private and unverifiable, the good side exits first. Insurance attracts the sick. Cheap freelance marketplaces attract the desperate. Job markets where nothing is checkable fill with people whose claims cannot be checked. The remedy is never trust — it is verification, warranty, or a costly signal.

08 Signalling — the informed side moves

The one condition that separates a signal from noise.

Spence's answer to the lemons problem: if you are the good type, do something that a bad type would find too expensive to copy. Education, in his model, raises wages partly independent of any skill it imparts — because it is cheaper to obtain for the able, so completing it sorts the population.

The single-crossing condition — the whole theory in one line A signal transmits information if and only if it is differentially costly: the cost of producing it must be lower for high types than for low types, by enough that low types decline to imitate. If the cost is the same for everyone, everyone sends it, and it means nothing.

Spence, M. (1973). Job market signaling. Quarterly Journal of Economics 87(3), 355–374. Foundational

Two equilibria are possible. In a separating equilibrium, types choose different signal levels and are correctly identified. In a pooling equilibrium, everyone sends the same signal and the market learns nothing — which is what happens to a credential once it becomes cheap enough that everyone acquires it.

Biology found it independently

Zahavi's handicap principle: the peacock's tail is honest because it is costly. Only a genuinely healthy bird can afford to drag it around. A gazelle that spots — leaping vertically on sighting a predator — is burning energy to announce that it has energy to burn, and predators preferentially chase the ones that don't. Waste is the mechanism, not a side effect.

Zahavi, A. (1975). Mate selection — a selection for a handicap. Journal of Theoretical Biology 53(1), 205–214. Formalised by Grafen (1990). Robust

Cheap talk

Costless, non-binding messages. Crawford and Sobel showed these convey real information only to the degree that the parties' interests already align. Where interests conflict, cheap talk degrades to noise — which is the formal reason unverifiable self-description is discounted to approximately zero.

Crawford, V. & Sobel, J. (1982). Strategic information transmission. Econometrica 50(6), 1431–1451.

Cheap talk
"I am highly proficient in distributed systems." Costs nothing to type. A person without the skill can type the identical sentence at identical cost. Information content: zero.
Costly signal
A repository with two years of commits; a specific number you can be interrogated on for forty minutes; a referral from someone spending their own reputation. Each is expensive for a low type to fake.
The negative signal A signal you point at but cannot back is worse than sending none. Silence leaves you unclassified; an invitation to verify that fails verification classifies you. Adverse selection punishes the unverifiable; failed verification punishes harder.

Countersignalling

The wrinkle. When quality is already evident by other means, the very highest types stop signalling — precisely to distinguish themselves from the anxious middle who must. Hence the worst-dressed person in the room being the most senior. Note the trap: countersignalling only works when your quality is already independently visible. Skipping the signal before that point is indistinguishable from having nothing to send.

Feltovich, N., Harbaugh, R. & To, T. (2002). Too cool for school? Signalling and countersignalling. RAND Journal of Economics 33(4), 630–649. Mixed

09 Screening — the uninformed side moves

Same information problem, opposite mover.

Signalling is the informed party proving their type. Screening is the uninformed party designing a menu that makes the types sort themselves. Insurance offers a high-deductible cheap plan and a low-deductible expensive one; the healthy pick the first, the sick the second, and the insurer learns a type it was never told.

Take-home taskUninformedTime cost is low for the able, prohibitive for the bluffing
WarrantyInformed (seller)Only affordable if the failure rate really is low

Rothschild, M. & Stiglitz, J. (1976). Equilibrium in competitive insurance markets. Quarterly Journal of Economics 90(4), 629–649.

10 The winner's curse

In a common-value auction, winning is evidence you were wrong.

Several bidders estimate the value of the same object. Estimates scatter around the truth. The winner is, by construction, the one who overestimated most. Unless bidders shade their bids downward to account for this, winning is systematically bad news — and the correction required grows with the number of competitors.

Outside auctions Applies wherever selection is by highest estimate. The candidate a company is most excited about is the one it has most overestimated. The project that looked best on paper had the most optimistic assumptions. The selection process itself introduces the bias, independent of anyone's judgement.

Capen, Clapp & Campbell (1971), from offshore oil lease bidding; formalised by Wilson and Milgrom & Weber (1982). Nobel 2020 to Milgrom and Wilson.

11 Bargaining — patience as power

Rubinstein's result, and the only number that matters in a negotiation.

In alternating-offer bargaining, agreement is reached immediately and the split is determined by relative impatience. The party who loses less from another round of delay captures more of the surplus. Not skill, not charm — discount rates.

Rubinstein, A. (1982). Perfect equilibrium in a bargaining model. Econometrica 50(1), 97–109. Robust

The practical form of impatience is your outside option — the Harvard Negotiation Project's BATNA, the best alternative to a negotiated agreement. Your leverage is not what you say inside the room; it is the quality of what happens if you walk out of it. This is why improving the alternative moves outcomes far more than improving the arguments.

Anchoring, and the correct defence Robust First offers exert a strong pull on final outcomes, even among experienced negotiators. The effective counter is not to argue with the anchor but to reorient attention to your own target and alternative before responding. Galinsky, A. & Mussweiler, T. (2001). First offers as anchors. JPSP 81(4), 657–669.

12 Evolutionary game theory

Equilibrium without anyone reasoning at all.

Maynard Smith removed rationality from the theory and kept the results. Strategies are not chosen, they are inherited or copied, and successful ones reproduce. An evolutionarily stable strategy is one that, once common, cannot be invaded by a rare mutant. Every ESS is a Nash equilibrium; the converse fails.

The Hawk–Dove game then predicts a stable mixture: too many hawks and they destroy each other, too few and hawkishness pays. The population settles at proportions set by the ratio of contest value to injury cost. No individual optimises; the distribution does.

Maynard Smith, J. & Price, G. (1973). The logic of animal conflict. Nature 246, 15–18. Robust

Why this matters for the rest of the note It is the bridge from "what should a rational agent do" to "what will actually be observed in a population over time" — markets, cultures, codebases, organisations. You do not need anyone to be clever for game-theoretic structure to show up in the outcome.

13 Mechanism design — inverse game theory

Stop predicting the game. Design one whose equilibrium is the outcome you want.

Given a desired outcome, what rules make self-interested play produce it? The central target is incentive compatibility: honesty must be the individually optimal move, so the mechanism never has to detect lying.

Vickrey (second-price) auction

Highest bidder wins, pays the second-highest bid. Bidding your true value becomes a dominant strategy — your bid decides whether you win, never what you pay. Strategising is eliminated by construction.

Vickrey, W. (1961). Nobel 1996.

Deferred acceptance

Gale–Shapley produces a stable matching — no pair would both rather defect to each other — and is truth-telling for the proposing side. Used for national medical residency matching and school assignment.

Gale & Shapley (1962). Nobel 2012 to Shapley and Roth.

The impossibility result Proved You cannot always have everything. Myerson–Satterthwaite (1983): for bilateral trade with private values, no mechanism is simultaneously efficient, individually rational and budget balanced. Some surplus is unavoidably lost. And Arrow's impossibility theorem (1951) rules out any voting rule satisfying a short list of reasonable conditions. Design has hard limits, not just hard problems.

14 Where the theory fails — and how it fails

The deviations are systematic, which makes them informative rather than embarrassing.

The ultimatum game

One player splits a sum; the other accepts (both paid) or rejects (both get nothing). Subgame perfection says offer the minimum and accept anything positive. Reality: offers below roughly 20–30% are rejected around half the time, and proposers anticipate this, typically offering 40–50%. People pay real money to punish unfairness.

Güth, Schmittberger & Schwarze (1982), JEBO. Replicated extensively. Robust

And it is cultural

Henrich and colleagues ran the game across fifteen small-scale societies. Behaviour varied widely with local norms of exchange and sharing. Among the Machiguenga, low offers were routinely accepted; in gift-economy cultures such as the Au and Gnau, hyper-generous offers were frequently rejected, because accepting a large gift incurs an obligation. There is no universal fairness constant.

Henrich, J. et al. (2001). In search of Homo economicus. American Economic Review 91(2); expanded in Behavioral and Brain Sciences (2005). Robust

How to hold the theory Game theory is not a prediction engine for human behaviour. It is a structural clarifier: it tells you what the incentives are, which tells you what has to be overcome. Where real players deviate — fairness, spite, trust, reciprocity — the deviation is itself the finding, and usually points at a payoff you failed to write down.

15 What these settle

The conclusions, stated flat.

QuestionSettled answer
Why do sensible people end up somewhere nobody wanted?Because equilibrium is a fixed point, not an optimum. Structure produces the outcome; no bad actor is needed.
Does having more options make me stronger?No. In strategic settings, visibly removing your own options is a source of power. Credibility comes from inability to yield.
Why is cooperation possible at all?Repetition with an open-ended horizon. Nice, retaliatory, forgiving and legible beats clever.
Why is an unverifiable claim worth so little?Cheap talk. If a low type can produce the identical message at identical cost, the message carries no information by construction.
What makes a claim believable?Differential cost. It must be something a weaker type could not afford to imitate. Nothing else qualifies.
Why do good options disappear from bad markets?Adverse selection. When quality is invisible, price converges to average, and above-average quality exits first.
What actually decides a negotiation?Relative patience, which in practice means the quality of each side's outside option — not the arguments made inside the room.
Why does winning a competitive bid feel bad later?The winner's curse. Selection by highest estimate selects the largest overestimate.
Can I design my way out of a bad game?Often — that is mechanism design. But not always: Myerson–Satterthwaite and Arrow set hard limits.
