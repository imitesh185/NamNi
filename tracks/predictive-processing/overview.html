Predictive Processing

You do not see the world. You see your brain's best current hypothesis about the world, continuously corrected by the small part of the incoming signal that the hypothesis failed to anticipate. Perception is not a feed being read; it is a prediction being tested. The senses do not deliver a picture — they deliver an error report on a picture the brain already had.

The one idea to carry. Only the unexpected travels. Whatever your model already accounts for is cancelled at the earliest opportunity and never reaches higher processing. This is why a correct prediction feels like nothing, why surprise is the unit of learning, and why comfort and understanding are so easy to confuse.

00 Why this exists

Three claims that reorganise everything downstream of them.

1 Perception is inference, not reception. Sensory data massively underdetermines its own cause. The brain must guess, and what you experience is the guess — not the data. Experience is the model, not the input.
2 The traffic runs downwards. The dominant flow in cortex is top-down prediction; the upward flow is residual error. In the visual system, far more fibres carry signals back toward the sensory periphery than forward from it. The architecture matches the theory better than the naive feed-forward story does.
3 Learning is driven by error, not by exposure. Repetition of the already-predicted teaches nothing. This is not a metaphor imported from psychology — it is the literal update rule in Rescorla–Wagner conditioning and in the firing of midbrain dopamine neurons.
Why it is worth your time It is the closest thing cognitive science currently has to a unifying account, and it reaches across perception, action, attention, learning, emotion, psychiatry and machine learning using one quantity: the difference between what was expected and what arrived.

01 The inverse problem

The reason a brain has no choice but to guess.

Light lands on a two-dimensional sheet of receptors. Infinitely many three-dimensional scenes would produce exactly that pattern: a small nearby object and a large distant one, a bright surface under dim light and a dim surface under bright light. The mapping from world to sensation discards information, and it cannot be run backwards. Recovering the cause from the effect is underdetermined in principle, not merely difficult.

Helmholtz named the solution in the 1860s: unconscious inference. The visual system supplies the missing constraints from prior knowledge about how the world tends to be, and settles on the most probable cause. You never experience the inference happening; you experience its conclusion, delivered as fact.

von Helmholtz, H. (1867). Handbuch der physiologischen Optik. Foundational

The everyday consequence Illusions are not malfunctions. They are the machinery working exactly as designed, on input engineered to make the most probable interpretation the wrong one. Knowing an illusion is an illusion does not dissolve it — your explicit belief and your perceptual prior are different quantities, held at different levels, and the higher one does not overwrite the lower.

02 The architecture

A hierarchy of models, each predicting the level beneath it.

Picture the cortex as a stack. Each level holds a model of what the level below it should be reporting, and sends that prediction downward. The lower level subtracts the prediction from what it actually has. Whatever remains — the prediction error — is the only thing sent upward. Higher levels then adjust their model to reduce future error.

Rao and Ballard built exactly this as a computational model of visual cortex and found it reproduced end-stopping and other "extra-classical" receptive field effects that had resisted explanation — neurons that stop responding when a line extends beyond their patch, because the extension makes the stimulus more predictable, not less present.

Rao, R. & Ballard, D. (1999). Predictive coding in the visual cortex. Nature Neuroscience 2(1), 79–87. Robust

Repetition suppression A directly observable prediction of the model: neural response to a repeated stimulus declines, not because the neurons tire but because the stimulus becomes predicted and therefore stops generating error. Related: mismatch negativity, an EEG signature that appears when a regular sequence of tones is violated — even when you are asleep or not attending. The brain is running predictions whether or not you are involved.

03 Precision — the part that does the real work

Not "how big is the error" but "how much should this error be trusted".

An error signal is useless without an estimate of its reliability. A prediction error from a noisy source should barely move the model; the same error from a reliable source should move it a lot. Precision is the inverse-variance weighting applied to each error signal before it updates anything.

This single knob carries an enormous amount of the theory's explanatory load:

Attention
Is precision-weighting, not a spotlight. Attending to something means increasing the gain on its prediction errors so they win the competition to update the model.
Sensory context
In fog, visual precision drops and priors dominate — you see what you expect. In bright light, error dominates and you see what is there.
Confidence
A high-precision prior held against low-precision evidence produces certainty that is impervious to correction. This is a mechanism, not a character flaw.
Pathology
Most predictive-processing accounts of mental illness are accounts of mis-set precision — see §10.
The generalisation worth keeping Every belief you hold is a pair: a claim, and a weight on how much new evidence is allowed to move it. Most reasoning failures are not wrong claims. They are correct claims held at the wrong precision — and the fix is the weight, not the content.

04 Free energy — the ambitious version

One principle proposed to cover perception, action, learning and life itself.

Friston's free energy principle proposes that any system that maintains itself against dissolution must act to minimise a quantity called variational free energy — a tractable upper bound on surprise. Perception minimises it by changing the model to fit the world. Action minimises it by changing the world to fit the model. Both are the same operation pointed in opposite directions.

Friston, K. (2010). The free-energy principle: a unified brain theory? Nature Reviews Neuroscience 11(2), 127–138. Influential · contested

The dark room problem If the goal is minimising surprise, the optimal organism finds a dark silent room and stays there forever — perfectly predictable, perfectly still. It does not happen. The standard reply is expected free energy: agents minimise surprise over the long run, which requires reducing uncertainty about the world, which mandates exploration. Curiosity falls out as a term in the objective rather than an add-on. Whether this reply is a genuine derivation or a repair is exactly where the argument sits.
How to hold this section Sections 01–03 are well-supported computational neuroscience. Section 04 is a much larger metaphysical claim resting on the same machinery. The strong form of the free energy principle is difficult to falsify — a frequent and fair criticism. Use predictive processing as the working model; treat the free energy principle as an interesting frame, not a settled result.

05 The evidence you can feel

Phenomena that are strange under the "perception is a feed" model and ordinary under this one.

The hollow mask Robust

A mask viewed from behind — genuinely concave — is seen as a normal convex face. The prior "faces stick out" overwhelms the depth cues. Notably, people with schizophrenia are less susceptible, consistent with weakened perceptual priors.

The McGurk effect Robust

Video of lips saying "ga" dubbed with audio "ba" is heard as "da" — a compromise neither channel contained. You cannot un-hear it by knowing. Perception is a fusion of evidence, not a transcript of one channel.

Sine-wave speech Robust

Degraded whistles sound like meaningless noise until you are told the sentence — after which they sound unmistakably like speech, permanently. The stimulus did not change. The prior did. This is the cleanest demonstration available that you hear your model.

You cannot tickle yourself Robust

Self-generated touch is predicted from the motor command and cancelled before it reaches awareness. Introduce a delay or a trajectory perturbation between your movement and the touch and the ticklishness returns in proportion.

Blakemore, Wolpert & Frith (1998), Nature Neuroscience.

Binocular rivalry Robust

Show each eye a different image and you do not see a blend — you see one, then the other, alternating. The system will not hold two incompatible hypotheses; it commits to one explanation at a time.

The blind spot Robust

Each eye has a receptor-free hole where the optic nerve leaves. You perceive no gap. Nothing is "filling it in" with pixels — there is simply no prediction error arising from a region that reports nothing, so the model proceeds unchallenged.

06 Action as prediction — active inference

The second way to reduce error: make the world match the guess.

There are exactly two ways to close a gap between prediction and reality. Change the prediction — that is perception and learning. Or change reality — that is action.

Active inference takes this literally. A motor command, on this account, is not an instruction to a muscle. It is a proprioceptive prediction: the brain predicts the sensory consequences of the arm already being raised, and classical reflex arcs then act to eliminate the resulting error by moving the arm. Movement is error-cancellation, and intention is a prediction the body is obliged to make true.

Why this reframe earns its keep It removes the awkward split between the system that perceives and the system that acts. Both are running one algorithm on one quantity. It also explains why attenuating your own sensations is necessary for action at all — if self-generated signals were not cancelled, every movement would be an avalanche of unexplained error.

07 Learning is literally error

The most solidly established piece of the whole picture.

Rescorla and Wagner showed in 1972 that classical conditioning is not driven by pairing but by surprise. If a cue already fully predicts an outcome, adding a second cue teaches nothing about it — the phenomenon of blocking. Learning occurs in proportion to how wrong the existing prediction was, and stops when prediction is accurate.

Rescorla, R. & Wagner, A. (1972). A theory of Pavlovian conditioning. Foundational

Two decades later, Schultz recorded midbrain dopamine neurons and found they do not signal reward. They signal reward prediction error. An unexpected reward produces a burst. Once a cue reliably predicts the reward, the burst migrates backwards to the cue and the reward itself produces nothing. Omit an expected reward and the neurons dip below baseline at exactly the moment it should have arrived.

Schultz, W., Dayan, P. & Montague, P.R. (1997). A neural substrate of prediction and reward. Science 275(5306), 1593–1599. Robust

The same equation twice That dip is a negative number in a subtraction. It is the identical update rule as temporal-difference learning in reinforcement learning — a case where a machine learning algorithm and a mammalian midbrain turned out to be computing the same thing. Not an analogy. The same equation.

08 Expertise is a better generative model

Why the expert sees a different scene, using the same eyes.

Chase and Simon showed chess masters reconstructing board positions after a five-second glance far better than novices — but only for positions from real games. On randomly scattered pieces the advantage collapsed to nearly nothing. The master has no superior visual memory. The master has priors that make legal positions highly predictable and therefore cheap to encode.

Chase, W. & Simon, H. (1973). Perception in chess. Cognitive Psychology 4(1), 55–81. Robust

The same result recurs everywhere it is looked for: radiologists detect abnormality at above-chance rates from a fraction of a second's exposure; experienced readers perceive words rather than letters. Expertise is compression, and compression is prediction.

The consequence for how skill is acquired If learning is proportional to prediction error, then a session that produced no error produced no learning, regardless of how long it lasted or how good it felt. Comfort is the absence of an error signal. The corollary is uncomfortable and unavoidable: a prediction you never committed to cannot be violated, so material consumed without a stated expectation generates nothing to learn from. Reading confirms; only a wrong guess updates.

09 Emotion and the body

The most contested extension, and the most interesting if it holds.

Turn the machinery inward. The brain also predicts the state of its own body — heart rate, gut, temperature, blood chemistry — from signals that are noisy and slow. This is interoceptive inference, and its regulatory purpose is allostasis: anticipating bodily needs before they become deficits rather than reacting after.

Barrett's theory of constructed emotion argues that an emotion is what you get when interoceptive prediction is categorised using a learned concept. The same physiological arousal becomes "fear" or "excitement" depending on the model applied. On this account emotions are not triggered and released; they are constructed and inferred, like colours.

Barrett, L.F. (2017). How Emotions Are Made. Also Barrett (2017), Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience. Influential · disputed

Where this is genuinely contested Basic-emotion theorists dispute it directly, arguing for evolutionarily conserved affect programmes with distinct signatures. The debate is live and neither side has closed it. What is not in serious dispute: interoceptive signals are predicted rather than merely read, and the same bodily state can be experienced as different emotions depending on context.

10 When priors and precision go wrong

Computational psychiatry — symptoms as mis-set parameters rather than deficits.

ConditionProposed computational accountStanding
PsychosisPrediction errors assigned excessive precision, so noise is treated as meaningful. Delusion is then a high-level model recruited to explain persistent unexplained salience.Mixed
AutismPriors weighted unusually low relative to sensory error — "hypo-priors" — producing sensory overwhelm and a world that fails to become predictable.Mixed
AnxietyA high-precision threat prior. Ambiguous evidence resolves toward danger, and the resulting avoidance prevents the error that would correct the prior.Mixed
Chronic painPain as inference about bodily threat rather than a readout of tissue damage — which is why pain persists after healing and varies with expectation.Mixed
HallucinationPriors so strong they generate percepts without input. Demonstrated experimentally: people with hallucination-prone profiles are more readily conditioned to hear a tone that is not played.Suggestive

Representative: Pellicano & Burr (2012), Trends in Cognitive Sciences (autism); Sterzer et al. (2018), Biological Psychiatry (psychosis); Powers, Mathys & Corlett (2017), Science (conditioned hallucinations).

Read these as frameworks, not diagnoses Each is a research programme with supportive findings and unresolved problems. They are included because the shape of the explanation is the point: a symptom recast as a parameter set to the wrong value, rather than a part that is broken. That reframing is doing real work in psychiatry even where the specific models remain unsettled.

11 Expectation as a cause, not a bias

The cleanest proof that priors alter the outcome and not merely the report.

Benedetti's open–hidden paradigm removes the reporting problem entirely. Give an analgesic openly, with the patient watching, and compare with the identical drug delivered by a pre-programmed pump at a time the patient does not know. Same drug, same dose, same bloodstream. The hidden administration is reliably less effective. The difference is the expectation, and it is a component of the drug's measured effect.

Benedetti, F. et al. Open versus hidden medical treatments. Prevention & Treatment (2003); extended in Lancet Neurology (2004). Robust

The point being made This is not "believing makes it so". It is that the prediction is one of the inputs from which the experience is constructed, on equal footing with the sensory evidence. Change the prior and you have changed a term in the computation whose output is the experience.

12 The criticisms, stated fairly

Held honestly, because a framework that explains everything explains nothing.

Unfalsifiability

In its strong form the free energy principle can accommodate any observation. A theory that cannot be embarrassed by data is a language, not a hypothesis. Critics have pressed this hard, and the reply — that it is a mathematical framework generating falsifiable process models — concedes part of the point.

Where are the error units?

The architecture predicts distinct populations computing prediction versus error. Evidence exists but is not decisive, and cortical microcircuitry has not cleanly delivered the required division of labour.

"Predictive processing" is not one theory

Rao–Ballard predictive coding, Bayesian brain accounts, active inference and the free energy principle are related but distinct, with different commitments and different evidence. They are frequently blurred together, including in popular treatments.

Alternative explanations survive

Several signature findings — repetition suppression especially — have adaptation-based accounts that do not require predictive coding. The framework is well-motivated, not established.

13 What these settle

The conclusions, stated flat.

QuestionSettled answer
Am I perceiving the world or a model of it?A model. The world underdetermines the sensation, so inference is mandatory, and what reaches awareness is the conclusion.
Why doesn't knowing about an illusion remove it?Explicit belief and perceptual prior sit at different levels. The higher one does not overwrite the lower.
What is attention?Precision-weighting. Turning up the gain on selected prediction errors so they are allowed to update the model.
Why does the expert see more from less?Better priors. Expertise is compression; compression is prediction. It is not superior raw memory or acuity.
What actually causes learning?Prediction error. Established in conditioning (Rescorla–Wagner) and in dopamine firing (Schultz). Exposure without error teaches nothing.
Why is comfortable study ineffective?Comfort is the absence of an error signal. No violated expectation, no update — and an expectation never stated cannot be violated.
Is expectation just a reporting bias?No. Open–hidden drug administration removes the reporting channel and the effect remains. Expectation is an input to the experience.
Is the free energy principle established?No. It is an influential and contested unifying claim. The underlying predictive coding is far better supported than the grand version.
