Apache Kafka

Kafka questions in senior interviews are almost never about the API. They are about what happens when something breaks — a consumer dies, a broker fails, a partition count changes, a producer outpaces a consumer. This branch is organised around producing those failures deliberately and watching what the clients actually do.

The single idea. The partition is the unit of everything. Ordering is per-partition. Parallelism is bounded by partition count. Assignment, replication, offsets and lag are all partition-scoped. Nearly every Kafka behaviour that surprises people follows from taking that sentence completely literally.
#partitions #rebalancing #isr #delivery-semantics #lag

00 The one question this branch exists to answer

Kafka's version of the slow-job question, and it has the same shape.

? “Consumer lag on one partition has been climbing for two hours while the others are fine. Walk me through your diagnosis.” It tests whether you can separate producer-side causes from consumer-side ones, whether you know that a single lagging partition means something structurally different from all of them lagging, and whether you reach for the key distribution before reaching for more consumers.

There is a second question, and it is the one that most reliably exposes shallow knowledge:

? “You have acks=all. Have you lost data?” The correct answer is “it depends on min.insync.replicas, and on whether unclean leader election is enabled” — because acks=all with min.insync.replicas=1 guarantees almost nothing, and most people who quote acks=all have never checked the second setting.
The failure mode of this branch Learning Kafka from configuration blog posts. Settings without mechanisms produce cargo cult. Every configuration in this branch should be understood as a position on a trade-off curve you have personally measured — which is exactly why all four labs are built around breaking a running cluster.

K1 Architecture

A distributed, partitioned, replicated commit log — and every word in that phrase is load-bearing.

WHAT IT IS. A cluster of brokers, each hosting partitions of topics. Every partition is an append-only ordered log with a single leader and zero or more follower replicas. A controller manages metadata and leadership.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because point-to-point integration between n systems requires n² connections, and because most consumers want to read data more than once, at their own pace, possibly weeks later. The log solves both by decoupling producers from consumers in time as well as in space.

DEPENDENCIES. L0 distributed minimum.

SUBTOPICS. Brokers, topics, partitions, replicas · the log as the core abstraction · the controller and its responsibilities · KRaft versus ZooKeeper and what actually changed · metadata propagation · why Kafka is not a queue and not a database · the difference between a topic and a partition in every operational sense.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free official documentation · Title: the Apache Kafka documentation, the "Design" section specifically
Why this one: free, and unusually good — it is one of the few project documentations written as a design rationale rather than a reference. The Design section explains why each decision was made, which is exactly what interviews probe.

DEEPER — Jay Kreps, "The Log: What every software engineer should know about real-time data's unifying abstraction" (free). It reframes databases, replication and integration as one idea.
OBSERVATION · Find the log on disk Create a topic with 3 partitions and produce a few thousand records. Then go into the broker container and look at the data directory. You will find one directory per partition replica, containing .log, .index and .timeindex files.

Dump a segment with kafka-dump-log and read the actual records with their offsets. Kafka stops being a service and becomes a set of files you can look at — and that shift is worth doing on day one of this branch.

CONNECTS TO. K2 · Data Engineering N3 (a table is a summary of a log) · Databases N4 (the WAL is the same structure).

K2 The storage engine

Why Kafka is fast, and why the answer is mostly "it does not fight the operating system".

WHAT IT IS. Each partition is a sequence of segment files with sparse offset and time indexes. Reads and writes are sequential. The OS page cache does the caching, and data moves from disk to socket without passing through application memory.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because sequential disk access is orders of magnitude faster than random access, and because the operating system already has a well-tuned cache — so the fastest design is one that uses it rather than duplicating it in the JVM heap.

DEPENDENCIES. K1 · OS N3 (page cache) · OS N5 (zero-copy is a syscall story).

SUBTOPICS. Segments, rolling and the active segment · the sparse offset index and why it is sparse · the time index · retention by time and by size, and that retention deletes whole segments · log compaction — tombstones, the cleaner, and compacted topics as a changelog · page cache and why Kafka heap should stay small · zero-copy transfer and what disables it · tiered storage.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free book · Title: Kafka: The Definitive Guide, 2nd edition — free PDF from Confluent — the storage internals chapter
Why this one: free, written largely by Kafka committers, and the storage chapter covers segments, indexes and compaction with the operational consequences attached.

DEEPER — the original Kafka paper (LinkedIn, 2011), free. Short, and it states the sequential-IO and page-cache argument in its original form.
OBSERVATION · Make compaction happen and watch a key survive Create a compacted topic. Produce the same key ten times with different values, plus a hundred other keys. Force segment rolling with a small segment size and trigger the cleaner.

Dump the log before and after. Only the latest value for each key survives — and the offsets are now non-contiguous, which is a detail that surprises people. You have just watched a topic become a table.

Then produce a null value as a tombstone and watch the key disappear entirely after the delete retention window. That is how CDC deletes propagate, and it connects directly to L5.

CONNECTS TO. K7 · OS N3 · L5 CDC · Databases N2 (LSM compaction is the same idea).

K3 Producers

Where durability, latency, throughput and ordering are all decided by four settings.

WHAT IT IS. A client that batches records per partition, optionally compresses them, sends them to the partition leader, and waits for a configurable degree of acknowledgement.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because sending one record at a time over the network is catastrophically inefficient. Almost all producer configuration is a position on the batching trade-off — wait longer, send more per request, get higher throughput and worse latency.

DEPENDENCIES. K1, K2.

SUBTOPICS. The default partitioner and key hashing · sticky partitioning for keyless records · linger.ms and batch.size as the throughput/latency dial · compression and where the CPU is paid · acks 0/1/all and the durability curve · retries and the duplicate they create · max.in.flight.requests and how it silently breaks ordering on retry · the idempotent producer, its sequence numbers and its per-partition scope · transactions and the transaction coordinator · buffer.memory and producer-side backpressure.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free official documentation · Title: the Kafka producer configuration reference, read in full once — it is shorter than it looks
Why this one: free, and the producer config page is where the trade-offs are stated precisely. Reading the actual definition of max.in.flight.requests.per.connection is how most people discover that their ordering guarantee was never real.

DEEPER — KIP-98 (exactly-once delivery) and KIP-679 (sticky partitioner). Free, and the KIPs are the design documents — they state the problem before the solution.

THE LAB. Laboratory 09 covers partitioning and ordering; Laboratory 12 covers idempotence and transactions.

The fact that catches most candidates With retries enabled and max.in.flight > 1, a failed batch can be re-sent after a later batch succeeded — reordering records within a single partition. Ordering per partition is not a Kafka guarantee, it is a consequence of your producer configuration, and the idempotent producer is what makes it hold at in-flight up to 5.

CONNECTS TO. K7 (acks means nothing without ISR) · K8 · K9.

K4 Consumers

A poll loop and an offset — and almost every consumer bug is one of those two.

WHAT IT IS. A client that fetches batches from assigned partitions, processes them, and records its position by committing an offset.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because the broker deliberately does not track per-consumer delivery. Pushing the position tracking to the consumer is what allows replay, multiple independent consumer groups, and a broker that stays simple and fast.

DEPENDENCIES. K1, K2.

SUBTOPICS. The poll loop and why it must be called regularly · fetch.min.bytes, fetch.max.wait.ms and consumer-side batching · max.poll.records · offset commits — auto versus manual, sync versus async, and where each loses or duplicates data · the __consumer_offsets topic · auto.offset.reset and the silent data-loss trap when a consumer is down past retention · max.poll.interval.ms and the silent eviction when processing takes too long · seeking and replay.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free book · Title: Kafka: The Definitive Guide, 2nd edition — the consumer chapter
Why this one: free, and it walks the commit strategies with their exact failure modes — which record is lost or duplicated in each — rather than describing them abstractly.
OBSERVATION · Lose data with auto-commit, then duplicate it Run a consumer with auto-commit enabled and a processing step that takes a second per batch. Kill it mid-batch. Restart. Depending on where the auto-commit fell, you either reprocessed records or skipped them entirely — and you have no way to know which without counting.

Now switch to manual commit after processing and repeat: you get duplicates but never loss. Then commit before processing: you get loss but never duplicates. Those are the only two options, and you must choose one.

Then trigger the silent eviction: set max.poll.interval.ms low and make one batch take longer. The consumer is removed from the group while still running, its partitions are reassigned, and its commit is rejected. This is one of the most confusing production incidents in Kafka and it has no obvious error message.

CONNECTS TO. K5 · K6 · K8.

K5 Consumer groups

How parallelism is coordinated, and why partition count is a ceiling.

WHAT IT IS. A set of consumers sharing a group id, among which partitions are distributed so that each partition is consumed by exactly one member. A broker acting as group coordinator runs the membership protocol.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because scaling consumption requires dividing the work, and dividing it requires agreement about who owns what. The partition is the indivisible unit, which is why more consumers than partitions leaves some idle — a fact that governs capacity planning.

DEPENDENCIES. K4.

SUBTOPICS. The group coordinator and its election · the join-group and sync-group protocol · the group leader computing the assignment client-side · assignment strategies — range, round-robin, sticky, cooperative-sticky — and what each optimises · why range assignment creates imbalance across multiple topics · static group membership · multiple groups on one topic and independent offsets.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free official documentation · Title: the Kafka consumer group protocol documentation, with kafka-consumer-groups --describe as the live instrument
Why this one: free, and the describe output is the single most useful diagnostic command in Kafka operations — current assignment, current offset, log end offset and lag per partition, in one table.
OBSERVATION · Find the ceiling Create a topic with 4 partitions. Start 2 consumers, then 4, then 6, in the same group. Measure throughput at each step and run --describe each time.

Throughput rises to 4 consumers and then stops. Consumers 5 and 6 are assigned nothing and idle permanently. Partition count is a hard ceiling on consumer parallelism, which is why partition count is a capacity decision made at topic-creation time and is painful to change later — as Lab 09 demonstrates.

Then start a second group on the same topic and watch both consume everything independently with separate offsets. That is the fan-out property, and it is the reason Kafka replaced point-to-point integration.

CONNECTS TO. K6 · K9 · Distributed Systems N6.

K6 Rebalancing

The most operationally consequential mechanism in Kafka, and the one most often misconfigured.

WHAT IT IS. The protocol by which partition ownership is redistributed when group membership or topic metadata changes.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because consumers fail and scale, and partitions must always have exactly one owner. The cost is that under the classic protocol, every consumer stops consuming while the reassignment happens — a stop-the-world pause proportional to the slowest member.

DEPENDENCIES. K5.

SUBTOPICS. Triggers: membership change, subscription change, partition count change · eager rebalancing and the revoke-everything step · cooperative incremental rebalancing and what it revokes instead · session.timeout.ms, heartbeat.interval.ms and max.poll.interval.ms and their interactions · rebalance storms — causes and the feedback loop that sustains them · static membership and rolling restarts without rebalance · the onPartitionsRevoked hook and committing before losing ownership.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free design document · Title: KIP-429 — Kafka Consumer Incremental Rebalance Protocol — freely available
Why this one: free, and it is the clearest existing explanation of the problem, because a KIP must state the deficiency before proposing the fix. It shows you exactly what was wrong with eager rebalancing, which is the thing an interview asks about.

THE LAB. Laboratory 10 — Rebalancing. This node is not learnable by reading.

The rebalance storm, stated as a loop Processing slows → a consumer exceeds max.poll.interval.ms → it is evicted → rebalance → all consumers pause → backlog grows → batches get larger → processing takes longer → another consumer is evicted. This is a reinforcing feedback loop, and it does not stop when the original slowdown ends — the same metastable failure structure as Distributed Systems N7.

CONNECTS TO. K9 · K10 · Systems Thinking N2.

K7 Replication

Where durability is actually determined — and where acks=all stops being enough.

WHAT IT IS. Each partition has a leader and followers. Followers fetch from the leader. The set of replicas sufficiently caught up is the in-sync replica set. The high watermark is the highest offset replicated to all ISR members, and consumers may not read beyond it.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because a broker will fail, and an acknowledged write must survive it. The design choice — ISR rather than a fixed quorum — trades some availability characteristics for the ability to tolerate n−1 failures with n replicas.

DEPENDENCIES. K1, K2, K3 · Distributed Systems N3.

SUBTOPICS. Leader and follower fetch protocol · the ISR and how a replica falls out · replica.lag.time.max.ms · the high watermark and why consumers cannot read uncommitted records · min.insync.replicas and its interaction with acks=all · unclean leader election as a deliberate availability-over-durability switch · leader election and the controller's role · preferred leader and partition balance · rack awareness · what happens to producers when ISR drops below the minimum.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free official documentation · Title: the Kafka documentation's replication section within Design
Why this one: free, and it explains explicitly why ISR was chosen over majority-vote quorum — which is a real distributed systems design argument, and answering "why not Raft?" well is a strong differentiator.

DEEPER — Jack Vanlightly's freely published analyses of Kafka replication and its failure edges. Rigorous and unusually honest about the corner cases.

THE LAB. Laboratory 11 — Replication & Broker Failure.

The configuration trio that must be understood together acks · min.insync.replicas · unclean.leader.election.enable

acks=all with min.insync.replicas=1 means the leader alone acknowledges — identical durability to acks=1, with worse latency. And unclean leader election set to true means an out-of-sync replica can become leader, silently discarding acknowledged writes. These three are one setting with three parts.

CONNECTS TO. K8 · K10 · Distributed Systems N3.

K8 Delivery semantics

The convergence point — and where "exactly-once" gets stated honestly.

WHAT IT IS. The end-to-end guarantee about how many times a record is processed, produced by combining producer acknowledgement, consumer offset handling and replication.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because a network cannot distinguish a lost request from a lost response, so every retry is potentially a duplicate. At-least-once plus idempotence is the only construction that survives contact with reality, and "exactly-once" is the name given to that construction when the system provides it for you.

DEPENDENCIES. K3, K4, K7 · Distributed Systems N1.

SUBTOPICS. At-most-once and where its data loss occurs · at-least-once and its duplicates · the idempotent producer — producer id, sequence numbers, per-partition scope, and what it does not cover · transactions — the coordinator, the transaction log, markers · isolation.level=read_committed and the LSO · read-process-write as one atomic unit · why end-to-end exactly-once requires the sink to cooperate · idempotency keys as the general answer when it cannot.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free design document · Title: KIP-98 — Exactly Once Delivery and Transactional Messaging — freely available
Why this one: free, and it is the primary source. It is explicit about the scope: Kafka-to-Kafka — which is precisely the boundary most candidates get wrong when they claim their pipeline is exactly-once.

THE LAB. Laboratory 12 — Delivery Semantics.

The honest answer to "does Kafka give exactly-once?" Within Kafka, yes — for a read-process-write cycle where the output is also Kafka, transactions make it atomic. To an external sink, no — unless that sink participates, either by being idempotent on a key you supply or by supporting a two-phase commit. "Effectively-once, via at-least-once delivery plus an idempotent sink" is the answer that demonstrates you have actually built one.

CONNECTS TO. L4 stream semantics (this is its foundation) · L5 pipelines (idempotent writes) · Distributed Systems N7.

K9 Performance and lag

Throughput, latency, and the metric every on-call engineer watches.

WHAT IT IS. The levers that trade throughput against latency, and consumer lag as the primary operational signal of the whole system's health.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because lag is the one number that reflects producers, consumers, partitions, rebalances and downstream systems simultaneously — which makes it both the best alert and the most ambiguous one. Diagnosing lag correctly requires separating the causes.

DEPENDENCIES. K3, K4, K5, K6.

SUBTOPICS. The batching trade-off on both sides · compression choice and its CPU cost · partition count — why more is not better: more open files, more memory, longer leader election, longer rebalance, more end-to-end latency · lag measurement and its per-partition nature · the four distinct causes of lag — slow processing, insufficient consumers, skewed partitions, and rebalance churn — each with a different fix · hot partitions and key design · throughput ceilings and where they actually sit.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free engineering writing · Title: Confluent's freely published benchmarking and partition-count guidance, read alongside your own JMX measurements
Why this one: free, and it states the partition-count trade-off in both directions with numbers. Most guidance only warns about too few partitions; the cost of too many is the part that distinguishes a considered answer.
The four-cause lag diagnosis, to internalise as a reflex 1. Is lag on all partitions or one? One → skew or a stuck consumer. All → capacity or downstream. 2. Is the consumer's processing rate steady? Falling → downstream slowdown. 3. Are there rebalances in the log? Frequent → churn, not capacity — and adding consumers makes it worse. 4. Is producer rate elevated? If yes, it is not a consumer problem at all.

Adding consumers is the reflex response and it is the correct fix in only one of the four cases.

CONNECTS TO. K6 · K10 · L2 skew (the identical phenomenon) · Probability N6 (a queue near saturation).

K11 Ecosystem

Kept deliberately short — two components you must be able to discuss, not master.

Kafka Connect. A framework for source and sink connectors with offset management, scaling and restart handled for you. Worth knowing: why it exists (every team writing bespoke ingestion is the problem it solves), how it handles offsets, single-message transforms, and its dead letter queue behaviour. It is also the natural entry point to L5 CDC via log-based source connectors.

Schema Registry. Centralised schema storage with compatibility enforcement. This is the single most important component for L6 data contracts — backward, forward and full compatibility are precise definitions of what a producer may change without breaking consumers, and they are the mechanism behind a data contract.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free official documentation · Title: the Kafka Connect documentation and the Confluent Schema Registry compatibility-types page
Why this one: free, and the compatibility-types page is a precise, short definition of breaking versus non-breaking change — which you will reuse directly in L6.

K12 When not to use Kafka

Judgment, and a question asked more often than people expect.

The strongest version of this answer Lead with the last row. "Most requests for global ordering are actually requests for per-entity ordering, which Kafka gives you for free by keying on the entity — and if it genuinely is global, Kafka can only provide it with a single partition, which forfeits all parallelism" demonstrates that you interrogate requirements rather than accept them, which is a senior signal.

09 Laboratory — Partitioning & Ordering

The foundational Kafka lab. Everything else assumes what this one teaches.

PROBLEM. Order-status events for each customer must be processed in the order they occurred. Cancellations must not overtake confirmations.

INITIAL SYSTEM. A topic with 6 partitions on the 3-broker cluster. A producer emitting order_created, order_paid, order_cancelled per order, with no key. A consumer group that records, per order id, the sequence of statuses it observed and flags any that arrived out of order. Also a per-partition record counter.
FAILURE TO INTRODUCE — three of them, in escalating subtlety 1. With no key, records for one order land on different partitions and are consumed by different members. Ordering is violated immediately and obviously.

2. Add the customer id as the key. Ordering now holds. Now increase the partition count from 6 to 12 while producing. The hash changes, existing keys move, and ordering breaks across the boundary — for a change that looks purely like scaling.

3. Restore 6 partitions and correct keys. Now set retries=5, max.in.flight.requests.per.connection=5, disable idempotence, and inject transient broker errors. Ordering is violated within a single partition — which most engineers believe is impossible.

CONCEPTS THAT EMERGE. Partition as the ordering unit → keys and the default partitioner → hash-modulo and why partition count is effectively immutable → consistent hashing as the alternative Kafka chose not to make → in-flight requests and retry reordering → the idempotent producer as the fix → sticky partitioning for keyless records → hot keys as the cost of keying.

The artifact is a table of key-strategy against ordering-violations against partition-balance — which shows in one view that ordering and even distribution are in direct tension.

REAL-WORLD CONNECTION. Every ordering bug in a keyed pipeline is experiment 4 or 6. The partition-count change is a genuine production trap — it looks like routine capacity work and silently breaks a guarantee downstream systems depend on. The correct operational answer is that partition count is immutable once keys carry semantics; you create a new topic instead.

PORTFOLIO VALUE. High. A partitioning laboratory with a pluggable key-distribution generator, an ordering-violation detector and a results table is a compact, complete and unusual artifact.

The data structures this lab forces Consistent hashing — experiment 4 makes you feel exactly why hash % n is catastrophic on resize, which is the motivating problem for it. A bounded min-heap — to find the top-K hot keys from a live stream without buffering everything, which is how you would actually detect experiment 3 in production.

10 Laboratory — Consumer Rebalancing

The lab that produces the most "I did not know it did that" moments.

PROBLEM. You need to scale consumers up and down, and deploy new versions, without interrupting processing. Currently every deployment causes a visible processing gap.

INITIAL SYSTEM. A topic with 12 partitions and steady production. A consumer group of 3 members using the default eager assignment, each logging every partition assignment and revocation with a timestamp, and emitting a heartbeat record every 100 ms so processing gaps are measurable to the millisecond.
FAILURE TO INTRODUCE Add a fourth consumer. All three existing consumers stop processing entirely while the group re-forms — a stop-the-world pause visible as a gap in every consumer's heartbeat stream. Nine partitions that did not need to move were revoked and reassigned anyway.

Then the second failure, which is worse: set max.poll.interval.ms to a few seconds and make processing occasionally slower than that. The group enters a rebalance loop it does not exit from, and throughput collapses to near zero while every consumer appears healthy.

CONCEPTS THAT EMERGE. The group coordinator and the join/sync protocol → eager rebalancing's revoke-all step → assignment strategies and their different disruption profiles → cooperative incremental rebalancing → session timeout vs heartbeat vs poll interval → rebalance storms as a reinforcing loop → static membership → committing in onPartitionsRevoked.

6Induce a rebalance storm; then attempt to fix it by adding consumersThe intervention that makes it worse

WHAT TO OBSERVE. Processing gap per consumer per event, in milliseconds; number of partitions revoked versus number that needed to move; rebalance frequency; duplicate count per strategy; and lag during and after each rebalance. Plot the heartbeat gaps as a timeline — the visual difference between eager and cooperative is dramatic and is the single most compelling artifact in this branch.

REAL-WORLD CONNECTION. This is the most common source of unexplained latency spikes in Kafka-based systems. Deployments cause rebalances; autoscaling causes rebalances; a slow downstream dependency causes evictions which cause rebalances. The rebalance storm in particular is an incident where every instinct — add capacity — is exactly wrong.

PORTFOLIO VALUE. Very high. A rebalancing simulator with a measured comparison of assignment strategies and a reproducible storm is a distinctive artifact, and the timeline plot communicates the result in five seconds.

11 Laboratory — Replication & Broker Failure

Where you find out what your durability settings actually purchased.

PROBLEM. Your producer receives a successful acknowledgement. A broker then fails. Is the record still there?

INITIAL SYSTEM. A topic with replication factor 3 across the 3-broker cluster. A producer that records the offset of every acknowledged send to an external file. A consumer that reads the topic completely after each failure event. The measurement is a set difference: acknowledged offsets minus readable offsets. Any non-empty result is acknowledged data loss.
FAILURE TO INTRODUCE Set acks=1. Produce continuously. Kill the leader broker for a partition mid-stream. After failover, compute the set difference. Records that were acknowledged no longer exist — the leader confirmed before any follower had them, and it died with them.

Then the more instructive failure: set acks=all but leave min.insync.replicas=1. Repeat. You lose data again, because "all" means "all in-sync replicas" and the ISR had shrunk to just the leader.

CONCEPTS THAT EMERGE. Leader and follower fetch → the ISR and how replicas fall out → the high watermark and read visibility → acks and min.insync.replicas as one combined setting → producer blocking when ISR is insufficient → unclean leader election as an explicit durability-for-availability trade → controller-driven leader election.

#ExperimentWhat it isolates
1Full matrix: acks ∈ {0,1,all} × min.insync ∈ {1,2,3}, kill leader each timeThe complete durability table — measured, not recited
2Measure producer throughput and p99 for each cell of the matrixThe cost side of the durability curve
3Slow one follower with tc netem and watch it leave the ISRISR shrink under partial failure, not just crash
4With min.insync=3, take one broker down and try to produceProducer blocks — availability sacrificed for durability, deliberately
5Observe the high watermark lagging the log end offset under loadWhy consumers cannot read the newest records
6Force an out-of-sync replica to become leader with unclean election enabledDeliberate, silent, acknowledged-data loss
7Repeat 6 with unclean election disabledThe partition goes offline instead — the other choice

WHAT TO OBSERVE. Acknowledged-but-missing record count per configuration; ISR size over time; high watermark versus log end offset; producer error type and duration when ISR is insufficient; failover duration. The artifact is the durability matrix — a grid of acks × min.insync with measured loss, throughput and latency in each cell. That single table answers half the Kafka durability questions you will ever be asked.

REAL-WORLD CONNECTION. Payments and financial systems live in this table. The acks=all with min.insync.replicas=1 misconfiguration is genuinely common and provides no durability benefit over acks=1 while costing latency — and it survives review precisely because "acks=all" looks correct.

PORTFOLIO VALUE. Very high, particularly for payments-shaped companies. A measured durability matrix with a reproducible harness is exactly the artifact that demonstrates you reason about correctness quantitatively.

12 Laboratory — Delivery Semantics

Where "exactly-once" becomes a precise claim with a stated scope.

PROBLEM. Consume payment events, aggregate a running total per account, and write the result. The total must be correct after any crash, at any point.

INITIAL SYSTEM. A read-process-write consumer: read from an input topic, compute a running sum, produce to an output topic, commit the offset. At-least-once configuration. A verifier that independently computes the true total from the input topic and compares.
FAILURE TO INTRODUCE — both directions Crash after producing but before committing. On restart the records are reprocessed and re-produced. The total is too high, and the output topic contains duplicates.

Now commit before processing instead. Crash in the same place. The total is now too low — records were skipped. You have demonstrated that with no further mechanism, you must choose which way to be wrong.

CONCEPTS THAT EMERGE. At-most-once and at-least-once as the only two naive options → the idempotent producer and its per-partition scope → transactions spanning produce and offset commit → read_committed and the last stable offset → why the offset commit must be inside the transaction → the boundary at an external sink → idempotency keys as the general answer → effectively-once as the honest name.

#ExperimentWhat it isolates
1Commit after processing; crash 20 times; count duplicatesAt-least-once, quantified
2Commit before processing; crash 20 times; count lossesAt-most-once, quantified
3Enable idempotent producer only; repeat experiment 1It does not fix reprocessing — a common misconception
4Wrap produce + offset commit in a transaction; repeatExactly-once within Kafka
5Consume the output with read_uncommitted vs read_committedAborted transactions are visible to one and not the other
6Kill the consumer mid-transaction; observe the abort and LSO behaviourTransaction coordinator recovery
7Measure throughput with and without transactionsThe cost of exactly-once
8Replace the sink with an external database, keep transactions onThe guarantee breaks at the boundary — the key result
9Add an idempotency key and upsert; repeat crashesEffectively-once to an external system
10Same, but with a non-idempotent sink such as an append-only logWhy sink cooperation is not optional

WHAT TO OBSERVE. Computed total versus true total after every crash; duplicate and missing record counts; throughput with and without transactions; and, in experiment 8, precisely where the guarantee stops holding. The artifact is a semantics table — configuration against duplicates, losses, throughput and scope. Experiment 8 is the one to be able to describe from memory, because it is where most candidates over-claim.

REAL-WORLD CONNECTION. Every financial pipeline. The claim "our pipeline is exactly-once" is usually true only within Kafka and false end-to-end, and being the person who states the boundary precisely is a strong senior signal — particularly at payments companies where the question is not academic.

PORTFOLIO VALUE. High. A crash-injection harness with a verifier and a measured semantics table is directly relevant to payments and financial-data roles.
