Pipelines & orchestration

You have built pipelines for five years, so most of this branch is consolidation. Two places, though, are where experience most reliably leaves a gap — CDC correctness, where rows go missing in three distinct silent ways, and backfill economics, where the question is how to reprocess two years of history through logic that has changed twice since.

The single idea. A pipeline is not a program that runs. It is a program that will be run again, at an unknown time, over data that has since changed. Idempotency exists because it will run again. Backfills exist because it will run over old data. Incrementality exists because the data changed. CDC exists because it changed underneath you. Testing exists because you cannot inspect the run that has not happened yet.

00 The one question this branch exists to answer

Asked in almost every senior data engineering interview, in one phrasing or another.

? “Your job failed halfway through and you re-ran it. What is the state of the target table?” “It depends on the write semantics, and only one of the three is safe to re-run without thinking.” Append duplicates. Upsert is idempotent only if the key is the grain. Partition overwrite is idempotent by construction — which is why it is the pattern to design toward rather than the one to fall back on.

The second is the one that separates people who have maintained pipelines from people who have written them:

? “Reprocess two years of history. The source schema changed twice in that period.” Four problems at once — correctness (today's logic does not apply to old data), ordering (which models can run in parallel and which cannot), cost (two years of compute arriving simultaneously), and source drift (dimension values were different then). The fourth one is L1 M9 arriving from the pipeline side, and recognising that is the senior move.
The failure mode of this branch Assuming familiarity is sufficient. Most engineers with five years of pipelines have never deliberately broken CDC and watched rows disappear — they have only ever fixed it after a user noticed. The two labs here exist to convert years of incident memory into a failure matrix you can recite.

01 The environment

One rule about clocks does more work here than any tool choice.

ComponentWhy
Airflow, and Dagster alongside itAirflow is what job descriptions ask for. Dagster's asset model is what an orchestrator should be — and building the same DAG in both is the fastest way to understand what task-based orchestration cost you

The rule that makes everything else in this branch possible Never call now() inside a pipeline. Every job takes its logical date as a parameter and derives every time boundary from it.

A pipeline that reads the wall clock cannot be run for a past date, cannot be tested deterministically, and cannot be backfilled at all. This one rule converts backfilling from a project into a loop, and it is the single highest-leverage convention in data engineering.

Two more: every model declares its write semantics — append, upsert, or overwrite-partition — in its config, where a reviewer can see it. And every run records rows in and rows out, which costs nothing and becomes the foundation of L6.

Reading this map P4 is the load-bearing node and everything below it assumes it. An incremental model that is not idempotent is a bug with a schedule. A backfill over non-idempotent writes is an outage you scheduled yourself. CDC is idempotency applied to a stream of changes you did not generate.

P1 ETL versus ELT

ELT won, and the usual explanation is not the real one.

WHAT IT IS. Whether transformation happens before the data lands in the warehouse, or after.

Why ELT actually won Cheap storage and elastic compute are the reasons everyone gives, and they are true. The reason that mattered more is organisational: transformation in the warehouse is SQL in version control that an analyst can read, review and fix. Transformation in flight is Python in a box only the data team can open.

ELT moved the transformation to where the people who understand the business already were. That is why analytics engineering exists as a role, and it is a better answer than "storage got cheap".

WHERE ETL STILL WINS. PII that must never land in the warehouse at all · formats the warehouse cannot read · very large reductions where landing the raw volume is genuinely wasteful · and regulated contexts where the raw data may not cross a boundary.

CONNECTS TO. P10 · L1 M3 · L6 governance.

P2 Orchestration

DAGs, schedules, sensors — and one confusion that causes more incidents than any other.

WHAT IT IS. The system that decides what runs, when, in what order, and what happens when something fails.

The most common orchestration bug in the world The schedule is not the dependency. Two jobs scheduled at 02:00 and 02:15 are not ordered — they are two independent jobs that happen to usually run in a convenient sequence.

It works perfectly until the first job is slow once, and then the second one silently processes yesterday's data and reports success. If B needs A, express it as a dependency or a data sensor. Time is not a dependency mechanism, and using it as one produces failures that are green.

SUBTOPICS. DAGs, schedules and catch-up semantics · task-based orchestration versus asset-based — "run this task" versus "this table should exist and be fresh", and how much lineage the second gives you for free · sensors, and the anti-pattern of a sensor occupying a worker slot for six hours (deferrable operators and reschedule mode as the fix) · retries, exponential backoff and when a retry is wrong · SLAs and alerting on lateness rather than on failure · dynamic task generation and its debugging cost · the difference between a task failing and a task producing wrong output, which is the whole of L6.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free documentation · Title: the Apache Airflow concepts documentation and the Dagster "Thinking in Assets" guide
Why both: free, and they disagree productively. Reading the asset-based framing after years of task-based Airflow reorganises how you think about pipelines — dependencies become statements about data rather than about execution, which is also how lineage and freshness stop being bolt-ons.

P3 Orchestrator internals

Enough to debug it under pressure, which is the only reason to know it.

WHAT IT IS. The scheduler loop, the executor, the task lifecycle, and the metadata database that is simultaneously the source of truth and the usual bottleneck.

SUBTOPICS. The scheduler loop and why the scheduler, not the workers, is normally the scaling limit · executor types and where the work actually runs · task instance states — queued, running, up_for_retry, upstream_failed, skipped — and what each tells you · the metadata database as a contention point · pools, priority weights and concurrency limits as the actual mechanism for protecting a production SLA from a backfill.

Zombie tasks, and where you have seen this before A worker dies without reporting. The scheduler sees a task that is running and has stopped heartbeating. It cannot tell a dead worker from a slow one — so it waits, then declares the task a zombie, then retries, and if it was wrong the task now runs twice.

That is L0 B8's undecidable timeout, arriving inside your orchestrator. Which is also why P4 is not optional: your orchestrator will eventually run a task twice, and it will be correct to do so.

P4 Idempotency and write semantics spine

Three write modes, three re-run behaviours, and one design decision that removes most of this branch's difficulty.

Write modeRe-running producesIdempotent when
AppendDuplicates, alwaysNever. Only safe behind a downstream dedup
Upsert / mergeThe same rows, if the key is rightThe merge key is the grain. A key finer than the grain silently under-deduplicates
Overwrite partitionAn identical partitionBy construction, provided the overwrite is atomic
The design decision to make once and never revisit Make the unit of work a partition. Make writing a partition an overwrite.

Then a re-run is free, a backfill is just many re-runs, and recovery from a failed run is "run it again" rather than an investigation. Almost every hard problem in the rest of this branch is easy under that convention and hard without it.
The gap in delete-then-insert Partition overwrite implemented as DELETE followed by INSERT is not atomic. There is a window in which a reader sees an empty partition and reports zero, entirely successfully.

This is exactly the failure L0 Lab 02 produces, and the fix is the same one: a table format whose commit swaps a single pointer. Partition overwrite is safe on Iceberg or Delta and unsafe on a directory of Parquet, and the difference is invisible until a reader happens to land in the window.

SUBTOPICS. Idempotency keys and deterministic derived keys · merge key selection and its identity with the grain · overwrite-by-partition semantics across engines · absolute values versus deltas · at-least-once orchestration as the default assumption · verifying idempotency by checksum rather than by inspection.

P5 Incremental models and change detection

Where "incremental" stops being a config value and becomes a correctness question.

WHAT IT IS. Processing only what changed, which first requires knowing what changed — and every method of knowing has a specific way of being wrong.

MethodFails on
High-watermark column (updated_at)Hard deletes · clock skew · backdated updates · and the transaction-boundary bug
Log-based CDCNothing, at the cost of privileges and operational burden (P7)
Snapshot diffNothing — but it reads everything, every time
Hash comparisonNothing detectable, but still requires reading the full source
The transaction-boundary bug, which is subtle and permanent You read WHERE updated_at > last_watermark and advance the watermark to now. A transaction that set updated_at to a moment before your read commits a few seconds after it. The row was invisible when you read, and its timestamp is now below your watermark forever.

You do not skip it once. You skip it permanently, and nothing will ever tell you. The mitigations are to lag the watermark behind the longest possible transaction, to use the commit timestamp rather than the row timestamp, or — properly — to read the log instead.

Four independent problems that arrive as one request.

AxisThe questionWhat goes wrong

The procedure, and it is the atomic swap again Backfill into a shadow table. Diff it against production over the overlap window. Then swap.

Write the new thing where nobody is looking, verify it, then flip one pointer. That is L0 B6 for the third time — after table commits and blue-green deployment — and here it converts a backfill from an irreversible act into a reviewable one.

P7 CDC — log-based versus query-based

Two techniques that produce genuinely different data, not two implementations of one idea.

WHAT IT IS. Query-based CDC polls the source for rows whose timestamp exceeds a watermark. Log-based CDC reads the write-ahead log and receives every committed change.

The distinction that matters, and it is not "log-based is better" Query-based CDC gives you the current state of rows that changed. Log-based CDC gives you the change events.

Those are different data supporting different questions. "How many times did this order change status, and in what order" exists only in the log — polling shows you the final state and silently discards every intermediate one. If your model needs event history, no amount of polling frequency recovers it.
Query-basedLog-based
Sees deletesNoYes
Sees intermediate statesNoYes
Load on the source's query pathYesNegligible
Privileges neededSELECTReplication
Operational riskMissed rows at transaction boundariesA stopped consumer retains WAL until the source's disk fills

P8 Snapshot and stream stitching

The hard part of CDC, and the part most treatments skip entirely.

WHAT IT IS. You need the history from before CDC was switched on. So you take a snapshot, start streaming, and must join the two without a gap and without a duplicate — while writes continue throughout.

WHY IT IS HARD. Because the snapshot and the stream overlap in time but not in order. A log event received during the snapshot may be older or newer than the snapshot's version of that same row, and arrival order does not tell you which.

THE MECHANISM. Record the log position at snapshot start. Apply log events from that position forward. Reconcile by primary key and version — an LSN or offset per row — never by arrival order. Upsert semantics then absorb the overlap harmlessly, because reapplying a change you already have is a no-op.

SUBTOPICS. Consistent snapshots and the locking cost of taking one · incremental and watermark-based snapshotting that avoids locking the table · chunked snapshots interleaved with the stream · resuming an interrupted snapshot · re-snapshotting a single table without stopping the connector.

P9 Deletes, tombstones and out-of-order updates

Three ways to lose rows, all of them silent. These are the three failures of Lab 16.

FailureMechanismFix
Invisible hard deleteNothing was updated; the row simply ceased to exist. Query-based CDC cannot see an absenceLog-based CDC, or a periodic reconciliation against a source key list
Evaporated tombstoneA null-value tombstone on a compacted topic is removed by the cleaner before your consumer reads it — and the deleted row resurrectsdelete.retention.ms greater than your worst-case consumer downtime
Reordered updateTwo updates to one key, consumed by parallel workers, applied in arrival order. The older winsUpsert only if the incoming LSN exceeds the stored one. Always. It costs one column
The single rule that prevents two of the three Store the source version — LSN, commit timestamp, or offset — on every row, and make every upsert conditional on it increasing. This is the same conditional write as L0's compare-and-swap commit, applied per row rather than per table. It is one extra column and one extra predicate, and it eliminates an entire class of silent corruption.

SUBTOPICS. Soft versus hard delete in the target, and why soft-deleted rows are still PII (L6) · the __deleted flag convention · reconciliation jobs that compare key sets rather than rows · truncates and DDL, which most CDC connectors handle badly · ordering guarantees when the target is partitioned differently from the source.

P10 Materialisation strategies

View, table, incremental, ephemeral — and the reconciliation everybody forgets.

WHAT IT IS. The choice of whether a model's results are computed at query time, stored, or stored and updated in place.

THE TRADE. Freshness against cost against complexity. A view is always fresh and always expensive. A table is cheap to read and stale between runs. An incremental table is cheap and fresh, and introduces a new failure mode of its own.

The failure that accumulates silently for a year An incremental model can drift from what a full refresh would produce. A late-arriving row outside the incremental window, a changed dimension, a fixed bug applied only to new data — each leaves the incremental table subtly different from the truth.

Every incremental model needs a scheduled full-refresh reconciliation, and most teams do not have one. The drift is invisible because there is nothing to compare against — which is precisely why the comparison has to be scheduled rather than performed when someone gets suspicious.

SUBTOPICS. View, table, incremental, ephemeral and materialised view · incremental strategies — append, merge, insert-overwrite, delete-insert · the lookback window and choosing it from the measured lateness distribution (L4 Lab 13 is the same measurement) · full-refresh cadence as a first-class schedule · when a view is the correct answer and materialising is premature.

CONNECTS TO. P5 · L1 M11 · L6.

P11 Testing pipelines and testing data

Two different activities that are constantly conflated, and both are required.

The distinction Testing the code uses fixed inputs and asserts deterministic outputs. It catches regressions you introduced. Testing the data asserts constraints on values you did not write. It catches reality changing.

They fail at different times for different reasons, and a team with only one of them is exposed in a direction it cannot see.

SUBTOPICS. Fixture-based unit tests with tiny handcrafted inputs — ten rows you wrote yourself beat a million you did not · assertion tests on outputs: uniqueness, not-null, referential integrity, accepted values, freshness, row-count bounds · contract tests at boundaries (L6) · testing against a sample rather than production · a test that the pipeline is idempotent — run twice, assert identical checksum — which almost nobody writes and which would catch most of Lab 19's failures automatically.

The test that is secretly a modelling assertion A uniqueness test on the grain columns is the grain, expressed as an executable claim. It is the only place in a codebase where L1 M5's sentence becomes something a machine can check — which is a good argument for writing it on every fact table, even where you are certain it holds.

P12 When a pipeline should not exist

The counterpart node, and the most valuable habit in the branch.

Do not build one when The source can be queried directly at acceptable cost. A pipeline that copies a small table so a dashboard can read it is a liability with a schedule.
The consumer is one person, once a quarter. That is a query.
The transformation is a view. Materialising something cheap adds a failure mode and a staleness window in exchange for nothing.
The request was for a table rather than for an answer. Ask what decision the data will inform. Sometimes the honest conclusion is that no pipeline is needed, and delivering that is worth more than delivering the pipeline.
The obligation nobody mentions when you build it Every pipeline you create is a permanent maintenance obligation, because nobody will ever ask you to delete one. Requests to build are constant; requests to decommission are approximately zero.

Pipeline sprawl is created here and paid for in L8, and a senior engineer is someone who counts the future cost at the moment of creation rather than at the moment of migration.

16 Laboratory — CDC Correctness

Rows lost three distinct ways, all silently.

PROBLEM. Replicate a Postgres orders table into a warehouse table. It must match the source exactly, continuously, under a live write workload.

INITIAL SYSTEM. Query-based CDC: poll WHERE updated_at > watermark every five minutes and upsert by primary key. Plus a full-table checksum comparator that runs independently and reports divergence — without which none of the failures below are visible.
FAILURE TO INTRODUCE — three, and each is silent 1. Hard delete. Delete a row at the source. It remains in the target forever. Nothing was updated, so nothing was polled.
2. Transaction boundary. A long transaction whose updated_at precedes your poll commits after it. The row is skipped permanently.
3. Reordered updates. Two updates to one key, consumed by parallel workers, applied in arrival order. The older value wins and will never be corrected.

Then switch to log-based CDC and meet two new failures: the evaporated tombstone, and the snapshot-to-stream gap.

CONCEPTS THAT EMERGE. Absence is not an update → watermark lag and commit timestamps → the WAL as the only complete record → LSN-conditional upserts → replication slots and source disk risk → tombstones and delete.retention.ms → consistent snapshots → stitching by key and version rather than by arrival.

#ExperimentWhat it isolates
1Query-based baseline, inserts and updates only. Verify by full-table checksumIt works — which is why the method survives in production
2Add deletes. Plot divergence over timeMonotonically increasing wrongness, never alerted
3Construct the transaction-boundary case with an explicit long transactionPermanent, undetectable row loss
4Introduce clock skew between source and pipeline; measure the loss windowWhy the watermark must lag
5Switch to Debezium; repeat experiments 2 and 3Both disappear — establish what log-based buys
6Rapid successive updates through parallel consumers: apply by arrival, then by LSN comparisonThe one-column fix for an entire failure class
7Stop the consumer for an hour. Watch pg_replication_slots. Compute time-to-disk-fullThe operational risk log-based CDC introduces
8Let a tombstone be compacted away before consumptionA deleted row resurrects
9Snapshot while writes continue; stitch to the stream; verify no gap and no duplicateThe part most treatments omit entirely
10Schema change at source: add a column, drop one, change a typeThree different downstream outcomes, one of which is silent

WHAT TO OBSERVE. The checksum comparison after every scenario — it is the instrument, and everything else is commentary. Divergence over time in experiment 2. Retained WAL in experiment 7. The exact moment the resurrected row reappears in experiment 8.

THE ARTIFACT. A CDC failure matrix — failure mode × query-based × log-based × mitigation × how you would detect it. The detection column is the one that matters, because every failure in this lab is silent by default.

REAL-WORLD CONNECTION. Every "the warehouse does not match the source" ticket, and the reason they take weeks: the divergence started months before anyone noticed, and there is no record of when.

PORTFOLIO VALUE. High, and specifically high for payments and any regulated context, where "our replica is provably consistent with the source, and here is the continuous check that proves it" is the actual requirement.

19 Laboratory — Backfill & Idempotency

Run it twice and everything doubles. Then reprocess two years and cause an outage.

PROBLEM. A daily aggregation pipeline. It must be safe to re-run, safe to run for any past date, and capable of reprocessing 24 months without breaking the daily schedule.

INITIAL SYSTEM. Append-mode writes. now() called inside the transformation. No partitioning. A run ledger recording parameters, row counts and an output checksum for every run — the instrument that makes idempotency checkable rather than assumed.
FAILURE TO INTRODUCE — six escalations 1. Re-run yesterday. Every number doubles.
2. Remove now() and discover the pipeline cannot be run for a past date at all — the logic assumed "today" in four places.
3. Switch to partition overwrite. Idempotent. Kill it mid-write on plain Parquet and a reader sees an empty partition and reports zero, successfully.
4. Backfill 24 months in parallel. The daily run misses its SLA — the backfill caused the incident.
5. The backfilled numbers differ from the originals for old dates. A dimension changed. Now somebody must decide whether history is allowed to change.
6. A model with a running total is corrupted by parallel backfill, because its partitions were never independent.

CONCEPTS THAT EMERGE. Write semantics and re-run behaviour → merge key as the grain → atomic partition overwrite → logical date parameterisation → resource isolation for backfills → order-dependence as a property to inventory → source drift and restatement policy → shadow-diff-swap as the safe procedure.

#ExperimentWhat it isolates

WHAT TO OBSERVE. Row counts and output checksums across double runs. The daily job's runtime during an unisolated backfill. The experiment-10 diff — which rows changed and by how much — because that diff is what you would show a stakeholder before restating anything.

THE ARTIFACT. An idempotency and backfill runbook: write semantics declared per model, an inventory of which models are parallel-safe, and the shadow-diff-swap procedure written down. The sentence it earns: "Every model declares its write semantics and its parallel safety, and no backfill reaches production without a diff."

REAL-WORLD CONNECTION. The re-run question is asked in nearly every senior interview. The backfill-caused-an-outage story is one almost every experienced engineer has and few can explain structurally.

PORTFOLIO VALUE. High, and it is the cheapest lab in the Forge to run — no cluster, no streaming, one afternoon.
