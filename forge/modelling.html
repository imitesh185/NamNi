Data architecture & modelling

This is where five years of experience shows most — and also where it is most often shallow in one specific place. Almost everyone can build a star schema. Far fewer can state a grain as a sentence before writing any SQL, or explain why type 2 alone does not answer the question the business actually asked.

The single idea. A data model is a set of decisions about what you will no longer be able to answer. Modelling is subtraction. Every aggregation, every conflated dimension, every dropped attribute closes a door — and the grain is the largest door of all, because you can always aggregate up and you can never recover detail you did not keep.
#grain #dimensional #scd #temporal #physical-design

00 The one question this branch exists to answer

It sounds like a formality. It is the whole examination.

? “What is the grain of this table?” A weak answer describes the table. A strong answer is a single sentence of the form “one row per X per Y per Z”, followed immediately by the list of questions that grain forecloses. The second half is what almost nobody supplies, and it is the half that demonstrates you understand modelling as an irreversible act.

The second question is the one that looks like a business trivia question and is actually a four-layer technical one:

? “A salesperson moves from the North region to the South. What happens to last year's report?” The correct first move is to ask a question back: should last year's sale appear under North or South? Both are legitimate, and the business must choose. And the real answer is usually “both, at different times” — which is why type 2 alone is insufficient and why type 6 exists.
The failure mode of this branch Confusing familiarity with depth. You have built dimensional models for years, which makes this the easiest branch to skim and the easiest to be caught in. The two sub-branches that reliably separate people are SCD beyond type 2, and physical design — the first because most engineers stop at type 2, the second because it is where modelling meets L2 performance and most modellers have never measured anything.

01 The environment

No cluster. What this branch needs instead is a requirement that changes when you are not expecting it.

ComponentWhy
DuckDB or Postgres, plus dbtModelling is expressed as SQL under version control. dbt is free, and the tests and documentation features are used directly in L6
A realistic seed dataset with orders, order lines, customers, products, promotions and a sales hierarchyMust contain a many-to-many relationship, a slowly changing attribute and a hierarchy — otherwise half the branch has nothing to model
A written requirements log, datedThe single most important instrument in this branch
Saved outputs of every report you produceLab 04 works by diffing a report against its own earlier output. Without the saved copy the central failure is invisible
An Iceberg or Delta table for the physical-design nodePartition and clustering experiments need a table format that can evolve, or M11 stays theoretical
The one methodological rule, and it is not optional Seal the future requirements in an envelope. Write all four requirement changes for Lab 03 before you start, then do not read change 2 until change 1 is delivered.

A modelling lab where you know the future requirements teaches nothing, because you will unconsciously model for them. The entire value of the exercise is being genuinely unable to answer a question, and that experience cannot be simulated once you have seen the question.
Two habits State the grain as a sentence, in a comment, at the top of every model file. It takes ten seconds and it is the single highest-value documentation in a warehouse. Record what each model cannot answer, alongside what it can. The second list is the one that gets you promoted and the one nobody writes.

M1 The data lifecycle

Generation → ingestion → storage → transformation → serving. Useful mainly for locating blame correctly.

WHAT IT IS. The five stages every piece of data passes through, plus the concerns that run underneath all of them — security, data management, orchestration, architecture and engineering practice.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because most data failures are attributed to the wrong stage. A "bad dashboard" is a serving-stage symptom of, almost always, a generation- or ingestion-stage cause — and teams spend weeks in the transformation layer looking for a bug that was never there.

DEPENDENCIES. None.

SUBTOPICS. The five stages and their distinct failure signatures · source systems as things you do not control · the boundary of your responsibility versus the boundary of your blame · the undercurrents, and why security and data management are not stages · where the data engineer's job sits relative to analytics engineering and platform engineering.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free articles · Title: the Kimball Group Design Tips archive and the dbt Analytics Engineering Guide
Why these: both free. The canonical text here is Kimball's Data Warehouse Toolkit, which is not free — but the Kimball Group published a free "Dimensional Modeling Techniques" reference covering all forty-plus techniques, plus roughly 180 short Design Tips, and together they cover the same ground with more worked examples.

M2 OLTP versus OLAP

Not a preference. Four opposing pressures that no single system resolves.

WHAT IT IS. Two workload shapes that pull storage, indexing, concurrency and normalisation in opposite directions at the same time.

PressureOLTP wantsOLAP wants
Access patternPoint reads and writes of whole recordsWide scans of few columns
StorageRow-oriented, B-tree indexedColumnar, sorted, compressed
ConcurrencyThousands of short transactionsFew long queries that monopolise memory
NormalisationNormalised, to avoid update anomaliesDenormalised, to avoid joins

WHY IT MATTERS TO YOU SPECIFICALLY. The boundary between these two systems is where the entire data engineering profession lives. CDC, ELT, replication and reverse ETL are all bridges over exactly this gap — which is why the gap is worth being able to describe precisely.

DEPENDENCIES. L0 B1 · L0 B10.

OBSERVATION · Why you do not run analytics on the primary Run a full-table analytical scan against a Postgres instance that is also serving transactions. Watch transactional p99 latency rise while the scan runs, and watch the buffer cache fill with pages the transactional workload did not want.

The failure is not that the analytical query is slow — it is that it degrades the system you were not supposed to touch. That is a measurement, and it is a much better answer than "because it's bad practice".

CONNECTS TO. M3 · L5 CDC · Databases N1.

M3 Warehouse, lake, lakehouse

Three real axes, and one debate that is mostly over.

WHAT IT IS. Three architectures distinguished by whether storage and compute are coupled, whether the format is open or proprietary, and whether schema is enforced on write or on read.

The honest current position Separated storage and compute has won and is now universal. Schema-on-read as a philosophy lost, because it produced swamps. What genuinely remains live is the open format question — and that is an economic argument about lock-in and multi-engine access, not a performance argument.

Being able to say that plainly, rather than reciting three definitions, is the difference between sounding current and sounding like a 2019 blog post.

SUBTOPICS. Coupled versus separated storage and compute, and what separation actually bought · open format versus proprietary and precisely what each locks in · schema-on-write versus schema-on-read and why the latter degraded into swamps · the medallion / bronze-silver-gold layering as a convention rather than an architecture · data mesh as an organisational answer, not a technical one, and therefore mostly an L8 topic.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free paper · Title: "Lakehouse: A New Generation of Open Platforms that Unify Data Warehousing and Advanced Analytics" (CIDR 2021), free
Why this one: free, and it argues the position rather than asserting it — including the concessions, which is where the useful content is. Read it as an argument to be evaluated, not a description to be memorised, since the authors have a commercial interest in the conclusion.
OBSERVATION · The entire argument for open formats, in one experiment Write one Iceberg table. Query it from DuckDB, from Spark, and from Trino — without copying anything. The fact that you can is the whole case, and it takes twenty minutes to demonstrate something that is otherwise argued about in slide decks.

CONNECTS TO. L0 B5 · L8 · M11.

M4 Batch, streaming and the hybrid nobody admits to

Lambda and Kappa, and why almost every real system is neither.

WHAT IT IS. Lambda runs a streaming layer for speed and a batch layer for truth, and reconciles them. Kappa says the streaming layer is sufficient and replay handles corrections. Most production systems are lambda while claiming to be kappa.

The reframe that makes "hybrid" stop looking like indecision The batch layer is not a legacy artifact. It is the correction mechanism for the streaming layer's watermark guess.

L4 W3 establishes that a watermark is a heuristic that will sometimes be wrong. A nightly batch recomputation is precisely the reconciliation that heuristic requires — which is why finance keeps one and will not give it up, and why "we're moving to pure streaming" usually means "we have not yet met the auditors".

DEPENDENCIES. L4 for the honest version of this node.

SUBTOPICS. Lambda and its duplicated-logic cost · Kappa and its dependence on replayability · micro-batch as a third option that is usually the right one · the latency ladder — daily, hourly, fifteen-minute, one-minute, sub-second — and the cost step between each · choosing the cheapest rung that meets the requirement.

CONNECTS TO. L4 W12 · L7.

M5 Grain spine

The only modelling decision that is genuinely unrecoverable.

WHAT IT IS. The precise definition of what one row of a fact table represents, stated as a sentence: "one row per order line, per shipment, per day."

WHY IT IS IRREVERSIBLE. You can always aggregate up. You can never disaggregate down. Every other modelling mistake costs a rewrite of code you still have; this one costs data you never captured, and no amount of engineering recovers it.

DEPENDENCIES. M2.

Kimball's four steps, and the one everybody skips 1. Select the business process. 2. Declare the grain. 3. Identify the dimensions. 4. Identify the facts.

Step 2 comes before step 3, and skipping it is the origin of most bad models. When the grain is declared after the dimensions, it becomes whatever the dimensions happened to imply — which is how mixed-grain fact tables get built by people who would never have built one deliberately.
The most common dimensional modelling bug in the world Order-header facts and order-line facts in the same table. The header amount is repeated on every line, so any aggregation that joins to a line-level dimension double-counts the header values by exactly the number of lines per order — and the result looks plausible, which is why it survives review.

The fix is two fact tables at two grains. Lab 03 experiment 5 builds the bug on purpose so that you have seen the number be wrong.

SUBTOPICS. Declaring grain as a written sentence · the finest practical grain as the default, and the storage argument against it (which columnar compression has largely dissolved) · mixed-grain fact tables and their signature · atomic versus aggregated fact tables coexisting · what a grain forecloses, written down alongside what it enables.

The interview move When handed any modelling question, state the grain out loud as a sentence before doing anything else. It reframes you from "someone who writes SQL" to "someone who models" in about four seconds, and it is entirely free — the sentence takes no preparation beyond the habit.

M6 Facts

Three types, three questions they answer cheaply, and one rule about ratios.

TypeOne row perWhat it makes cheap
TransactionalEventEverything, at the cost of size. The most flexible and the default
Periodic snapshotEntity per period"What was the balance at month end" — and it is the only model that records that nothing happened
Accumulating snapshotProcess instance, updated in place as milestones complete"Average days between picked and shipped" — near-impossible from a transactional fact, trivial here

DEPENDENCIES. M5.

The additivity rule, stated as an instruction Never store a ratio. Store the numerator and the denominator, and compute the ratio at query time.

A ratio cannot be aggregated — the average of percentages is not the percentage of the total — and somebody will eventually sum or average that column. Storing the components makes the wrong answer impossible rather than merely discouraged, which is the correct place to solve it.

Additive (most measures) · semi-additive (balances — additive across everything except time) · non-additive (ratios, percentages, unit prices).

SUBTOPICS. The three fact types and their update semantics · additive, semi-additive and non-additive measures · factless fact tables — for events with no measure, and for coverage questions · why "which promoted products sold nothing" is unanswerable without a factless coverage table, and why that is a genuinely non-obvious modelling move · degenerate dimensions living on the fact.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free reference · Title: Kimball Group, "Dimensional Modeling Techniques" — the free official technique reference
Why this one: free, and it is a numbered list of every pattern in the discipline with a paragraph each. Skim it whole once; you will discover three or four patterns you have needed and reinvented badly.

M7 Dimensions

Including the two patterns that quietly produce wrong numbers.

WHAT IT IS. The descriptive context of a fact — who, what, where, when, why — modelled as wide, denormalised tables joined by surrogate keys.

DEPENDENCIES. M5.

PatternWhat it solves
Conformed dimensionMakes two fact tables comparable. Achieving one is an organisational accomplishment, not a technical one
Degenerate dimensionAn identifier with no attributes — the order number — kept on the fact rather than given a table
Junk dimensionBundles a dozen low-cardinality flags into one small dimension rather than a dozen columns
Role-playing dimensionOne date dimension, viewed three times as order date, ship date, delivery date
Bridge tableMany-to-many — a patient with several diagnoses. And it always requires an explicit double-counting decision
Late-arriving dimensionA fact referencing a member that does not exist yet — handled with an inferred member, then backfilled
The bridge table trap Joining a fact to a dimension through a bridge multiplies the fact rows by the number of bridge entries. Sum the amount and you have counted each sale once per diagnosis.

The fix is an allocation factor on the bridge — weights summing to one — and the choice of weights is a business decision you must not make silently. Most people do not know this exists, and it produces wrong numbers that look entirely reasonable.

SUBTOPICS. Surrogate keys and why natural keys fail as joins · durable natural keys alongside surrogates (essential for M9) · dimension hierarchies, fixed and ragged · the unknown / inferred member · dimension outriggers and when they are justified · snowflaking a single large shared dimension.

CONNECTS TO. M8 · M9 · Lab 04.

M8 Star versus snowflake

The real trade-off, which is not the one usually stated.

WHAT IT IS. Star keeps each dimension in one denormalised table. Snowflake normalises dimensions into hierarchies of tables.

The trade-off is not storage Columnar compression makes the redundancy in a denormalised dimension nearly free — a repeated category name compresses to almost nothing. So the disk argument, which is the one usually given, is dead.

The live trade-off is join count and comprehensibility against update cost. Star: fewer joins, easier for BI tools and humans, but a hierarchy change may touch millions of rows. Snowflake: cheaper hierarchy updates and a genuinely shared large dimension, at the cost of every query being harder to write and to optimise.

Modern answer: star, almost always. Know the two conditions that flip it, and say them, rather than treating the choice as a matter of taste.

DEPENDENCIES. M7.

CONNECTS TO. M10 · L0 B9 — join count is a planner problem, and cardinality estimation degrades with it.

M9 Slowly changing dimensions, types 1–6 deepen

Most people know type 2 and stop. The types are not a difficulty ladder.

WHAT IT IS. The family of techniques for handling a dimension attribute that changes over time.

The reframe that makes all six memorable They are not levels of sophistication. They are answers to different questions.

Do I need history at all? → 0, 1, 2  ·  How much history — all of it, or just the previous value? → 2 versus 3  ·  Does type 2 explode my row count? → 4, 5  ·  Do I need the historical and the current view at the same time? → 6.

"Type 2 is the good one" is the belief this node exists to remove.
TypeMechanismCorrect when
0Never change the valueOriginal credit score, original acquisition channel
1OverwriteIt is a correction, not a change. A misspelled name
2New row, effective dates, current flagAnything you will report on historically. The workhorse
3Add a "previous value" columnExactly one prior state matters — one reorganisation, one rebranding
4Mini-dimension: split rapidly-changing attributes into their own dimensionType 2 on a 50-attribute customer dimension explodes the row count
5Type 4 plus a type 1 outrigger to the current mini-dimension rowYou need the mini-dimension and fast access to current values
6Type 2 rows that also carry a current-value columnBoth "as it was" and "as it is now", from one query
Beyond type 6 — the bi-temporal case, and a bridge worth naming Type 2 records when something was true. It has no mechanism for when you found out — so a retroactive correction ("the move was actually the 1st, not the 15th") cannot be represented without destroying the audit trail.

Bi-temporal modelling keeps both valid time and transaction time, and that is exactly the event-time versus processing-time distinction from L4 W1, arriving in a completely different branch. Valid time is when it was true in the world; transaction time is when the system learned it. Naming that equivalence out loud is a strong senior signal, and it is the kind of connection that only appears once you have both branches.

M10 Normalisation, Data Vault and One Big Table

Three patterns that are usually presented as rivals and are actually layers.

WHAT IT IS. Three ways to organise data that is not yet, or no longer, in a star schema.

PatternReal value propositionReal cost
3NF integration layer (Inmon)A single integrated, non-redundant enterprise modelSlow to build, and unusable for BI without marts on top
Data Vault — hubs, links, satellitesAuditability and parallel loading with no cross-source dependency — every source loads independentlyA great many joins. It is a raw-integration pattern, not a serving pattern
One Big TableZero joins for one known access pattern; columnar pruning makes width freeCannot answer a question it was not shaped for
The sentence that resolves the argument Model for the query you know; keep the grain fine enough for the ones you do not.

Serving BI directly from a Data Vault is a misunderstanding of what a vault is for, and saying so — with the reason, which is that hubs and satellites optimise for load-time independence rather than query-time convenience — is a clean way to demonstrate that you know the pattern rather than the name.

M11 Physical design deepen

Where modelling meets L0 and L2, and where most modellers have never measured anything.

WHAT IT IS. Partitioning strategy, clustering and sort order, and file sizing — the decisions that determine what a query costs, as opposed to what it can express.

The division of labour, in one line The grain decides what you can ask. The physical design decides what it costs. Two independent decisions that are constantly conflated, usually by people who partition on a column nobody filters on and then wonder why it did not help.

DEPENDENCIES. M5 · L0 B7 · L0 B2.

SUBTOPICS. Partition column selection — in most queries' predicates and low enough cardinality, both conditions · partition pruning, and confirming it happened rather than assuming · clustering, sorting and Z-ordering as the tool for high-cardinality filter columns · sorting as what makes footer statistics selective · file sizing and the small-files problem · hidden partitioning in Iceberg versus Hive-style directories, and the class of bug it eliminates · when to pre-aggregate and when it is premature.

OBSERVATION · Confirm the pruning, do not assume it Partition a table, then run a query with a predicate on the partition column and read the plan for the number of partitions actually scanned.

A predicate wrapped in a function, or with a type mismatch, silently disables pruning — WHERE date_trunc('day', ts) = ... against a ts-partitioned table scans everything, and nothing warns you. Having been caught by this once is worth more than reading about it three times.

CONNECTS TO. L2 S11 · L0 Lab 01 · L8 cost.

M12 Semantic and metric layers

Where the grain decision presents its bill.

WHAT IT IS. A definition of each business metric expressed once, in version control, from which queries are generated — rather than the same logic copy-pasted into forty dashboards.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because three teams will otherwise produce three definitions of "active user", three numbers, and a meeting. The problem is not that the numbers differ; it is that nobody can say which is right, because the definitions live in dashboard SQL nobody reads.

DEPENDENCIES. M5 · M6.

Why this node is in L1 and not L8 A metric layer is a modelling artifact, not a platform feature. It can only express metrics the underlying grain supports. You cannot define "sessions per user per week" over a table whose grain is monthly, and no amount of tooling will rescue that.

This is the node where an insufficient grain finally becomes undeniable to people outside the data team — which is why M5 and M12 are the first and last nodes of the same argument.

SUBTOPICS. Metric definitions as code · dimensional consistency across metrics · the pattern behind dbt MetricFlow, Cube and LookML rather than the products · governed versus ad-hoc access · the certification question — which tables are trustworthy, and who says so · why a semantic layer without L6 contracts underneath it is decoration.

03 Laboratory — The Grain Trap

A requirement change that no amount of SQL can satisfy.

PROBLEM. Model a retail business for reporting. Requirement one: daily sales by store and product. Deliver it.

INITIAL SYSTEM. A fact table at the grain "one row per store per product per day". Correct, efficient, and exactly what was asked for. Plus three further requirements, written in advance and sealed — not to be read until the previous one ships.
FAILURE TO INTRODUCE — four sealed requirements 1. "Break it down by promotion." The daily summary aggregated promotion away. Rebuild at a finer grain and reprocess. Annoying, recoverable.
2. "How many baskets contained both A and B?" This is not hard. It is impossible. Basket composition was destroyed by the grain, and no SQL, no tooling and no amount of time recovers it.
3. "What is average order value?" You have lines but no order-level fact — and the naive answer double-counts.
4. "What did the report say last March, before the restatement?" Straight into Lab 04.

CONCEPTS THAT EMERGE. Grain as an irreversible decision → the finest practical grain as the default → mixed-grain fact tables and double counting → additivity and the ratio rule → periodic versus accumulating snapshots → factless facts for coverage questions → the real storage cost of fine grain, measured rather than feared.

#ExperimentWhat it isolates
1Model at day-store-product grain; deliver requirement 1. Record rows and query timeThe baseline that is genuinely correct for what was asked
2Attempt the promotion breakdown. Write down precisely why it failsRecoverable loss — costs a reprocess
3Rebuild at transaction-line grain. Measure storage and query cost against the summaryThe cost of fine grain is far smaller than people assume, because columnar
4Attempt basket affinity at each grainUnrecoverable loss. The centre of the lab
5Build a deliberately mixed-grain fact (header + line). Compute revenueThe double count, and how plausible it looks

WHAT TO OBSERVE. Which questions become unanswerable at each grain, and which merely become expensive — the distinction is the whole lab. The storage delta in experiment 3. The magnitude of the double count in experiment 5.

THE ARTIFACT. A grain decision record: the grain as a sentence, the questions it answers, the questions it forecloses, and the storage cost of one level finer. The third column is the one nobody writes, and it is the one that reads as senior.

REAL-WORLD CONNECTION. Every "can you just add a breakdown by X" request that turns into a three-week reprocessing project — and the rarer, worse version where the answer is "we cannot, and we never will be able to."

PORTFOLIO VALUE. Medium-high, and different in kind from the others: this is a written artifact demonstrating judgement rather than a system demonstrating skill. Both are needed.

The data structure this lab forces Basket affinity is a frequent-itemset problem, and the obvious pairwise self-join is quadratic in basket size. You are not required to implement Apriori or FP-growth here — but noticing that the query you were casually asked for is combinatorial is the point, and it is the same instinct that the DSA table is built to develop.

04 Laboratory — Temporal Modelling

A salesperson moves region and last year's report changes.

PROBLEM. Priya moves from North to South on 15 June. Sales reports must remain correct — and "correct" turns out to have two incompatible meanings that the business has never been asked to choose between.

INITIAL SYSTEM. A type 1 salesperson dimension. The region is overwritten. Plus a saved copy of last year's regional report, produced before the change — without which the central failure of this lab is invisible.
FAILURE TO INTRODUCE Re-run last year's report and diff it against the saved copy. A number that was published, screenshotted and cited in a board pack is now different, and nobody edited anything.

Then fix it with type 2 — and discover the second failure immediately: "show me all of Priya's sales this year" now returns her as two people, because a naive group-by on the surrogate key splits her across the effective-date boundary.

CONCEPTS THAT EMERGE. Type 1 as destruction of history → type 2 and effective dating → surrogate versus durable natural keys → "as-was" and "as-is" as two legitimate and distinct questions → type 6 → late-arriving facts and dimensions → retroactive correction, which type 2 cannot express → valid time versus transaction time → bi-temporal modelling.

#ExperimentWhat it isolates
1Type 1 overwrite; re-run last year's report; diff against the saved outputThe published number changed. The whole lab in one diff
2Type 2 with effective_from / effective_to / is_current; re-runHistory is now stable
3Aggregate this year by surrogate keyPriya appears twice. The type 2 side effect nobody warns about
4Add a durable natural key; aggregate on itBoth questions answerable — the reason both keys exist
5"Current region for all historical sales": via a type 6 column, then via a join to is_current. Compare costType 6 as denormalised convenience with a maintenance cost
6Late-arriving fact: a 10 June sale arrives on 20 June. Ensure it binds to the 10 June dimension versionThe most common type 2 implementation bug
7Late-arriving dimension: a sale references an unknown salesperson. Inferred member, then backfillReferential integrity without blocking the load
8Retroactive correction — the move was actually 1 June. Attempt it with type 2 aloneType 2 has no mechanism for "when we found out"
9Implement bi-temporal (valid time + transaction time); redo experiment 8Answer both "what is true" and "what did we believe on date D"
10Type 4 mini-dimension for 20 rapidly-changing attributes; compare row counts to pure type 2Why types 4 and 5 exist at all

WHAT TO OBSERVE. The experiment-1 diff, kept as a file — it is the most persuasive artifact in the lab. Row-count growth of type 2 versus type 4. The query cost of reconstructing an "as of" view. And in experiment 8, exactly which question becomes unanswerable.

THE ARTIFACT. A temporal decision table — question type → SCD type required → storage cost → query cost. It converts "which SCD type should we use" from an opinion into a lookup.

REAL-WORLD CONNECTION. Regulated reporting, financial restatement, and every "why did the number change" escalation. In payments and finance the bi-temporal requirement is not exotic — it is the baseline, because "what did we believe on the reporting date" is an audit question, not an analytics one.

PORTFOLIO VALUE. High, and specifically high for finance and payments roles. Bi-temporal modelling implemented and explained is a genuine differentiator, because most candidates have not heard the term.
