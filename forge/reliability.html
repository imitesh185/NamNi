Reliability & governance

This branch exists because of one asymmetry, and everything in it is a response to that asymmetry. It is also, more than any other branch, what a company means when it says it wants someone senior — because the failures here are not solved by knowing a tool.

The single idea. A broken service returns an error. A broken pipeline returns plausible numbers. Nothing throws. Nothing pages. A human reads the number, believes it, and acts on it — and by the time anyone doubts it, they have been acting for weeks. Every technique in this branch is defence against plausibility.
#silent-failure #contracts #lineage #detection-latency #governance

00 The one question this branch exists to answer

Most candidates answer a different question, confidently.

? “How would you know if this pipeline started producing wrong data?” The common answer — "we'd get an alert if it failed" — answers a question that was not asked. The pipeline did not fail. It succeeded, on schedule, and wrote numbers that are wrong. A strong answer names the checks, states which failure each one catches, and admits which failures none of them catch.

The second is the change-management version, and it is where lineage earns its cost:

? “A producer is about to rename a column. What happens?” Without lineage: you find out when a dashboard breaks, and you do not know how many others will. With table-level lineage: "thirty-seven tables might be affected." With column-level lineage: "three will break, here they are, and here are their owners." That gap is the entire business case, stated in one sentence.
The quantity this branch actually engineers Detection latency — the interval between a fault occurring and someone knowing.

Cost ≈ blast radius × detection latency × decision stakes. You cannot much reduce blast radius — a warehouse exists precisely to make data widely reachable. Stakes are not yours to set. Detection latency is the only term you control, which is why almost all of L6 is about noticing rather than preventing.

01 The environment

Resist buying tooling for a problem you have not yet measured.

ComponentWhy
The L5 pipelines and run ledgerYou cannot instrument what you have not built. The run ledger is already half an observability system
dbt tests, plus Soda Core or Great ExpectationsAll free. The honest note: dbt tests plus a metrics table gets you most of the way, and knowing that is worth more than knowing the tools
A fault injector — deterministic, parameterised, repeatableThe instrument of Lab 17. You must be able to introduce a specific silent fault on demand and measure how long it survives
OpenLineage + Marquez, or DataHub / OpenMetadataFree and open source. Lab 20 needs a real lineage graph, not a diagram
A SQL parser — sqlglot or equivalentColumn-level lineage is produced by parsing, and building a small version yourself teaches more than adopting one
Two alert channels, deliberately separatedA page and a ticket queue. If everything goes to one channel, you have one channel that is ignored
Three habits, and the first one is a discipline Every check must state its false-positive cost and the action its owner will take. If nobody can say what they would do when it fires, do not build it. Measure detection latency, not detection. "We caught it" is meaningless without "in four minutes" or "in six weeks". Alert on the data, not on the job. A green job with wrong output is the entire subject; alerting on job failure is solving the easy half.

R1 The asymmetry spine

The defining fact of the discipline, and the reason this branch is not optional.

WHAT IT IS. In software, a bug produces an exception, a 500, a failed health check — a signal that something is wrong, emitted by the system itself. In data, a bug produces a number. Numbers do not throw.

WHY IT COMPOUNDS. Because the number is consumed by a human who will believe it. There is no type system between your pipeline and someone's decision. By the time anyone doubts the figure, they have been acting on it for weeks and reporting it upward.

DEPENDENCIES. L5 — you need pipelines to instrument.

The framing to carry into an interview "The failure mode I design against is not the pipeline breaking. It is the pipeline succeeding and being wrong."

That single sentence reorganises how an interviewer hears everything you say afterwards, because it demonstrates that you have understood the discipline's actual hazard rather than importing a web-services mental model into it.

CONNECTS TO. R2 · R9 · Data Engineering N7.

R2 The five quality dimensions

And a ranking, because a list of five is not advice.

DimensionThe checkCatchesCost
Freshnessmax(event_time) against an expectationStalled sources, stalled watermarks, dead schedulesNear zero. Start here, everywhere
VolumeRow count within bounds, by partitionPartial loads — the most common silent failure there isNear zero
DistributionNull rate, mean, cardinality, category proportionsSemantic changes, unit changes, upstream logic changesReal, in false positives
SchemaColumns present, types unchangedStructural breaks — and remarkably little elseNear zero
UniquenessThe grain columns are uniqueJoin fan-out, duplicate loads, broken merge keysModerate — it is a full scan
The prioritisation, which is the actual content of this node Freshness and volume catch the majority of real incidents and cost almost nothing. Put them on every table before you put a distribution check on any table.

Teams routinely start with elaborate statistical profiling and never get freshness checks onto half their tables — which is backwards, and the reason is that profiling is more interesting to build than a max(timestamp) comparison.
PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free documentation · Title: dbt's tests and source freshness documentation, plus Soda Core's check reference
Why this one: free, and dbt's built-in tests are exactly the four cheapest checks — unique, not_null, accepted_values, relationships — which is a useful signal about what actually matters. Anything beyond those four should be justified by an incident you have had.

R3 Referential integrity across systems

Inside a database the engine enforces it. Across systems there is only reconciliation.

WHAT IT IS. Facts referencing dimension members that do not exist, dimension members with no facts, and counts that should agree between two systems and do not.

WHY IT IS SEPARATE FROM R2. Because it cannot be checked from inside one dataset. It requires reading two systems and comparing them, which nothing will trigger for you — nothing goes wrong; things merely stop being true.

The cheap version that catches the expensive failure Compare key sets, not rows. Pull the primary keys from source and target, hash each set, compare. Cheap enough to run hourly on tables far too large to compare row by row.

This is the only thing that catches the invisible hard delete from L5 P9, and it is roughly thirty lines of code.

SUBTOPICS. Orphaned facts and the inferred-member pattern · scheduled reconciliation as a first-class job · sampling versus full comparison and when each is defensible · reconciliation across a system boundary you do not control · what to do when the source is itself wrong.

CONNECTS TO. L5 Lab 16 · L1 M7 · R9.

R4 Anomaly detection and its false-positive economics

The most seductive idea in the branch, and the one most likely to make things worse.

THE IDEA. Learn what normal looks like, alert on deviation. It is appealing because it appears to require no domain knowledge.

WHY IT MISBEHAVES. Because data is seasonal, bursty, and legitimately changes. Black Friday is an anomaly. A marketing campaign is an anomaly. Onboarding a large client is an anomaly. A naive detector fires on all of them, correctly identifying that something is unusual and uselessly failing to identify that it is fine.

The economics, stated plainly An alert that is wrong ninety percent of the time trains people to ignore the channel — which means the other ten percent is ignored too.

A bad detector is worse than no detector, because it consumes the signal value of the channel it uses. This is the argument that stops a well-meaning quality initiative from making a team permanently less responsive, and it is worth being able to make it out loud.
The two-channel rule Page when someone is being harmed right now. Ticket for everything else. Almost every data quality signal is a ticket — and deciding that per check, in advance, is what keeps the page meaningful.

The practical middle ground: wide static thresholds catch most things at near-zero false-positive cost; use statistical detection only where you have measured the base rate; and route anything uncertain to the ticket queue by default.

R5 Data contracts

A producer obligation and a consumer expectation, enforced by a machine.

WHAT IT IS. A written, versioned specification of a dataset: its schema, the meaning of each field, its guarantees (freshness, completeness, uniqueness), and the process by which it may change.

The distinction that decides whether it works A contract without enforcement is documentation, and documentation drifts.

The only enforcement point that works is the producer's CI pipeline — the build fails when a change would break the contract. That moves the cost to the person making the change, at the moment they make it, which is the only arrangement that survives contact with a deadline.
The reason contracts fail, and it is not technical The producing team did not sign up to be a data provider. An application team owns a database because their service needs one; your dependency on it is something that happened to them.

A data contract is a negotiation artifact at least as much as a technical one, and proposing one without an answer to "what is in it for the producer" is how the initiative dies. That negotiation is an L8 problem, which is why L6 and L8 are adjacent.

SUBTOPICS. Contract contents — schema, semantics, guarantees, change process, owner · contracts at which boundaries (source-to-lake, lake-to-mart, mart-to-consumer) and why not everywhere · versioning · the schema registry as the enforcement mechanism for streams (L3 K11) · contract tests in CI on both sides.

R6 Compatibility, precisely defined

Backward, forward, full — and the change type that no schema check will ever catch.

KindMeansExampleUpgrade order
BackwardThe new schema can read old dataAdding an optional fieldConsumers first
ForwardThe old schema can read new dataRemoving an optional fieldProducers first
FullBothAdding an optional field with a defaultEither
BreakingNeitherAdding a required field; changing a type; renaming anythingCoordinated, or not at all
The mnemonic, and why people get this backwards The direction is named after the data, not the code. Backward compatibility means the new code is compatible backwards, with data written before it. The practical payoff is knowing which side to upgrade first, which is the only reason the distinction is worth holding.

And note that a rename is a delete plus an add — the most destructive change in the table, and the one most casually proposed.
The semantic break, which no schema check catches The column stays. The type stays. The meaning changes. amount was in rupees and is now in paise. Every value is a hundred times larger and every schema validation passes.

Only a distribution check catches this — which is why R2 and R6 are complements rather than alternatives, and why a contract must specify semantics and not only structure. Lab 17's second fault is exactly this.

R7 Observability

You are not observing a system. You are observing a dataset, and the tooling differs accordingly.

WHAT IT IS. A time series per table, not a trace per request. Freshness, row counts, run duration and its trend, check pass rates — and one metric nobody has: the age of the oldest unresolved data quality failure.

The minimum viable version, which is genuinely sufficient to start One table: (table_name, run_id, timestamp, rows_in, rows_out, duration, checks_passed, checks_failed). One dashboard over it.

That is it. No vendor, no platform, no evaluation cycle. Build this before evaluating anything, because it will tell you what your actual failure profile is — and the tool you would have bought first is usually not the one that profile calls for.

SUBTOPICS. Metrics per dataset rather than per service · why request tracing does not transfer · trend detection on run duration as an early warning for cost and for skew · check pass rate as a health signal · surfacing freshness to consumers, in the dashboard itself, so that staleness is visible where the decision is made rather than in a channel they do not read.

CONNECTS TO. R8 · L5 run ledger · R12.

R8 Data SLAs and SLOs

Imported from SRE, and usually imported badly.

WHAT IT IS. A numerical definition of "working": "the orders table is no more than ninety minutes stale, ninety-nine percent of the time." Measured continuously, with an error budget that is spent deliberately.

The question that exposes most "data SLAs" Who is the SLA with, and what happens when it is breached?

An SLA implies a consumer who cares and a consequence for failure. Most published data SLAs have neither, which makes them aspirations written in the grammar of commitments. Saying so — and then proposing an SLO with a named owner instead — is a genuinely senior contribution.
The move that saves the most money in this entire branch Negotiate the SLO down. Most consumers ask for real-time and need daily, because "real-time" is free to ask for.

An SLO conversation is where L4 W12 and L5 P12 get their evidence — you are not arguing against streaming, you are asking what decision changes if the number is four hours old, and letting the answer decide the architecture.

R9 Incident response for silent failure

Three ways it differs from a service incident, and one step everybody skips.

DifferenceConsequence
There was no pageYou learn from a human's doubt. The first task is establishing when it started, which requires history you may not have kept
The blast radius is downstream and already consumedNumbers were reported and decisions were made. The response includes communication, not only repair
Rollback is insufficientFixing forward leaves wrong history. You must also decide whether to restate — which is L1 M9 and L5 P6 again
The runbook, and the step people skip Detect → establish the time window → establish the blast radius via lineage → notify → fix → backfill → verify → communicate the restatement.

Notify before you fix. Consumers acting on wrong data need to stop acting now, not in three hours when the repair is finished. Engineers reliably invert these two steps because fixing feels like progress and notifying feels like admitting fault.

R10 Lineage spine

Table-level answers "what might break". Column-level answers "what will".

WHAT IT IS. The dependency graph of your data — which tables feed which, and at the finer granularity, which columns feed which.

HOW IT IS PRODUCED. By parsing SQL (practical, and buildable yourself with a parser like sqlglot) · by instrumenting the engine (OpenLineage) · or by manual declaration, which rots immediately and should not be attempted.

UseWhat it enables
Impact analysisBefore a change: who breaks, and who owns them
Root causeAfter an incident: walk upstream instead of guessing
DeprecationKnowing when the last consumer has moved off
DeletionFinding the tables nobody reads — the most underrated use, and the one that pays L5 P12's bill
PII tracingWhere did this column's data end up (R11)
The honest cost note Column-level lineage is expensive to build and to keep accurate. Table-level covers most incident work. Build table-level first and universally; add column-level only where the blast radius justifies it. Saying that in an interview signals that you have costed the thing rather than admired it.

R11 Governance

Access, PII, retention — and one genuinely hard problem.

WHAT IT IS. Column-level access control, masking and tokenisation, PII classification as a required tag, and retention policies that are enforced rather than documented.

The hard problem: deletion in an immutable architecture An erasure request arrives for one subject. Your lakehouse is built on immutable files and retained snapshots. You rewrite the affected files, expire every snapshot that still references the old ones — and then remember where else that data went.

The same rows are in your Kafka topics, your backups, your dev environment, the extract someone emailed, and the Parquet files in a bucket nobody catalogued. Deletion is a distributed problem dressed as a table operation, which is why it is the question that separates people who have done this from people who have read about it.
The answer worth having ready Crypto-shredding. Encrypt each subject's data with a per-subject key. To erase, delete the key. Deletion becomes O(1) and works everywhere the ciphertext travelled — backups, topics, forgotten buckets — without finding any of them.

It is not free: key management becomes critical infrastructure, and losing a key is an unrecoverable data loss. But it is the design that actually solves the problem rather than chasing copies, and being able to propose it is a strong signal in any regulated context.

SUBTOPICS. Column-level access control and masking · dynamic masking by role · tokenisation versus encryption versus hashing, and why hashing is usually insufficient for low-cardinality PII · classification as a required tag enforced at creation, not audited afterwards · retention as an automated expiry job · soft-deleted rows are still PII (L5 P9) · data residency.

CONNECTS TO. R10 · L0 B5 · L8 access at platform scale.

R12 Cost attribution and query economics

Where L0's measurement becomes an organisational lever.

WHAT IT IS. Knowing which team, job or dashboard caused which portion of the bill — bytes scanned plus compute time, tagged and aggregated.

The behavioural finding Cost visibility changes behaviour more than cost limits do. A team that can see its own bill optimises. A team that hits a quota files a ticket.

Which means showback is usually the higher-leverage intervention, and it is far cheaper to build than real chargeback.

WHAT YOU FIND THE FIRST TIME YOU ATTRIBUTE COST. A handful of queries are most of the bill · a dashboard refreshing every five minutes that three people open weekly · SELECT * issued by a BI tool · and unpartitioned scans of the largest table in the warehouse. All four are fixable in a day once visible, and invisible forever otherwise.

17 Laboratory — Silent Failure

Four faults that leave every pipeline green.

PROBLEM. A working pipeline suite. Every job succeeds. Every dashboard renders. Introduce four faults that produce wrong data without producing any failure, then instrument until each one is caught — and measure how long each took to catch.

INITIAL SYSTEM. The L5 pipelines, green, with no data tests at all. Plus a deterministic fault injector and a saved copy of the correct outputs.
FAILURE TO INTRODUCE — four, all green 1. Partial load. A paginated source silently returns 60% of rows. Counts drop. Nothing errors.
2. Semantic change. amount moves from rupees to paise upstream. Schema identical, types identical, every value a hundred times larger.
3. Join fan-out. A dimension acquires duplicate rows. The join multiplies facts. Revenue rises twelve percent and every job succeeds.
4. Timezone shift. An upstream system moves from IST to UTC. Every daily boundary slides by five and a half hours — wrong for one day, then subtly wrong at the boundary forever.

CONCEPTS THAT EMERGE. Green is not correct → the five quality dimensions and what each actually catches → why schema checks are nearly useless against real failures → distribution checks and their false-positive cost → seasonality → detection latency as the engineered quantity → page versus ticket routing.

#ExperimentWhat it isolates
1All four faults, no checks. Measure how long until you notice by inspectionThe honest baseline, and it is uncomfortable
2Add freshness checks; re-run all fourCatches none of them — freshness is necessary and not sufficient
3Add row-count bounds per partition; re-runFault 1 caught. Record the detection latency
4Add schema checks; re-runNone of the four is caught. The instructive negative result of the lab
5Add distribution checks — mean, null rate, cardinalityFaults 2 and 4 caught
6Add uniqueness on the grainFault 3 caught, immediately and unambiguously
7Add cross-system key-set reconciliationWhat it adds beyond everything above
8Replay a simulated sale day through the tuned distribution checks. Count false positivesThe cost side of R4, measured rather than asserted
9Compute precision per check type; assign page or ticket to eachRouting as an engineering decision, not a default
10Build the full fault × check × detection-latency tableThe artifact

WHAT TO OBSERVE. Which checks catch which faults — the mapping is not intuitive, and experiment 4 is the surprise. Detection latency for each fault under each configuration. False positives under seasonality in experiment 8.

THE ARTIFACT. A fault-to-check matrix with measured detection latencies, plus a page/ticket routing decision per check. The sentence it earns: "Schema checks caught zero of the four most common silent failures. Volume and distribution checks caught all four, at a false-positive cost of N per week."

REAL-WORLD CONNECTION. Every data quality tool evaluation, and every post-incident review that concludes "we should have had monitoring" without specifying which monitoring. This lab specifies it, with numbers.

PORTFOLIO VALUE. Very high. It is a measurement study on a topic normally discussed in adjectives, and the matrix is directly usable in a real job in week one.

20 Laboratory — Contracts & Lineage

A schema change caught before it ships, rather than after it breaks a dashboard.

PROBLEM. A producer team is about to rename a column. Today, you would find out when something downstream breaks — and you cannot say in advance how much.

INITIAL SYSTEM. A realistic dependency graph: one source table, several intermediate models, a dozen leaf reports with different owners. No contracts, no lineage, no CI gate.
FAILURE TO INTRODUCE Rename the column. Watch the cascade. Then count how many consumers broke — and, more importantly, how many you did not know existed until they broke.

That second number is the one that justifies everything built in the rest of this lab, and it is always larger than the estimate you wrote down beforehand. Write the estimate down first.

CONCEPTS THAT EMERGE. Table-level lineage by SQL parsing → column-level lineage and the precision it buys → contracts as schema plus semantics plus guarantees → compatibility classification → CI enforcement at the producer → expand-migrate-contract as the safe deprecation path → the friction cost of a gate, which decides whether it survives.

#ExperimentWhat it isolates
1Rename with no defence. Estimate the blast radius first, then measure itThe gap between what you believed and what was true
2Build table-level lineage by parsing the SQL; re-run the impact analysis"Thirty-seven might break" — useful, and imprecise
3Build column-level lineage for the affected path"Three will break, here are the owners." The whole business case
4Write contracts for three exposed tables: schema, semantics, freshness guarantee, ownerWhat a contract must contain to be actionable
5Add a CI check classifying a proposed change as backward / forward / full / breakingEnforcement at the producer, where the cost belongs
6Test the classifier against six changes: add optional, add required, drop, rename, widen type, narrow typeWhere the rules are genuinely subtle
7Make a semantic-only change (units). Confirm the classifier misses it. Add the distribution check that catches itThe limit of structural contracts
8Implement expand-migrate-contract: add new, dual-write, migrate consumers, remove oldThe only safe rename, and how long it really takes
9Use lineage to find tables with no downstream consumers; propose deletionsL5 P12's bill, arriving with evidence
10Measure the CI gate's friction: added build time, false blocks, developer complaintsA gate developers route around is worse than none

WHAT TO OBSERVE. Predicted versus actual blast radius at both granularities. Whether the CI gate is respected or bypassed. The true elapsed time of experiment 8 — expand-migrate-contract is correct and slow, and knowing how slow is what lets you plan a migration honestly.

THE ARTIFACT. A contract specification, a working CI gate, and a lineage-driven impact report — plus the friction measurement from experiment 10, which is the finding most write-ups of this topic omit.

REAL-WORLD CONNECTION. This is the entire "data contracts" discourse, made concrete and measured. Most of that discourse is advocacy; almost none of it reports what enforcement costs.

PORTFOLIO VALUE. High, and the most L8-adjacent artifact in L6 — it is a platform capability built at small scale, which is exactly the transition this Forge is aimed at.
