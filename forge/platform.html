Data platform engineering

The shift from building pipelines to building the thing other people build pipelines on. The mental change is from solving a problem to solving a class of problems for people you will never meet — which is a product discipline at least as much as an engineering one, and that is precisely why it changes your title.

The single idea. Every capability you offer becomes a promise you cannot withdraw. A platform's cost is not what it takes to build. It is what it takes to never break — for every team that adopted it, in every way you did not anticipate, for as long as the company exists. Which means the discipline is as much about what you decline to offer as about what you ship.

00 The one question this branch exists to answer

Every sub-branch below is an answer to some part of it.

? “How does a company let three hundred people work with data without three hundred bespoke pipelines and three hundred conflicting definitions of ‘active user’?” Catalog, contracts, self-service, golden paths and chargeback are not five topics. They are five parts of one answer.

The second question is the one that tests whether the mental shift has actually happened:

? “A team ignored your platform and built their own pipeline. What do you do?” The junior answer is enforce the standard. The senior answer is that this is a bug report about your platform. They chose the harder path, which means yours was harder still, or they did not know it existed, or it did not do what they needed. Find out which, then fix that.
The failure mode of this branch Building capability instead of adoption. A platform with ten features and two users has failed. One with two features and forty users has not. And a mandate converts an adoption problem into a compliance problem — which produces shadow systems you cannot see, and the pipeline you cannot see is strictly worse than the one you disapprove of.

Reading this map Two spines, and they are of different kinds. E1 is a disposition — it changes how every other node is judged. E6 is infrastructure — it is what every other capability queries.

Access control needs to know what exists and who owns it. Cost attribution needs to map queries to datasets. Deprecation needs to know who reads what. Discovery needs all three. Every other platform capability is, structurally, a query against the catalog — which is why building it late is the most common way platform efforts stall.

E1 Platform as product spine

Your users are engineers, and they have an alternative: doing it themselves.

WHAT CHANGES. You stop having requirements and start having users. You stop shipping features and start measuring adoption. You stop being able to fix things by knowing something, because the people using it will not know it.

THE PRODUCT QUESTIONS. Who are the users, and how do they differ? What job are they trying to do? What is their current alternative, and how bad is it really? Why would they switch, and what does switching cost them?

The metric Adoption, not capability. And beneath it, one number: time-to-first-success — how long from "I want to ingest this source" to "the data is queryable and monitored".

It is measurable, it is comparable over time, and it is the number that correlates with whether people use the thing. Publish it. A platform team that reports time-to-first-success is having a different conversation from one that reports features shipped.
On mandates A mandate converts an adoption problem into a compliance problem. Compliance produces the minimum acceptable use of your platform plus a shadow system for the real work. You have not removed the duplication; you have made it invisible.

Mandates are occasionally correct — for security, for PII, for anything with a legal edge. Everywhere else, if you need a mandate, the product is not good enough yet, and the mandate will conceal that fact from you for about two years.

CONNECTS TO. E2 · E11.

E2 Golden paths and adoption

Not the only way. The easy way.

WHAT IT IS. One supported route through a common task, which is fast, documented, and actually works. Deviation remains possible and is simply not supported.

The rule that decides adoption before you write any code Make the right thing the easy thing. If the compliant path takes two days and the non-compliant path takes two hours, you have chosen non-compliance on your users' behalf — and then you will spend a year attributing it to culture.

ADOPTION TACTICS THAT WORK. Start with one team as a design partner rather than a survey of ten · instrument usage so you know what is actually used · measure and publish time-to-first-success · migrate the first team yourself, at your cost, so there is a reference · and write the documentation by watching someone follow it, not by describing what you built.

The design error that guarantees low adoption Building for the hypothetical hardest user. There is always one team with an unusual requirement, and designing the platform around them produces something too complex for the eighty percent whose needs are ordinary. Serve the common case beautifully and let the hard case escape to a supported off-ramp. An escape hatch is a feature, not a failure.

CONNECTS TO. E3 · E7 · Lab 18.

E3 Self-service ingestion

The most requested capability, and the one most likely to manufacture a swamp.

WHAT IT IS. A declarative source definition — usually a short YAML file — that produces a working pipeline: connector, landing in the standard format and location, catalog registration, a quality baseline, and cost tags.

THE SCOPE DECISION. Which source types get self-service, and which require an engineer. Postgres, S3 prefixes, REST endpoints and Kafka topics are templatable. The proprietary system with a bespoke auth flow is not. Publish the supported list, and make the boundary explicit rather than discovered.

The failure that self-service creates faster than humans ever could Self-service ingestion without self-service governance manufactures a swamp at machine speed. A hundred new tables, none owned, none catalogued, none tested, none retained deliberately.

The template must emit the contract, the checks, the owner and the retention policy — not just the pipeline. If governance is a separate step, it will not happen, because it is the step that benefits someone other than the person doing the work.

CONNECTS TO. L5 P7 · L6 R5 · E6.

E4 The compute platform

Multi-tenancy, isolation and quotas — with one non-obvious organising principle.

THE PROBLEMS. Noisy neighbours · one team's backfill starving another team's production run (L5 P6 at organisational scale) · unbounded ad-hoc queries · a runaway job with no timeout.

The principle Isolate by workload class, not by team. A team's production job has far more in common with another team's production job than with its own ad-hoc exploration.

Production, scheduled-non-critical, ad hoc, and backfill are four classes with four different latency tolerances, four different failure consequences and four different appropriate resource guarantees. Slicing by team gives every team a small copy of the same contention problem.

MECHANISMS. Separate pools, queues or warehouses per class · admission control and concurrency limits · timeouts as a default rather than an exception · priority and preemption · and the intervention that works best, which is not a control at all — showing people their own usage.

CONNECTS TO. E9 · L2 · L7 D7 — multi-tenancy for internal teams and for customers is the same problem with different consequences for a leak.

E5 Storage and table format standardisation

Boring on purpose, and the boringness is the value.

WHAT IT IS. One table format, one file format, one layout convention, one naming convention, one retention default. Deviation is possible and requires a written reason.

The argument for standardising, stated honestly The cost of a second format is not the second format. It is that every future capability must support both — the catalog, the access control layer, the quality framework, the cost attribution, the migration tooling, and everything you have not built yet.

Standardisation buys compounding leverage and costs optionality. Say both halves; a platform engineer who only says the first half sounds like someone who has never been the team blocked by a standard.

SUBTOPICS. Zone conventions — raw, staged, curated — and what each guarantees · naming that encodes ownership and grain · retention defaults that expire rather than accumulate · the exception process, which must exist and must be cheap · a convention everybody follows beats a better convention half of them follow, which is the whole reason to pick quickly and move on.

CONNECTS TO. L0 B5 · L1 M11 · E6.

E6 The catalog spine

Not a search tool. The substrate every other capability queries.

WHAT IT HOLDS. Tables and schemas · owners · lineage (L6 R10) · classifications and PII tags (L6 R11) · quality status and freshness · cost · and usage — who queried what, and when.

CapabilityThe catalog query it depends on
Access control (E8)What exists, who owns it, what is classified as sensitive
Cost attribution (E9)Which dataset a query touched, and which team owns it
Deprecation (E10)Who still reads this, and when did they last
DiscoveryAll of the above, plus semantics
Incident response (L6 R9)Blast radius, and the owners to notify
The one way catalogs reliably fail Populated by hand. It rots in weeks, and a catalog that is thirty percent wrong is worse than none, because people stop trusting the seventy percent.

It must be populated by instrumentation: pipelines register themselves, lineage comes from parsing, usage comes from query logs, freshness comes from the run ledger. If a human has to remember to update an entry, that entry is already wrong.

And the adoption measure is not completeness — it is search-to-answer. A complete catalog nobody searches is a database with good intentions.

CONNECTS TO. E8 · E10 · L6 Lab 20.

E7 Developer experience

Including one practice that makes data pull requests reviewable at all.

WHAT IT IS. Local development against realistic sample data · a scaffolding command that produces a correct pipeline in one step · CI that runs exactly the checks production runs · a preview environment · and fast feedback.

The metric Time from clone to a passing local run. If it exceeds an hour, people will develop against production — and then you have two problems. This number is worth defending aggressively, because everything else in developer experience is downstream of it.
The practice that makes a data pull request reviewable Run the modified model on a fixed sample and diff its output against the current model's output. Post the diff on the pull request.

Code review of a SQL change is nearly worthless — the reviewer cannot see what the change does to the numbers. A row-count delta plus a sample of changed rows converts "looks fine to me" into an actual review, and it is the data equivalent of the diff that makes software review work at all. Remarkably few teams do this.

SUBTOPICS. Sample data that preserves shape — cardinality, skew and nulls — rather than the first thousand rows · unit tests on transformation logic · contract checks in CI · a compile or dry-run stage · secrets and environment parity · and the scaffolding command, which is the golden path made executable.

CONNECTS TO. L5 P11 · L6 R5 · E2.

E8 Access control at platform scale

The shift from granting access to defining policy.

WHAT CHANGES AT SCALE. Granting per person per table works for thirty tables and collapses at three thousand. The unit of administration has to become the policy, not the grant.

The principle Attach policy to classification, not to table names. A newly created column tagged as PII is protected because it was tagged — not because someone remembered to add a grant.

Table-name-based access control rots the instant someone creates a table, which is continuously. This is also the concrete reason E6 must come first: policy needs something to attach to.

SUBTOPICS. Role design that maps to job functions rather than to org charts · request and approval workflow with a default expiry · time-bounded access for incidents · periodic access review · masking and tokenisation applied by policy (L6 R11) · and the two audit questions, which are different: who can see this, and who did.

CONNECTS TO. E6 · L6 R11 · L7 D7.

E9 Cost management and chargeback

Including the number that keeps your team funded.

THE SEQUENCE. Attribute → show back → budget with alerts → charge back, if ever. Most organisations should stop after the second step, because visibility changes behaviour more than limits do (L6 R12).

The number platform teams forget, and then lose their budget The platform's own cost must itself be attributable — and set against what it saved.

If you cannot say "the platform absorbed X in shared cost and removed Y duplicated pipelines and Z engineer-weeks", you will lose the budget conversation to a product team that can quantify its work. Platform teams are the easiest thing to cut precisely because their value is diffuse, and the defence is arithmetic rather than advocacy.

UNIT ECONOMICS TO PUBLISH. Cost per pipeline · cost per TB served · cost per query, by class · and the trend on each, which matters more than the level.

CONNECTS TO. E4 · L6 R12 · L0 Lab 01 — cost is bytes scanned, all the way down.

E10 Migration and deprecation

The hardest thing in L8, and what actually consumes platform teams.

WHAT IT IS. Removing or replacing something hundreds of consumers depend on, without breaking any of them, and without the migration taking three years.

THE PATTERN. Expand → migrate → contract (L6 Lab 20), at organisational scale: add the new thing, dual-write, move consumers, then remove the old. Plus the strangler variant for whole systems — new writes to the new system, background migration of history, and a facade routing reads.

The two things that make deprecation possible at all Lineage tells you when the last consumer left — it is the only reliable signal, and it is why E6 gates this node entirely. You cannot deprecate what you cannot see.

And budget the migration at the moment you build the thing. Every platform capability should ship with an answer to "how would we remove this" — because L5 P12 is true at platform scale too: requests to build are constant and requests to decommission are approximately zero.
The last five percent Ninety-five percent of consumers migrate when asked. The last five percent never migrate voluntarily — the owner left, the team was reorganised, or the dashboard is loadbearing for someone senior and nobody knows it.

Deprecation therefore needs a dated policy, an announced end date, and a deliberate breakage plan — usually a short scheduled outage of the old path, well in advance of removal, which surfaces the invisible consumers faster than any survey.

CONNECTS TO. E6 · L6 R6 · L5 P12.

E11 When not to build a platform

The counterpart node, and at this level it is a career-relevant judgement.

Do not build one when There are fewer than about three teams. Two teams do not need a platform; they need to talk to each other.
The variation is genuine. If every team's needs really do differ, a platform is a straitjacket and what they needed was a consultant.
You could buy it. A platform team's job is not to build everything — sometimes the golden path is a vendor's product with your conventions wrapped around it.
The organisation will not fund the maintenance. A platform built and then unstaffed is worse than no platform, because teams depend on it while it decays.
The general test A platform is a bet that the cost of building the abstraction is less than the cost of the duplication it removes. Count the duplication first. Three teams solving the same problem three ways is evidence. One team predicting that others will eventually need this is not.

CONNECTS TO. L5 P12 · L4 W12 · L3 K12 · L2 S13 — five "when not to" nodes across the Forge, and together they are a single professional disposition.

18 Laboratory — Mini Data Platform

Another team must ship a pipeline without talking to you. capstone

PROBLEM. Build the smallest complete platform — small in scale, complete in shape — and then use it as somebody else.

INITIAL SYSTEM. Everything from L0 through L6 exists, as your parts: undocumented, uncatalogued, and dependent on knowledge that lives only in your head.
THE CONSTRAINT THAT MAKES THIS A LABORATORY After building it, you must use it as a stranger: fresh environment, documentation only, and no right to patch anything you find broken.

Every point where you have to guess is a documentation bug. Every point where you reach for knowledge only you have is an adoption barrier. Write each one down instead of fixing it. The list is the real output of the lab.

THE DELIVERABLE. A scaffolding command that generates, from one short config file: a working pipeline, a contract, quality checks, catalog registration and cost tags · one documented golden path for one source type · an automatically populated catalog · CI enforcing contracts and checks · a per-team cost report · and a written deprecation policy.

#ExperimentWhat it isolates
1Build the scaffold; generate a pipeline. Count the lines of config a user must writeThe size of the ask you are making of your users
2Time-to-first-success as a stranger. Fresh environment, docs only, no fixesThe headline metric. Record every guess
3Give the docs to an actual other person; watch without helping. Count interventionsThe gap between your documentation and your assumptions
4Add a second source type. Measure how much generalised versus how much you rewroteIf most of it was rewritten, you built a script, not a platform
5Violate the contract in a generated pipeline; confirm CI blocks itEnforcement at the point of change
6Skip catalog registration deliberately; see whether anything noticesIf nothing does, that is the finding
7Run two workloads concurrently, one a backfill. Measure the production SLA. Then add pool isolationE4, measured (L5 Lab 19 at platform scale)
8Produce the per-team cost report; reconcile it against the actual billAttribution that does not reconcile is worse than none
9Deprecate one dataset end to end: announce, dual-run, confirm via lineage that the last consumer left, removeE10, the whole loop, once
10Measure your own time split: supporting users versus buildingThe ratio that predicts whether a platform is sustainable

WHAT TO OBSERVE. Time-to-first-success, before and after fixing the friction list. The intervention count in experiment 3. The generalisation ratio in experiment 4. And the support-to-build ratio in experiment 10, which is the number that tells you whether the golden path is genuinely self-service or merely documented.

THE ARTIFACT. A working mini-platform, plus the friction list produced by using your own product as a stranger. The sentence it earns: "A new source of a supported type goes from request to queryable data in forty minutes, by someone who has never spoken to me, and it arrives catalogued, contracted, quality-checked and cost-tagged."

REAL-WORLD CONNECTION. This is the job. Not an approximation of it — the same activity at a smaller scale.

PORTFOLIO VALUE. The capstone of the entire Forge. It demonstrates the transition the whole structure was aimed at, and it is the one artifact that reads as platform engineer rather than as data engineer with good projects.

:: Interview defence

Answers here are judged on judgement and on organisational realism, not on architecture.

The questionNodeWhat a strong answer contains
What is a data platform for?E1Removing duplication and divergence at a scale where coordination stops working
A team bypassed your platform. What do you do?E1Treat it as a bug report. Find out which of the three causes it was
How do you measure a platform's success?E2Adoption, and time-to-first-success. Not features shipped
Would you mandate its use?E1Only for security and legal edges. Elsewhere a mandate hides the product's weakness
Where would you start with nothing?E6The catalog, populated automatically — everything else queries it
How do you stop self-service becoming a swamp?E3The template emits governance, not just a pipeline
How do you isolate tenants on shared compute?E4By workload class, not by team — and why that is the right cut
How do you deprecate something 200 teams use?E10Expand-migrate-contract, lineage for the exit signal, and a plan for the last five percent
Chargeback or showback?E9Showback first. Visibility beats limits, and it is far cheaper to build
How do you justify the platform team's cost?E9Duplication removed and engineer-weeks saved, quantified. Arithmetic, not advocacy
How do you make data PRs reviewable?E7Run on a sample and post the output diff. Few teams do this
When would you not build a platform?E11Fewer than three teams, genuine variation, or unfunded maintenance
The move that distinguishes a senior answer here Talk about people as a design constraint, without cynicism. "They will route around this if it takes longer than the alternative" is an engineering statement, not a complaint about culture. Candidates who describe platforms purely in terms of components are describing infrastructure; candidates who describe incentives, adoption and the cost of switching are describing a platform — and only the second group has done the job.
