Branch L4 · new · requires L3 · the differentiator

Stream processing semantics

This is the branch that most reliably separates candidates. Almost everyone can describe a Kafka consumer. Far fewer can say what a watermark is actually guessing about, what "exactly-once" demands from a sink, or why an unbounded stream-stream join is a memory leak with a schedule.

The single idea. A stream has no end, so "all the data" never arrives. Every concept in this branch is a way of deciding when to stop waiting. Windows are the unit you stop waiting for. Watermarks are the guess about when it is safe to stop. Allowed lateness is how much you un-stop. State is what you must remember while waiting. TTL is when you stop remembering. Join time bounds are how long you wait for the other side. Backpressure is what happens when you cannot keep up with the waiting.

00 The one question this branch exists to answer

It has no single correct answer, which is exactly why it works as a question.

? “You compute hourly revenue from a stream. At 14:00 you emit the 13:00–14:00 total. At 14:05 an event with timestamp 13:47 arrives. What do you do?” Drop it and be quietly wrong. Wait longer and be slowly right. Restate and force every downstream consumer to accept that a published number can change. Or side-output it and hand the problem to a human. The answer reveals whether you understand that completeness in a stream is unknowable, not merely difficult.

There is a second question, and it is the operational counterpart — the one that exposes people who have read about streaming but never run it:

? “Your job ran at a steady 10,000 events per second for six hours and then died with OOM. Throughput never changed. What died?” State, not throughput. A streaming job's memory is not a function of its rate — it is a function of key cardinality × retention. And the follow-up that most people miss: doubling the memory makes it die after twelve hours instead of six. You bought hours, not a fix.
The failure mode of this branch Learning the vocabulary without ever producing a wrong number. Watermarks, allowed lateness and exactly-once are trivially memorisable and almost impossible to reason about until you have watched a count come out too low. Every lab here begins by producing a demonstrably incorrect result and then earning each mechanism as the fix for it.
Why this branch is worth disproportionate effort L2 and L3 are branches where you are competing against many people who also know Spark and Kafka. This is the branch where the field thins. The concepts are engine-independent, they transfer to any tool the company happens to use, and they are the ones that appear in system design rounds rather than trivia rounds.

Three habits to establish before the first lab Never let a test depend on wall-clock time. Every result must be reproducible by replaying the same input, which means event-time everywhere and a generator you can rerun. Log the current watermark alongside every emitted result — "window fired at watermark T" is the line that explains almost every surprising output. Record state size and checkpoint duration on every run, even when the run is about something else. State growth is invisible until it is fatal, and the whole skill is seeing it early.
The engine choice, stated honestly Flink teaches the semantics; Structured Streaming gets you hired. Do the labs in Flink because the mechanisms are visible — you can watch a watermark advance per subtask and see state on disk. Then port the first lab to Structured Streaming. The list of things that became harder or impossible in the port is more valuable than either implementation, and it is a far better answer to "which would you choose" than any blog comparison.

W1 The three times

Event time, processing time, ingestion time — and why the distinction is the entire subject.

WHAT IT IS. Event time is when the thing happened, carried in the record. Processing time is when your operator saw it. Ingestion time is when it entered the pipeline. The gap between event and processing time is skew, and it is never zero, never constant, and has no upper bound.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because a batch job has all the data by definition, so the distinction is invisible there. A streaming job never has all the data, so every result is a statement about a subset — and which subset depends entirely on which clock you used to define it.

DEPENDENCIES. L3 — you need a real stream with real arrival disorder.

SUBTOPICS. The three times and where each is assigned · skew and its distribution · why processing-time results are non-deterministic across reruns and event-time results are not · the replay test as the decisive argument · timestamp assignment at the source versus at the broker · clock skew on the producing devices · out-of-order arrival as the normal case, not the exception · what "real-time" means when someone says it in a requirements meeting.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free articles · Title: Tyler Akidau, "Streaming 101" and "Streaming 102" — O'Reilly Radar
Why this one: free, diagram-driven, and the clearest writing that exists on this material. These two articles are also chapters 1 and 2 of the book Streaming Systems — read them first and decide afterwards whether the rest of the book is worth buying.

DEEPER — "The Dataflow Model" (VLDB 2015), free from Google Research. It is the paper that unified batch and streaming into one model, and its four questions — what results, where in event time, when in processing time, how do refinements relate — are a complete framework for interrogating any streaming requirement.
OBSERVATION · The replay test Compute the same hourly count twice from the same input: once with processing-time windows, once with event-time windows. They differ, which is unsurprising.

Now replay the identical input a second time. The event-time results are byte-identical to the first run. The processing-time results are different again — because they were never a property of the data, only of how fast your machine happened to be reading that afternoon.

That is the entire argument for event time, and it takes twenty minutes to produce.

CONNECTS TO. W2 · W3 · Distributed Systems N3 (there is no global clock — this is that fact arriving as a data problem).

W2 Windows

The unit you agree to stop waiting for.

WHAT IT IS. A rule that assigns each event to one or more finite buckets so that an unbounded stream can be aggregated at all. Tumbling (fixed, non-overlapping), sliding (fixed, overlapping), session (gap-defined and dynamic), global (with a custom trigger).

WHY IT EXISTS. Because COUNT(*) over an infinite stream never returns. A window is the smallest amount of finiteness you can impose while still answering the question.

DEPENDENCIES. W1.

SUBTOPICS. Window assignment at arrival versus firing at trigger — two separate events people conflate · tumbling and its alignment to epoch versus to a business day · sliding windows and the fan-out multiplier · session windows and session merging when a late event bridges two sessions · global windows with custom triggers · early and late firings · triggers as a separate axis from windows · window state lifetime.

The one people get wrong A sliding window of size 1 hour with a 1-minute slide means every single event belongs to 60 windows simultaneously. State is 60×. Output volume is 60×. Checkpoint size is 60×. A sliding window is a memory decision disguised as an analytics choice, and nobody mentions this when they ask for "a rolling one-hour metric updated every minute".

The senior response is to ask whether an incrementally-maintained aggregate over a tumbling window would satisfy the requirement — it usually does, at 1/60th the cost.
PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free official documentation · Title: the Apache Flink documentation, "Operators → Windows"
Why this one: free, and it separates the four orthogonal pieces — assigner, trigger, evictor, allowed lateness — that every other treatment blurs together. Once you see that windows and triggers are independent axes, early-firing and restatement stop being special cases.

DEEPER — "Streaming 102" again, specifically the animated window diagrams. They show session merging better than prose can.
OBSERVATION · Watch two sessions become one Run a session window with a 30-minute gap. Produce activity at 10:00 and again at 11:00 — two distinct sessions. Now inject a single late event with timestamp 10:40.

The two sessions merge into one, and the previously-emitted results for both are superseded. A single record has retroactively changed the shape of the output — which is why session windows cannot be computed with a naive incremental aggregate, and why they are the most expensive window type to maintain.

CONNECTS TO. W3 · W7 (window state is state) · Lab 13.

W3 Watermarks spine

A guess, made from data, about a fact you cannot know.

WHAT IT IS. An assertion flowing through the pipeline that says: "I believe no further event with timestamp ≤ T will arrive." It is generated by a strategy — most commonly bounded out-of-orderness, meaning max timestamp seen − a fixed delay — and it advances monotonically.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because a window must fire at some point, and there is no signal in the world that says "the data is complete now". The watermark is the pipeline's substitute for that missing signal, and it is a heuristic, not a fact.

DEPENDENCIES. W1 · W2.

SUBTOPICS. Watermark generation strategies · bounded out-of-orderness and how to pick the bound · the watermark advances as the minimum across all inputs · per-partition watermarks and why they matter · idle sources stalling the global watermark and idleness detection · watermark propagation through operators · perfect versus heuristic watermarks · the latency versus completeness trade-off expressed as a measured curve rather than an opinion.

The definition to be able to say under pressure "A watermark is a claim about completeness, derived from the data, that may be wrong in both directions." Too aggressive and you drop real events. Too conservative and every result is late for no benefit. There is no correct watermark delay — there is only a curve, and an operating point you chose on it deliberately. Lab 13 exists to make you the person who has actually plotted that curve.
The incident everyone eventually has The watermark is computed from data, not from the wall clock. If one input partition goes quiet — a region with no overnight traffic, a producer that crashed, a topic partition with no assigned producer — the global watermark is the minimum across inputs, so it stops advancing entirely.

No windows fire. No output appears. No error is logged. Nothing is late; nothing is wrong; nothing is emitted. The job looks healthy in every metric except the one nobody is watching. Idleness detection is the fix, and knowing this failure exists is a strong senior signal because it is learned almost exclusively by having suffered it.
PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free official documentation · Title: Flink docs, "Concepts → Timely Stream Processing" plus "Generating Watermarks"
Why this one: free, and it is the only mainstream documentation that treats watermark generation as a pluggable strategy you are expected to reason about, rather than a single knob. The idleness section is short and worth more than its length.

DEEPER — "Streaming 102", the watermark section, for the intuition; and the Dataflow paper's treatment of heuristic versus perfect watermarks for the formal version.

W4 Late data

Three responses, and each one writes a contract with everybody downstream.

WHAT IT IS. An event whose timestamp is below the current watermark — it arrived after you decided to stop waiting for it. The options are to drop, to restate, or to side-output.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because the watermark is a guess, so being wrong is not an edge case — it is the expected condition. The question is never whether late data occurs, only what you have agreed to do about it.

DEPENDENCIES. W3.

ResponseWhat it costsWhat it demands downstream
DropSilent inaccuracy, proportional to your watermark delayNothing — which is why it is the default, and why it is dangerous. Acceptable only if you measure and publish the drop rate
RestateWindow state kept alive for the whole allowed-lateness period, plus repeated outputAn upsert-capable sink, and consumers who accept that a published number can change. This is the one people do not think through
Side-outputA second stream and a reconciliation processA human or a batch job that closes the loop. Very common in finance, where an unexplained restatement is worse than a known exception queue

SUBTOPICS. Allowed lateness and its state cost · output modes — append, update, complete — and what each permits · side outputs and reconciliation tables · the lambda architecture as an institutionalised answer to this problem · the kappa response — restate, and make the sink cope · measuring and publishing your own drop rate · idempotent restatement.

The line that reframes the whole conversation "Drop or restate is not an engineering decision. It is a product decision, and I would escalate it rather than make it silently."

This is one of the highest-value sentences in the entire Forge. It signals that you understand data systems as contracts with people rather than as pipelines, which is exactly the distinction between a senior engineer and a strong mid-level one. It is also simply true — the cost of a wrong number differs by six orders of magnitude between a marketing dashboard and a settlement file, and you are not the person who knows which this is.
PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free official documentation · Title: Flink, "Allowed Lateness" and "Side Outputs"; and the Spark Structured Streaming Programming Guide, the "Handling Late Data and Watermarking" section
Why both: reading them side by side is the fastest way to see that Spark gives you drop-or-restate and Flink gives you drop-or-restate-or-divert — a concrete capability difference rather than a philosophical one, and the first entry in your W11 comparison.

CONNECTS TO. Lab 13 · L6 data contracts · L1 (a restatement is a slowly-changing fact).

W5 Keyed state

The key decides the partition decides the parallel unit decides the skew. For the third time.

WHAT IT IS. Memory that survives between events, scoped to a key and an operator. Value state, list state, map state, aggregating state. The keyspace is partitioned across parallel subtasks, so each subtask owns a disjoint slice of the state.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because almost nothing interesting is computable from a single event. Counting, joining, deduplicating, sessionising and detecting patterns all require remembering something, and in an unbounded stream "remembering" is the expensive word.

DEPENDENCIES. W1.

SUBTOPICS. Keyed versus operator state · state primitives and when each fits · state backends — heap versus RocksDB, and the serialisation cost per access · key groups and how state is redistributed when parallelism changes · state schema evolution · queryable state · timers as state.

The isomorphism to name out loud A hot key in a stateful streaming job is the same phenomenon as a skewed shuffle partition in Spark and a hot partition in Kafka. Same power-law cause, same symptom — one parallel unit doing most of the work — and largely the same fixes: change the key, salt it, or handle the heavy hitters separately.

This is the third appearance of one idea in three different systems. Being able to say "this is the same problem I showed you in Spark, arriving through a different door" is worth more in an interview than any individual fact in this branch.
PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free official documentation · Title: Flink docs, "Concepts → Stateful Stream Processing" and "Working with State"
Why this one: free, and it makes state an explicit first-class object with a lifecycle, which is precisely the mental model that transfers to every other engine — including the ones where state is hidden from you.

DEEPER — the free Ververica / Apache Flink training exercises on GitHub. They are hands-on, small, and the stateful-enrichment exercise is a good half-hour before Lab 14.

CONNECTS TO. W6 · W7 · L2 S10 skew · L3 K9.

W6 Checkpointing and recovery spine

How a job that has been running for a week survives a machine dying.

WHAT IT IS. Asynchronous barrier snapshotting. Barriers are injected at the sources and flow with the records; each operator snapshots its state when it has received barriers from all of its inputs; the collection of snapshots plus the source offsets forms a globally consistent cut.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because stopping the world to take a consistent snapshot of a distributed dataflow would make the system unusable. Barrier alignment achieves consistency without ever pausing the whole job — it is Chandy-Lamport applied to dataflow.

DEPENDENCIES. W5 · Distributed Systems N5.

SUBTOPICS. Barriers and alignment · why alignment stalls under backpressure and unaligned checkpoints as the response · checkpoint interval versus recovery time · incremental checkpoints with RocksDB · savepoints versus checkpoints — one is for the operator, one is for the system · restoring into a modified job graph · operator UIDs, without which you cannot restore after a code change · checkpoint duration exceeding the interval as a death spiral.

The operational scar worth acquiring deliberately If you do not assign explicit operator UIDs, the state is keyed to auto-generated identifiers derived from the job graph. Change one line of topology and the restore fails — or worse, silently restores nothing into a renamed operator. You now have a job with a week of accumulated state that cannot be upgraded without losing it.

Experiment 9 of Lab 15 produces this on purpose, because it takes ten minutes to reproduce and is otherwise learned during an outage.
PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free paper · Title: Carbone et al., "Lightweight Asynchronous Snapshots for Distributed Dataflows" (arXiv, free)
Why this one: short, readable, and it states the alignment mechanism precisely enough that the backpressure interaction in W10 becomes obvious rather than mysterious.

DEEPER — Chandy and Lamport's original distributed snapshots paper (free). Worth thirty minutes once, because the same algorithm turns up in distributed debuggers and garbage collectors and you will recognise it thereafter.
OBSERVATION · Watch a checkpoint get slower Plot checkpoint duration and size against state size across a run that grows state steadily. Then find the point where checkpoint duration approaches the checkpoint interval.

Past that point the job spends most of its time checkpointing, which slows processing, which grows the backlog, which grows state, which lengthens the next checkpoint. It is a reinforcing loop with the same shape as the rebalance storm in L3 K6 and the retry storm in Distributed Systems N7.

CONNECTS TO. W9 · W10 · Lab 15.

W7 State growth and TTL

The dominant operational problem in streaming, and it is not a performance problem.

WHAT IT IS. The tendency of keyed state to grow without bound, because keys keep being new and nothing ever removes the old ones. It is the leading cause of streaming job death.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because retention is implicit unless you make it explicit. Every stateful operator is an accumulator by default; bounding it is an act you must perform.

DEPENDENCIES. W5 · W6.

The sentence that answers the six-hour-OOM question A streaming job's memory is not a function of throughput. It is a function of key cardinality × per-key size × retention.

Which is why doubling the memory buys hours, not correctness. Growth is linear in time and memory is a constant; the intersection simply moves. Saying this out loud, unprompted, is one of the clearest senior signals available in a streaming interview.

SUBTOPICS. Sources of unbounded growth — unbounded key cardinality, windows held open by allowed lateness, joins without time bounds, deduplication sets, timers that never fire · state TTL and its cleanup strategies, including the lag between logical expiry and physical removal · window state cleanup · approximate structures as a deliberate accuracy trade — Bloom filters for membership, HyperLogLog for cardinality, t-digest for quantiles · computing a state budget before writing the job · state size as an SLO.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free official documentation + blog · Title: Flink docs, "State Time-To-Live", plus the Flink blog posts on RocksDB state backend tuning
Why this one: free, and the TTL documentation is unusually honest about the fact that expiry is lazy — state is removed when it is next accessed or compacted, not when it expires, which is a distinction that shows up as a memory graph that does not fall when you expect it to.

DEEPER — for the approximate structures, the original HyperLogLog paper and any solid Bloom filter derivation. You need to be able to compute the false-positive rate from the parameters, because Lab 14 asks you to decide whether that rate is acceptable.

W8 Streaming joins

Where an unbounded join reveals itself as a memory leak with a schedule.

WHAT IT IS. Three distinct operations that share a name. Stream-stream joins two unbounded inputs within a time bound. Stream-table enriches a stream from a reference dataset. Temporal joins to the version of the reference data as of the event's own event time.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because to join A with B you must buffer A until B could still plausibly arrive — and "could still arrive" is unbounded unless you bound it. The time bound is not an optimisation; it is what makes the operation possible at all.

DEPENDENCIES. W3 · W5 · W7.

The cost is computable before you write the job State ≈ event rate × join interval × record size, for each side. 10,000 events/sec × a 1-hour interval × 500 bytes is about 18 GB per side. You can therefore reject an impossible requirement in the design meeting rather than discovering it in production — and doing that arithmetic aloud in a system design round is a disproportionately strong move.

SUBTOPICS. Interval joins and choosing the bound from the actual lateness distribution · outer joins in streaming and when the null is emitted · enrichment done wrong in two standard ways — per-event lookups to an external database, and a dimension loaded once at startup that is stale forever · broadcast state for small, slowly-changing reference data · temporal joins and versioned tables · join skew.

Enrichment: the two wrong answers (a) Look it up in Postgres per event. That is a network round trip per record. At 10,000 events/sec with a 2 ms round trip you need 20 concurrent lookups just to keep up, and the database is now your throughput ceiling and your availability dependency. Same structure as Networking N6 — round trips dominate, not bandwidth.

(b) Load the dimension once at startup. Now it is stale for the entire lifetime of a job that is designed to run for months. The correct answer is usually a broadcast changelog stream from a compacted Kafka topic — which is exactly what L3 K2 built.
The temporal join, and why finance insists on it A payment in EUR arrives with event time 13:47 yesterday. Which exchange rate applies — the one in force at 13:47, or today's? A normal stream-table join silently gives you today's.

A temporal join is a Type 2 slowly-changing-dimension lookup expressed in streaming. It is the same idea as L1's temporal modelling, and recognising them as one concept in two branches is precisely the kind of connection that makes an answer sound like experience rather than revision.
PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free official documentation · Title: Flink SQL docs, "Joins" — regular, interval, temporal and lookup joins, each with its state implication stated
Why this one: free, and it is the rare reference that tells you which joins retain state forever. The phrase "the required state may grow indefinitely" appears in that documentation and should be read as a warning rather than a footnote.

CONNECTS TO. W7 · L1 temporal modelling · L2 S9 (a broadcast join and broadcast state are the same instinct).

W9 End-to-end exactly-once convergence

A claim that is meaningless without a stated scope.

WHAT IT IS. The property that each input record affects the final output exactly once, despite arbitrary failures. It requires three things simultaneously: a replayable source, checkpointed state, and a sink that is either idempotent or participates in a two-phase commit.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because checkpointing alone gives you exactly-once state and says nothing about output. A job can recover its counters perfectly and still have written the same row to your warehouse twice.

DEPENDENCIES. W6 · L3 K8 · L3 Lab 12 is the direct prerequisite, and L0 B8 is where the underlying impossibility is stated.

RequirementMechanismWhat breaks without it
Replayable sourceKafka offsets stored in the checkpoint, not committed independentlyRecovery cannot reconstruct the input, so nothing else matters
Recoverable stateCheckpointing (W6)Counters are wrong after every restart
Cooperative sinkIdempotent writes, or two-phase commitDuplicated output despite everything upstream being correct

SUBTOPICS. Idempotent sinks — upsert by a deterministic key, and why writing an absolute value is naturally idempotent while writing a delta is not · two-phase-commit sinks — pre-commit at checkpoint, commit on completion notification · the failure window between checkpoint-complete and commit · transaction timeout must exceed the checkpoint interval, or you lose data · transactions surviving a job restart · effectively-once as the honest name.

The formulation to use "We are exactly-once from Kafka to Kafka via transactions, and effectively-once into the warehouse because the sink is idempotent on (entity_id, version)."

Stating the scope is the differentiator. "Our pipeline is exactly-once" is, in almost every real system, true inside the engine and false at the edges — and being the person who names the edge is a stronger signal than any amount of confidence about the middle.
PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free blog + docs · Title: the Apache Flink blog post "An Overview of End-to-End Exactly-Once Processing in Apache Flink", plus the TwoPhaseCommitSinkFunction documentation
Why this one: free, and it walks the pre-commit / commit protocol step by step with the exact failure points marked. It is also candid that the guarantee is a property of the whole chain, not of the framework — which is the point of the node.

DEEPER — KIP-98 again, from the sink side this time. Having read it for L3 and re-read it here is how the two branches fuse.

W10 Backpressure

In the engine it is backpressure; in Kafka it is lag. Same problem, two vantage points.

WHAT IT IS. The condition where an operator cannot consume its input as fast as it arrives, and the resulting slowdown propagates upstream until it reaches the source.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because unbounded buffering is not an option — a buffer that absorbs a permanent rate mismatch is just a slower way to run out of memory. Propagating the pressure to the source is the only stable response.

DEPENDENCIES. W6 · L3 K9.

The connection to name Credit-based flow control pushes pressure upstream until the source stops reading from Kafka. At that moment your engine-side backpressure becomes consumer lag in Kafka. They are one phenomenon observed from two places, and an engineer who says so has just demonstrated that L3 and L4 are joined in their head rather than filed separately.

SUBTOPICS. Detection — busy, idle and backpressured ratios per subtask · credit-based flow control · the diagnostic procedure · checkpoint alignment stalling under backpressure and unaligned checkpoints · the four responses — scale out, optimise the operator, buffer, or shed load · parallelism bounded by source partition count · load shedding, sampling and graceful degradation · why adding parallelism sometimes makes it worse.

The reading procedure Walk backwards from the sink. The bottleneck is the first operator that is busy while its upstream is backpressured. Everything upstream of it is backpressured as a consequence and is innocent; everything downstream is idle and is also innocent.

This is a specific, procedural answer to "how do you find the slow part", which is a question most candidates answer with a shrug and a metric name.
PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free blog · Title: the Flink blog, "A Deep-Dive into Flink's Network Stack" and "How to Identify the Source of Backpressure"
Why this one: free, and it explains credit-based flow control from the buffer level up, which is what turns backpressure from a red square in a UI into a mechanism you can reason about.

CONNECTS TO. L3 K9 lag · Distributed Systems N7 · Systems Thinking N4 (this is a balancing loop, and load shedding is what you add when the balancing loop is too slow).

W11 Engines compared — Structured Streaming versus Flink

The real differences, which are not the ones in the marketing comparisons.

WHAT IT IS. A capability comparison you assemble yourself by porting Lab 13 from Flink to Structured Streaming and recording what became harder.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because "which would you use" is a near-certain interview question, and the difference between a memorised answer and an earned one is audible.

DEPENDENCIES. W3 · W4 · W5 · L2.

ExecutionMicro-batch; latency floor is the trigger intervalPer-record; latency floor is the operator chain

The answer to give "Structured Streaming when the latency requirement is minutes and we already run Spark — because the marginal operational cost is near zero. Flink when the semantics are the hard part: sub-second latency, complex event-time logic, large keyed state, or side-outputs for reconciliation."

Then add the operational sentence — that introducing a second stream processor is a staffing decision as much as a technical one. That last clause is what makes the answer sound like it came from someone who has had to justify a platform choice rather than compare two feature lists.
PRIMARY RESOURCE

W12 When not to stream

The counterpart node, and often the strongest thing you can say in a design round.

WHAT IT IS. The set of conditions under which a streaming job is the wrong answer, and a scheduled batch job is the right one.

Do not stream when The consumer reads the result once a day. A daily dashboard fed by a streaming job has batch requirements and streaming costs.
Correctness requires completeness and lateness is unbounded. Financial close and reconciliation want a cutoff, not a guess.
The logic needs full history or global state. You would be reimplementing a batch engine badly.
A five-minute scheduled job would meet the SLA. It usually would, and it is not streaming.
Nobody is on call. A batch job is a program; a streaming job is a service. A failed batch job at 3 a.m. is a rerun. A failed streaming job at 3 a.m. is an incident, plus a state recovery, plus a backlog to burn down.
The interrogation to run when someone says "real-time" "How many seconds is real-time? Who reads this, and how often? What decision changes if the number is fifteen minutes old?"

The requirement collapses to a fifteen-minute batch job more often than not. Asking these three questions before designing anything is a senior move in an interview and a career-lengthening habit in reality — the streaming systems that are hardest to justify are the ones nobody was allowed to question.

CONNECTS TO. L7 · L3 K12 · L2 S13 — the three "when not to" nodes together are a single disposition, and it is the one that separates engineers from tool users.

13 Laboratory — Watermark & Late Data

Correct hourly counts that were already published wrong.

PROBLEM. Count completed orders per region per hour, from an event stream, and publish each hour's total at the top of the following hour. 5% of events arrive 1–30 minutes late; 0.1% arrive up to 6 hours late, from mobile clients that were offline.

INITIAL SYSTEM. Event-time tumbling hourly windows. Watermark = maximum observed timestamp, with zero delay. Late events dropped. No allowed lateness. Plus a batch oracle that computes the true count from the complete day of data.
FAILURE TO INTRODUCE — four escalations 1. The counts are low. Every hour. Compare against the oracle: roughly 5% missing, and the report went out already.
2. Set the watermark delay to 30 minutes. Counts improve markedly. You now publish 30 minutes later, and the 0.1% is still gone.
3. Set the delay to 6 hours. Counts are nearly exact. The dashboard is six hours behind and nobody will use it.
4. Idle one input partition. The watermark stalls at the minimum across inputs and no windows fire at all. Zero output, zero errors, every health check green.

CONCEPTS THAT EMERGE. Event time versus processing time → window assignment and firing → the watermark as a heuristic → bounded out-of-orderness → the latency/completeness curve → allowed lateness and restatement → the upsert requirement it imposes on the sink → side outputs and reconciliation → per-partition watermarks and idleness → session merging.

#ExperimentWhat it isolates
1Baseline: zero delay, drop late. Measure error against the oracle, per hourQuantify wrongness before fixing anything
2Sweep the watermark delay: 0, 1, 5, 15, 30, 60, 360 minutes. Plot completeness against emit latencyThe curve. This is the artifact of the lab
3Change the lateness distribution from uniform to long-tailed; re-run the sweepThe knee moves — there is no universal delay

WHAT TO OBSERVE. Completeness percentage against watermark delay; time-to-first-output; restatement counts; state size; and in experiment 7, the flat line where output should be. The artifact is the completeness–latency curve with your chosen operating point marked and justified.

REAL-WORLD CONNECTION. Every "why doesn't the real-time dashboard match the daily report" ticket ever raised. The answer is almost always a watermark delay that nobody chose deliberately — it was a default, and the drop rate was never measured.

PORTFOLIO VALUE. Very high, and unusual. Most streaming projects demonstrate that a pipeline runs. This one demonstrates a measurement: "at a 15-minute watermark we capture 99.4% of events at 15 minutes' latency; the remaining 0.6% is reconciled by a nightly batch." That single sentence is a complete senior answer to a question most people answer with a shrug.

The data structure this lab forces To emit events in event-time order from an out-of-order stream you must buffer and release, which is a min-heap keyed by timestamp, bounded by the watermark — pop everything below the watermark, hold the rest. The watermark is not merely a correctness concept; it is the bound that makes the buffer finite.

14 Laboratory — Stateful Processing & State Growth

A job that runs perfectly for six hours and then dies.

PROBLEM. Maintain per-user session metrics — session length, event count, last seen — over a continuous stream, and deduplicate a feed in which 2% of events are duplicates.

INITIAL SYSTEM. Keyed state on the heap backend. No TTL. Deduplication by an exact set of event IDs. Session windows with a 30-minute gap. Throughput held constant throughout.
FAILURE TO INTRODUCE Run it. It is flawless for six hours. Then GC time climbs, throughput sags, and it dies with OOM. The input rate never changed.

Now the instructive part: double the heap and re-run. It dies at twelve hours. You bought hours, not a fix — and having personally watched that happen is what makes the lesson stick where a blog post would not.

CONCEPTS THAT EMERGE. Keyed state and its partitioning → bounded versus unbounded key cardinality → state size as a function of cardinality × retention → heap versus RocksDB backends → the serialisation cost of a disk-backed backend → state TTL and lazy cleanup → approximate structures and the accuracy trade → checkpoint duration as a function of state size → recovery time → hot keys.

#ExperimentWhat it isolates
1Fixed key cardinality (10k users). Plot state size over timeIt plateaus
2Unbounded cardinality (new IDs forever). Same plotLinear, forever. The two curves side by side are the whole lesson
3Correlate state size with heap usage and GC time until death; record time-to-OOMThe failure is memory, not compute
4Double the memory; re-run; record the new time-to-OOMLinearity, confirmed by measurement
5Switch to RocksDB. Measure survival and the throughput costDisk-backed state is a trade, not a free fix
6Add state TTL. Find the plateau, and the lag before expired state actually disappearsLazy cleanup — the graph does not drop when you expect
7Replace the exact dedup set with a Bloom filter. Measure memory saved and the false-positive rateThen decide whether a false positive is acceptable here. In payments it is not — that decision is the point
8Plot checkpoint duration against state size; find where duration approaches the intervalThe checkpoint death spiral
9Kill and restore. Measure recovery time against state sizeLarge state makes recovery an outage, not a blip
10Introduce a hot key holding 40% of events; observe per-subtask state and lagThe power law, third appearance

WHAT TO OBSERVE. State size over time for both cardinality regimes; GC time; time-to-OOM at two memory settings; per-access latency on each backend; checkpoint duration and recovery time against state size; per-subtask skew. The artifact is a state budget — cardinality × per-key bytes × retention, computed in advance and then checked against measurement.

REAL-WORLD CONNECTION. The most common cause of streaming pages at 3 a.m., and the one least likely to be diagnosed correctly, because every throughput metric looks normal right up to the end.

PORTFOLIO VALUE. High, and rare. Being able to compute a streaming job's memory requirement before writing it — and then show the graph proving the estimate was right — is a capability very few candidates can demonstrate.

The data structure this lab forces The dedup path walks the entire ladder in one afternoon: hash set (exact, unbounded, fatal) → map with TTL (bounded by time, still large) → Bloom filter (bounded by design, with a false-positive rate you must compute and then justify) → LSM-backed store (bounded by disk, paying serialisation per access). 15 Laboratory — End-to-End Exactly-Once

Kill it mid-stream and prove, by count, that nothing was lost and nothing was duplicated.

PROBLEM. Maintain a running balance per account from a payment stream, written to an external database. The balance must be exactly correct after any crash, at any point, including a crash during the write.

INITIAL SYSTEM. Kafka source → keyed running sum → JDBC sink. Checkpointing enabled. At-least-once delivery to the sink. Plus a verifier that independently computes the true balance from the input topic, and a crash injector that kills the job at random points.
FAILURE TO INTRODUCE Kill the job twenty times at random. Balances are too high. Records were reprocessed and re-added after each restart.

Checkpointing protected the state and did nothing whatsoever for the sink. That is the realisation the lab exists to produce: exactly-once state is not exactly-once output, and the two are commonly confused by people who are otherwise fluent.

CONCEPTS THAT EMERGE. Checkpointed state versus emitted output as separate guarantees → replayable sources → idempotent writes, and why absolute values are idempotent while deltas are not → two-phase-commit sinks and the pre-commit/commit protocol → the failure window between them → transaction timeout versus checkpoint interval → operator UIDs and upgradability → effectively-once as the honest name → the cost of the guarantee, in throughput.

#ExperimentWhat it isolates
1Checkpointing off; crash repeatedlyThe floor: both state and output wrong
2Checkpointing on; crash repeatedly. Compare state correctness to output correctnessThe two guarantees, isolated. The central result
3Idempotent sink: upsert the absolute balance rather than applying a deltaWhy the shape of the write determines the guarantee
4Non-idempotent sink: an append-only ledger tableSink cooperation is not optional
5Two-phase-commit sink to Kafka; inspect the transactionsExactly-once, engine to engine
6Set the transaction timeout below the checkpoint interval; crashReal data loss. The production footgun
7Crash specifically between checkpoint-complete and commitRecovery must re-commit, not re-process
8Vary the checkpoint interval; plot recovery time and duplicate window against itThe interval is a trade-off, not a default
9Change the job graph with no operator UIDs; attempt restore. Then add UIDs and retryUpgradability as a design-time decision
10Measure throughput and latency at at-least-once versus exactly-onceThe cost, quantified rather than assumed

WHAT TO OBSERVE. Computed balance against true balance after every crash; duplicate and missing counts; throughput at each guarantee level; and in experiment 6, the exact records that vanished. The artifact is a guarantee table — source × state × sink → the actual end-to-end guarantee, with measured duplicate and loss counts in every cell.

REAL-WORLD CONNECTION. This is the payments interview, more or less verbatim. "Our pipeline is exactly-once" is usually true within the engine and false end-to-end, and the candidate who states where the boundary sits — and can show a table proving they found it by experiment — is answering a different, better question than the one asked.

PORTFOLIO VALUE. The highest of the three. A crash-injection harness with an independent verifier and a measured guarantee table is directly relevant to every regulated-data role, and it is not a project that can be faked by following a tutorial.

The move that distinguishes a senior answer here Give the trade-off before the mechanism. Every question in this branch has an answer of the form "you are trading completeness against latency, or accuracy against memory, and here is where I would sit and why". Candidates who name the mechanism first sound like they have read about streaming. Candidates who name the trade-off first sound like they have operated it — and the second group is very much smaller.
