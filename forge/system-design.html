Data system design

This is the round you will be judged on most heavily for a senior role, and it is a performance as much as a knowledge test — moving from vague requirements to a defended design in forty-five minutes, out loud, while being interrupted. Which means it is practised differently from everything else in the Forge: by writing, then by saying it.

The single idea. You are not being asked for the best design. You are being asked to show that you know what you traded away. Nobody can evaluate an architecture in forty-five minutes. Everybody can evaluate whether you know the cost of your own choices — and whether you found that cost yourself or waited to be told.
#trade-offs #failure-modes #estimation #cost #synthesis

00 What is actually being tested

And why generic system design preparation transfers only partially.

? “Design a system that ingests five hundred thousand clickstream events per second and lets analysts query them.” The correct first move is not to draw a box. It is to ask five questions, estimate two numbers out loud, and only then propose anything. Candidates who begin drawing have already told the interviewer that they design from patterns rather than from requirements.
What makes this different from generic system design Generic system design asks "how many servers?" Data system design asks "what happens to this number when the data is late, skewed, or wrong?"

Load balancers, caches and QPS are not the vocabulary here. The vocabulary is throughput, latency, correctness, schema evolution, cost and recovery — and the questions are about the data's own properties rather than about the traffic hitting it.
The failure mode of this branch Practising it silently. Writing a good design document is necessary and completely insufficient — the round is spoken, interrupted, and time-boxed. Every one of the seven designs below should be written first and then delivered aloud in under twenty minutes, from the document, then again without it. The gap between what you can write and what you can say under interruption is the actual thing being trained.

01 The six axes

Every trade-off in every design lands on at least one of these.

AxisThe questionWhere it was learned
ThroughputEvents per second, bytes per day, and the peak-to-average ratioL3 K9 · L2
LatencyHow stale may the answer be, in secondsL4 W3 · L1 M4
CorrectnessWhat is the cost of a wrong number, and for how longL6 R1 · L4 W9
Schema evolutionWhat happens when the producer changes somethingL6 R6 · L0 B5
CostStorage, compute, and the staffing cost of another system to operateL0 Lab 01 · L6 R12
RecoveryHow long to catch up after a day of downtime, and what is lostL4 W6 · L5 P6
Recovery is the axis nobody mentions "A day of downtime on a daily batch job is one missing partition and a re-run. A day of downtime on a streaming job is a backlog that takes six hours to drain at double throughput — during which you are also processing new data." Bringing recovery up unprompted is one of the most reliable ways to sound like someone who has operated systems rather than designed them.

02 The seven data characteristics

Interrogate these before proposing anything. Two of them are almost never asked about, and both determine the architecture.

CharacteristicWhy it changes the design
VolumeBytes per day decides storage tiering and whether compute is the cost driver
VelocityRate, and the peak-to-average ratio — you design for peak, you pay for average
CardinalityDecides state size, aggregate explosion, dedup feasibility and partition count. Almost never asked about
SkewDecides whether your partitioning works at all. Also almost never asked about, and always present
LatenessDecides the watermark, the reprocessing strategy and the reconciliation layer
MutabilityAppend-only is easy. Updates and deletes change everything downstream
RetentionDecides cost, tiering, and whether deletion is even possible
The two questions that separate you immediately "What does the cardinality of the key look like?" and "Is the distribution skewed — are there hot keys?"

Nearly nobody asks these, and they determine whether the design works. They are also the questions that let you reference L2 skew, L3 hot partitions and L4 state growth as one phenomenon — which is the strongest single move available in this round.

03 The method

Seven steps. Three of them are what make it data system design rather than system design.

#StepWhat it contains
1RequirementsWho consumes this, and what decision does it drive. Not "what should it do"
2Data characteristicsThe seven above, with numbers, estimated aloud
3OptionsAt least two, named — even if one is obviously better
4Trade-offsEach option against the six axes
5DesignThe chosen one, drawn, with the key decisions justified
6Failure modesWhat breaks, how you would know, and the blast radius
7CostStorage, compute, and the cost of one more system to operate
Steps 2, 6 and 7 These three are the difference, and they are the three most people skip.

Step 2 because it requires arithmetic in public. Step 6 because it requires admitting your design breaks. Step 7 because it requires an opinion about money. A design with all three is senior almost independently of the architecture it proposes.
Time budget for forty-five minutes Requirements and characteristics: eight minutes, and do not rush this. Options and trade-offs: seven. Design: fifteen. Failure modes: eight. Cost: four. Buffer: three.

If you are drawing at minute four, you are already failing. If you have not reached failure modes by minute thirty-five, cut the design short and go there — the failure section is worth more than the last third of the architecture.

04 The first five minutes

Five questions, in this order, before anything else.

#QuestionWhat it settles
1Who consumes this, and what decision does it drive?Latency requirement and correctness stakes, both at once
2How many seconds is "real-time"?Collapses most streaming requirements into batch
3What is the volume, and the peak-to-average ratio?Whether you are designing for Tuesday or for Black Friday
4Is the data mutable? Can it arrive late, and how late?The entire correctness architecture
5Retention, and who is allowed to see it?Storage cost and the governance layer
The best single question in the set "What happens if this number is wrong for a day?"

The answer sets your entire correctness budget in one sentence. "The marketing team makes a slightly worse decision" and "we misreport to the regulator" are the same question with two architectures, and asking it is how you avoid designing the expensive one for the cheap problem — or, far worse, the reverse.

05 Numbers to know cold

Doing arithmetic aloud is a large part of the performance. These are the constants that make it possible.

QuantityRule of thumb
Event volume1 KB × 100k/sec = 100 MB/s ≈ 8.6 TB/day raw
CompressionParquet + zstd on event data: roughly 10×. So 8.6 TB/day → ~900 GB/day
Kafka partitionComfortably ~10 MB/s. 100 MB/s needs 10–20 partitions for throughput, more for consumer parallelism
Spark taskShould process 128 MB – 1 GB. A 1 TB job is roughly 1,000–8,000 tasks
Object storage~100 MB/s per connection. Bandwidth comes only from parallelism
NetworkWithin a datacentre ~0.5 ms. Cross-region tens to hundreds of ms
Warehouse scanOrder of $5 per TB scanned. A daily 1 TB full scan ≈ $1,800/year, forever
Streaming recoveryA day of backlog drains in ~half a day at 2× throughput — while also taking new data
Why this section exists "That is about nine hundred gigabytes a day compressed, so two years of retention is roughly six hundred and fifty terabytes — which is a storage problem, not a compute problem" is a sentence that takes fifteen seconds and changes how the rest of the conversation goes. The numbers do not need to be right. They need to be reasoned, aloud, and roughly right.

D1 Clickstream and event ingestion at scale

The append-only case. Easy correctness, hard volume, and one constraint unique to mobile.

THE PROMPT. Five hundred thousand events per second from web and mobile clients. Analysts need to query it. Some subset needs to be near-real-time.

CHARACTERISTICS. Volume 500 MB/s ≈ 43 TB/day raw, ~4 TB/day compressed · velocity bursty at 5–10× peak · cardinality high on user_id, low on event_type · skew from bots and celebrity content · lateness from mobile clients offline for hours or days · mutability none — append only · retention typically 13 months hot.

DESIGN. Stateless autoscaled collectors → Kafka, partitioned by session ID rather than user ID (better distribution, and it preserves the ordering that actually matters) → two independent consumers: a raw archiver writing Iceberg partitioned by hour, and a streaming aggregator for the real-time subset only → hourly compaction.

DecisionThe trade
Partition key: session vs user vs randomOrdering against skew. Session is usually the right point on that curve
At-least-once + dedup on event_id, not exactly-onceExactly-once costs throughput and buys nothing here — a duplicated pageview is not a duplicated payment
Archive everything raw, aggregate a subsetStorage is cheap; unasked questions are expensive (L1 M5)
FAILURE MODES — and the fourth one is specific to clients you do not control Bot traffic creating a hot partition (L3 K9) · mobile events arriving hours late and missing hourly windows (L4 W3) · small files from the streaming writer (L0 B7) · and a schema change from an app version you cannot force-upgrade.

You cannot deploy to a phone the user has not updated, so the event schema must remain forward-compatible essentially forever. That constraint does not exist in server-to-server designs, and naming it is a strong signal that you have shipped a mobile pipeline.

COST. 4 TB/day × 13 months ≈ 1.6 PB. Storage dominates; compute is bursty and should be spot/preemptible. WEAK ANSWER: partitions by user ID, insists on exactly-once, ignores late mobile events, assumes old app versions eventually disappear.

D2 Real-time analytics serving

Usually three different requirements wearing one name.

THE PROMPT. A dashboard showing metrics updated within seconds, over billions of rows, for hundreds of concurrent users.

The insight that is the design "Real-time analytics" is normally three requirements bundled together: high-concurrency fixed aggregations, low-latency point lookups, and ad-hoc exploration. Separating them is the design; serving all three from one system is the mistake.

DESIGN. A streaming job maintaining pre-aggregated rollups → a real-time OLAP store (Druid, Pinot or ClickHouse) for the fixed high-concurrency aggregations · a key-value store for point lookups · the warehouse for ad hoc. Recent data from the real-time layer and history from the batch layer, unioned at query time.

DecisionThe trade
Pre-aggregate vs compute on the flyFast and inflexible against flexible and slow. Pre-aggregate the top twenty query shapes only
Approximate vs exactHyperLogLog for distinct counts, t-digest for quantiles. Ask whether 0.5% error is acceptable — it usually is, and it changes the cost by an order of magnitude
Union at the seamCorrect, and the seam is where the two layers will disagree

FAILURE MODES. Cardinality explosion in the rollups — adding one high-cardinality dimension multiplies the pre-aggregate size, and this is the classic way these systems become unaffordable · the real-time and batch layers disagreeing at the join point · concurrency limits under a Monday-morning dashboard load · the streaming job's state growing (L4 W7).

COST. Real-time OLAP stores are memory-resident and cost scales with cardinality, not with row count — which is why the rollup dimension list is a budget decision. WEAK ANSWER: one system for all three query shapes, and no question about acceptable approximation.

D3 CDC replication to a warehouse

The mutable case, and the one design where you can take down the source system.

THE PROMPT. Replicate two hundred tables from Postgres into the warehouse, near-real-time, and prove they match.

CHARACTERISTICS. Mutability is the whole point · volume moderate · lateness is not the issue; ordering is · retention driven by the source's own retention plus audit.

DESIGN. Debezium → Kafka, one topic per table keyed by primary key → a sink performing LSN-conditional upsert into Iceberg → a scheduled key-set reconciliation job comparing source and target (L6 R3).

DecisionThe trade
Log-based over query-basedDeletes and intermediate states, at the cost of replication privileges (L5 P7)
Copy-on-write vs merge-on-read, per tableWith two hundred tables you want both, chosen by update frequency — cheap writes and expensive reads, or the reverse
One topic per tableIndependent retention, compaction and replay per table, at the cost of topic sprawl
FAILURE MODES — name the first one before you are asked A stalled consumer retains WAL until the source database's disk fills. This design is the only one in the set where your pipeline can take down the production transactional system, and saying so unprompted is the single strongest move available in this design (L0 B10).

Then: out-of-order updates (L5 P9) · the snapshot-to-stream seam (L5 P8) · tombstones compacted before consumption · a schema change at source with no coordination.

COST. Dominated by compaction, not by ingest. WEAK ANSWER: query-based polling, no delete handling, no reconciliation, and no awareness that the replication slot is a loaded weapon.

D4 Financial transaction analytics with audit requirements

the one to be best at Correctness dominates; everything else is negotiable.

THE PROMPT. Payments data feeding both analytics and regulatory reporting. Every figure must be auditable and reproducible.

The clarifying question that reframes the whole design "What must be reproducible — a number, or the entire report as it stood on the filing date?" The second answer requires bi-temporal modelling and snapshot-pinned report runs. The first does not. Most candidates never ask, and design for the cheaper one by accident.

DESIGN. An immutable event log as the system of record → bi-temporal warehouse models (L1 M9) carrying valid time and transaction time → every report run records the snapshot IDs it read, so it can be reproduced byte for byte later — this is what table-format time travel is genuinely for → effectively-once end-to-end with a written scope statement (L4 W9) → daily reconciliation against the source of record → append-only audit tables.

DecisionThe trade
Batch with a hard cutoff, plus a streaming view labelled provisionalStreaming results depend on a watermark, which is a guess. A regulator does not accept a guess — but the business still wants the intraday number
Late transactions to an exception queue, not a silent restatementA known, reviewed exception beats an unexplained number change (L4 W4)
Retention obligation vs erasure requestA genuine legal conflict. Usually the transaction record is retained and the PII is pseudonymised — and crypto-shredding is the mechanism (L6 R11)

FAILURE MODES. A transaction arriving after the reporting cutoff · a restatement of an already filed report, and the process for it · the deletion/retention conflict · an exactly-once claim that is true inside Kafka and false at the warehouse boundary · and reproducibility quietly lost the day someone expires an old snapshot.

COST. Retention dominates, and audit copies multiply it. WEAK ANSWER: claims exactly-once without scoping it, has no restatement process, and treats correctness as a tunable rather than as the requirement.

D5 Feature platform for ML

One data engineering responsibility inside ML infrastructure, and it is unambiguous.

THE PROMPT. Serve features to models in production, and generate training data that is consistent with what was served.

THE DEFINING PROBLEM. Train/serve skew — the same feature computed two different ways in two places, by two teams, with two subtly different definitions.

DESIGN. One definition, two materialisations. A single transformation feeds an offline store (Iceberg, for training) and an online store (Redis or DynamoDB, for serving). Never two implementations, regardless of how much faster the second one would be to write.

Point-in-time correctness, which is the thing to be able to explain Training data must join the feature value as of the prediction time, not as of now. Otherwise the model trains on information that did not exist when the decision was made.

That is a temporal join (L4 W8) and a Type 2 lookup (L1 M9) — the third appearance of one idea. Getting it wrong produces a model that scores brilliantly offline and fails in production, and it is a data engineering failure rather than a modelling one.

FAILURE MODES. Train/serve skew from two implementations · label leakage from a non-point-in-time join · online store staleness against a freshness SLO nobody wrote · cardinality of the entity space exceeding the online store's budget.

COST. The online store dominates, and it scales with entities × features. WEAK ANSWER: two pipelines, and no mention of point-in-time correctness.

D6 Large-scale reporting and the semantic layer

An organisational problem with a technical component — and saying so is the correct opening.

THE PROMPT. Three hundred analysts, thousands of dashboards, three conflicting definitions of "active user", and a warehouse bill growing faster than headcount.

THE OPENING QUESTIONS. How many of those dashboards were opened last month? What are the top twenty queries by cost? Where do the conflicting definitions physically live? All three are answerable from metadata you already have, and all three reframe the problem from "we need a tool" to "we need to see what is happening".

DESIGN. Conformed dimensions (L1 M7) · metric definitions in version control (L1 M12) · a tiered trust model — certified datasets for anything that leaves the company, self-serve for exploration, clearly labelled · pre-aggregation for the top query shapes only · cost attribution per team (L6 R12) · lineage-driven deletion of the dashboards nobody opens (L6 R10).

THE FAILURE MODE THAT KILLS THIS DESIGN People route around the semantic layer. A certification queue that takes two weeks produces a shadow warehouse in someone's personal schema within a month. If they route around it, it has failed, regardless of its technical merit — so the design must include a fast path, and the fast path is the actual engineering problem.

COST. Attribution reveals it, and the four findings are always the same: a handful of queries dominate, a dashboard refreshes far more often than anyone reads it, a BI tool issues SELECT *, and something scans the largest table unpartitioned. WEAK ANSWER: proposes a tool.

D7 Multi-tenant SaaS data architecture

One distribution fact drives the entire design, and it is always true.

THE PROMPT. Analytics for thousands of customer tenants, with isolation guarantees.

The fact to state first The tenant size distribution is a power law. Always. The largest tenant is bigger than the bottom half combined. Which means a single uniform architecture is wrong at both ends — over-engineered for the long tail and under-provisioned for the whale.

THE ISOLATION SPECTRUM. Shared table with a tenant_id column (cheapest, weakest) → shared table partitioned or clustered by tenant → schema per tenant → database per tenant (strongest, most operational cost). The answer is usually a hybrid: shared for the long tail, dedicated for the largest tenants, with a defined promotion path between tiers.

THE CLARIFYING QUESTION. "Is the isolation requirement legal, contractual, or performance?" — three different answers with three different architectures, and they are frequently conflated by the person asking.

FAILURE MODES — the first one ends the company The cross-tenant data leak. A missing WHERE clause in one query is not an acceptable distance between "working" and "catastrophe". The design must make the leak structurally impossible — row-level security enforced by the engine, or physical separation — rather than checked by convention.

Then: a large tenant starving the shared tier · per-tenant schema drift · a tenant requesting deletion from shared physical files · and the migration when a tenant outgrows its tier, which must be designed before it is needed.

COST. Per-tenant chargeback is a product requirement here, not merely an internal one — customers will ask what they are paying for. WEAK ANSWER: one shared table, a WHERE tenant_id = as the only isolation, and no plan for the largest customer.

:: How this round is actually failed

Almost never by proposing the wrong architecture.

Anti-patternWhat it signals
Drawing before askingYou design from patterns rather than from requirements
Naming tools instead of mechanisms"Kafka and Spark" is a shopping list. "A partitioned durable log so consumers can replay independently" is a design
No numbersThe design is not evaluable, and you know it
One option presented as obviousYou have not considered alternatives, or you cannot articulate why you rejected them
No failure sectionThe most common gap and the most damaging
Refusing a constraint"Assume no managed services" is a test of adaptability, not a trick
Over-engineeringStreaming for a daily report. Strong candidates propose the simpler thing surprisingly often
Ignoring costA design that would cost more than the product earns
The single highest-value habit Say the trade-off out loud at the moment you make the choice — not at the end when asked. "I am partitioning by session rather than user; that costs me per-user ordering and buys me a distribution that will not develop a hot partition when a bot arrives." One sentence, at the moment of decision, repeated ten times across a design, is what the round is measuring.

:: The interruptions

What the interviewer says mid-design, and what each one is testing.

They sayThey are testingWhat a strong response does
“What if the volume were a hundred times larger?”Whether your design has a scaling axis or needs a rewriteName the component that breaks first, and at what number
“What if you could not use Kafka?”Whether you know what Kafka was providingDurability, replay, and consumer decoupling — then propose a substitute for each
“How would you know it was broken?”L6, directlyNamed checks and an expected detection latency
“That is expensive. Make it cheaper.”Whether you know which component dominates costCut the dominant line, and state what correctness or latency you gave up
“Now it has to be sub-second.”Whether you know the latency ladder's cost stepsWhich rung, what it costs, and what becomes approximate at that rung
“Why not just use a database?”Whether you can defend complexity — and whether you would concedeSometimes the honest answer is "you are right, up to about this volume"
“What would you build first?”Prioritisation, and whether you can ship an incrementThe thinnest end-to-end slice that produces one real number
“What is the biggest risk in your design?”Nothing. This is a giftName a real one. A candidate who names a genuine weakness is instantly more credible than one who cannot find any
Practice protocol Write each of the seven designs as a document. Then deliver it aloud in under twenty minutes from the document. Then again without it. Then have someone interrupt you three times from the table above. The written version is preparation; the third delivery is the skill.

These seven documents are also portfolio artifacts directly — a repository of design documents with failure-mode and cost sections is unusual, readable in five minutes, and demonstrates the exact thing the round is trying to measure.
