Apache Spark

Five years of Spark usage produces fluency with the API and, usually, a set of performance habits absorbed from colleagues without their reasons attached. This branch replaces the habits with mechanisms — so that "we repartitioned before the join" becomes a decision you can defend, quantify, and know the failure mode of.

The single idea. Almost every slow Spark job is slow because of data movement, and almost all data movement is the shuffle. Stage boundaries, join strategy, skew, spill, AQE and memory pressure are not separate topics — they are six views of the same event. Understand the shuffle completely and the rest of Spark becomes consequences.
#shuffle #catalyst #skew #memory #spark-ui

00 The one question this branch exists to answer

Every senior Spark interview converges on it, in some phrasing.

? “This job used to take 12 minutes and now takes 90. Walk me through how you would find out why, and what you would change first.” Nothing about syntax. It tests whether you can move from a symptom, to a specific stage, to a specific cause, to a change with a predicted effect — out loud, without the cluster in front of you.

Answering it requires four things in a specific order, and this branch is organised around them:

The failure mode of this branch Reading about Catalyst and AQE without watching them act. Every concept here has an observable consequence in an explain plan or the Spark UI. If you cannot point to where you would see it, you do not have it — because that is exactly the follow-up question.

01 The environment

One setup decision determines whether the labs teach anything. Get this right first.

Local mode alone is not sufficient for this branch. Several of the most important behaviours — shuffle fetch across the network, executor loss, external shuffle service, per-executor memory pressure — either do not occur or are invisible with a single JVM.

Three settings to fix before starting Enable the event log so every run is inspectable afterwards. Set executor memory deliberately small — a few hundred megabytes — so spill and OOM happen at data sizes you can generate in seconds rather than hours. Turn AQE off by default and switch it on explicitly per experiment, because AQE silently fixes several of the problems these labs exist to make you see.

Data. Generate it rather than downloading it. You need control over cardinality, skew and size, and a generator with tunable key distribution is itself the first useful artifact of this branch.

S1 Architecture

Who runs what, where, and what dies when.

WHAT IT IS. A driver process holding the SparkContext and the DAG scheduler; executors as long-lived JVMs holding cached data and running tasks; a cluster manager allocating them. One application contains many jobs, each job many stages, each stage many tasks.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because the unit of parallelism must be smaller than the unit of failure, and both must be smaller than the unit of scheduling. The task is the retry unit, the executor is the loss unit, the application is the billing unit — and confusing those three is the source of most operational misunderstanding.

DEPENDENCIES. L0 distributed minimum.

SUBTOPICS. Driver responsibilities and why driver OOM is different from executor OOM · executor lifecycle and dynamic allocation · cluster manager options and what each actually changes · client vs cluster deploy mode and where the driver lives · cores, slots and task concurrency · what survives an executor loss and what does not.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free online book · Title: The Internals of Apache Spark — Jacek Laskowski, free at books.japila.pl
Why this one: free, and it is written from the source code outward. It is the only comprehensive free treatment of Spark's internals that stays current, and it is the reference you will return to for every node in this branch.

DEEPER — the Spark source on GitHub, specifically DAGScheduler and TaskSchedulerImpl. Read after S2, not before.
OBSERVATION · Find where your driver actually is Run the same job in client mode and in cluster mode on the standalone cluster. In one case the driver is your terminal; in the other it is inside the cluster. Now kill the submitting terminal in each case and observe which job survives.

Then trigger the two OOMs deliberately: a collect() of a large dataset kills the driver; a wide aggregation with tiny executor memory kills an executor. Note that the first produces no retry and the second does — the task is the retry unit, and the driver has no equivalent.

CONNECTS TO. S2 · S8 (driver vs executor memory) · Infrastructure (containers and resource limits).

S2 Execution model

Lazy evaluation, the DAG, and the one question: what creates a stage boundary?

WHAT IT IS. Transformations build a logical plan and execute nothing; an action triggers the scheduler to compile that plan into a DAG of stages, each stage a set of tasks that can run without moving data between partitions.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because laziness lets the optimiser see the whole computation before choosing how to execute it — which is what makes predicate pushdown, column pruning and join reordering possible at all. Eager evaluation would forfeit every optimisation in S5.

DEPENDENCIES. S1.

SUBTOPICS. Transformations vs actions · narrow vs wide dependencies · what creates a stage boundary — the single most asked question in this branch · DAG scheduler vs task scheduler · pipelining within a stage · task locality levels · speculative execution · why a job count can exceed your action count.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free online book · Title: Learning Spark, 2nd Edition — Damji et al., free PDF from Databricks — chapters 2–3
Why this one: free, current, and it establishes the job/stage/task vocabulary precisely before any performance discussion. Getting this vocabulary exact is what lets you describe a problem specifically in an interview rather than saying "the job was slow".
OBSERVATION · Predict the stage count, then check Write five short queries — a filter, a filter plus map, a groupBy, a join, a join followed by a groupBy. Before running each, write down how many stages you expect and why. Then run and check the UI.

You will be wrong at least once, and the wrongness is the lesson. Every stage boundary is a shuffle, and every shuffle is a stage boundary — that identity is the most useful single fact in this node, and deriving it from your own wrong prediction makes it permanent.

Then find the surprise: run a query with a single action and observe more than one job in the UI. Work out what caused the extra job. It is usually the optimiser sampling for statistics, or a Dataset operation that must inspect the data to plan.

CONNECTS TO. S3 directly — this node exists to arrive at it · S12 · Computing N7 (cost models).

S3 The shuffle spine

The single most important sub-branch in L2. Everything else radiates from it.

WHAT IT IS. The redistribution of data across partitions so that all records with the same key land on the same task. Map-side: partition, sort, and write to local disk. Reduce-side: fetch the relevant blocks from every upstream executor over the network, merge, and process.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because grouping and joining require co-location, and co-location requires movement. There is no way to compute a global aggregate without moving data — the only question is how much, how well overlapped, and whether it fits in memory on arrival.

DEPENDENCIES. S2. Benefits from Computing N4 and OS N4 — shuffle is disk and network behaviour wearing a Spark costume.

SUBTOPICS. Shuffle write path: bucketing by partition, sorting, spilling, the index file · shuffle read path: fetch requests, remote vs local blocks, fetch failures and stage retries · the external shuffle service and why it decouples shuffle files from executor life · push-based shuffle · spark.sql.shuffle.partitions and why the default is almost always wrong · shuffle spill (memory and disk) as distinct metrics · why a fetch failure retries a whole stage rather than a task · shuffle compression and serialisation cost.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free official documentation · Title: the Spark Tuning Guide and SQL Performance Tuning pages, read with The Internals of Apache Spark chapters on the shuffle system
Why this one: both free. The official tuning guide states the levers; Laskowski explains what they act on. Reading the lever without the mechanism is how people acquire performance superstitions.

DEEPER — Spark source: ShuffleMapTask, SortShuffleWriter, ShuffleBlockFetcherIterator. Worth one afternoon after Lab 05.

THE LAB. Laboratory 05 — Shuffle & Skew, below. This node is not learnable by reading.

The mental model to build here A shuffle is a distributed sort-and-transfer with a memory budget. Every shuffle problem is one of four things: too much data moved · moved unevenly · not fitting in memory on arrival · or a failure forcing it to happen twice. Being able to say which of the four you are looking at, from the UI, is the skill this node is for.

CONNECTS TO. S4 (how much and where) · S8 (fitting) · S9 (avoiding) · S10 (evenness) · S7 (fixing it at runtime).

S4 Partitioning

The lever that controls parallelism, memory per task, and how much shuffle happens at all.

WHAT IT IS. How the dataset is divided across tasks — on read, after a shuffle, and by explicit instruction.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because the partition is simultaneously the unit of parallelism, the unit of memory pressure, and the unit of skew. Too few and you lose parallelism and risk OOM; too many and scheduling overhead and small-file writes dominate.

DEPENDENCIES. S2, S3.

SUBTOPICS. Input partitioning: maxPartitionBytes, file splitting, and why non-splittable compression destroys parallelism · default parallelism vs shuffle partitions and the difference · repartition vs coalesce — full shuffle vs narrow merge, and the trap where coalesce reduces upstream parallelism too · hash vs range partitioning · repartitionByRange and its sampling · bucketing and pre-shuffled joins · partition pruning vs partition count.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free official documentation · Title: Spark SQL Performance Tuning — the partitioning and bucketing sections
Why this one: free, and the bucketing section in particular explains the one technique that removes a shuffle entirely rather than making it cheaper, which is the strongest available optimisation and the least used.
OBSERVATION · The coalesce trap Build a job with an expensive map over 200 partitions, then write the output. Add coalesce(1) before the write to avoid small files. Runtime collapses to single-threaded.

Coalesce does not add a shuffle, so it propagates backwards — the whole upstream stage now runs with one task. Replace it with repartition(1) and the map runs at full parallelism with a shuffle before the write. Compare the two runtimes and note that the "cheaper" operation was catastrophically more expensive. This is the most common Spark performance mistake in production and it is entirely counter-intuitive.

CONNECTS TO. S3 · S10 · S11 (small files) · Data Engineering N2.

S5 Catalyst

The optimiser as an object you can inspect, argue with, and occasionally overrule.

WHAT IT IS. The query compiler: parse into an unresolved plan, resolve against the catalog, apply rule-based rewrites, choose physical operators with cost-based input, and generate code.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because the declarative promise — you say what, Spark decides how — requires something to decide. Catalyst is why the same result can be computed a hundred times faster without changing your code, and why it sometimes is not.

DEPENDENCIES. S2 · Databases N3 (query planning is the same discipline).

SUBTOPICS. The four phases and what each can and cannot do · rule-based rewrites: predicate pushdown, column pruning, constant folding, filter reordering, null propagation · cost-based optimisation and its dependence on statistics · ANALYZE TABLE and what happens without it · reading explain(mode="formatted") and explain("cost") · why UDFs are optimisation barriers · the difference between the parsed, analysed, optimised and physical plans.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free online book · Title: The Internals of Spark SQL — Jacek Laskowski, free at books.japila.pl
Why this one: free, and it documents the individual optimiser rules by name. Being able to name the rule that fired — or did not — is the difference between describing the plan and explaining it.

DEEPER — the original Catalyst paper, Spark SQL: Relational Data Processing in Spark (SIGMOD 2015), freely available. Short, and it states the design goals directly.

S6 Tungsten

Why Spark stopped being a JVM object-graph engine.

WHAT IT IS. The execution backend: binary row format outside the JVM heap, whole-stage code generation that collapses an operator chain into a single tight loop, and vectorised readers for columnar sources.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because at scale the bottleneck stopped being I/O and became CPU, garbage collection and virtual function call overhead. Tungsten's answer was to stop creating Java objects per row and stop dispatching per operator.

DEPENDENCIES. S5 · Computing N4 (cache locality) · Computing N1 (binary representation).

SUBTOPICS. UnsafeRow and off-heap memory · why serialisation dominates without it · whole-stage code generation and the WholeStageCodegen boxes in the UI · when codegen is disabled and why · vectorised Parquet reading · cache-friendly memory layout · the practical consequence: why DataFrame operations beat RDD operations by large factors.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free engineering post · Title: the Databricks engineering blog posts on Project Tungsten and whole-stage code generation — free
Why this one: free, written by the implementers, and they include the benchmark reasoning. They also explain why the JVM was the problem, which connects this directly to Computing N4's memory hierarchy.
OBSERVATION · Watch codegen collapse a pipeline Run a chain of filters and projections and open the physical plan. Note the WholeStageCodegen box wrapping several operators. Then insert a Python UDF in the middle and look again — the box splits in two, with the UDF between them.

You have just made the optimisation barrier visible. Time both. Then replace the UDF with a native expression and watch the box re-fuse and the runtime drop. This single observation is the complete argument for avoiding UDFs, and it is far more convincing than being told.

CONNECTS TO. S5 · S8 (off-heap memory) · Computing N4.

S7 Adaptive Query Execution

The optimiser admitting it cannot know enough before the job runs.

WHAT IT IS. Re-optimisation at stage boundaries using real runtime statistics: coalescing shuffle partitions, splitting skewed join partitions, and switching join strategy after seeing actual sizes.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because Catalyst plans with estimates, and estimates on multi-stage pipelines compound their errors. AQE exists because static cost-based optimisation is structurally unable to know the size of an intermediate result.

DEPENDENCIES. S3, S5. Understand the problems before the feature that hides them.

SUBTOPICS. Dynamically coalescing shuffle partitions — and why this makes shuffle.partitions tuning less critical · skew join handling by splitting large partitions · runtime join strategy switching to broadcast · dynamic partition pruning · the configuration surface and the thresholds that matter · what AQE cannot fix — skew inside a single key, skew in a non-join aggregation, and bad partitioning at read time.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free official documentation + free engineering post · Title: Spark SQL Performance Tuning, the AQE section, with the Databricks AQE announcement post
Why this one: both free, and the announcement post shows the before-and-after plans. Seeing the plan change at runtime is the whole point, and the documentation alone does not convey it.
OBSERVATION · Run every experiment twice The discipline for this node: every experiment in Labs 05 and 08 is run with AQE off, then on. Record both. The delta is the measurement of what AQE does, and it is far more informative than either number alone.

Then find AQE's limits deliberately: construct a skew where a single key is enormous. AQE can split a partition but cannot split a key, so the straggler remains. That boundary is the answer to "when do you still need salting?" — a question you will be asked.

CONNECTS TO. S10 · S9 · S4.

S8 Memory and spill

Five distinct OOMs with five distinct fixes — and most people know one.

WHAT IT IS. The unified memory model: a region shared between execution (shuffle, sort, join hash tables) and storage (cached blocks), with execution able to evict storage but not the reverse, plus reserved and user memory outside it.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because static partitioning between caching and execution wasted whichever half was idle. Unified memory made the boundary dynamic, at the cost of making OOM diagnosis harder — the failure now depends on what else was happening.

DEPENDENCIES. S1, S3, S6.

SUBTOPICS. Executor memory layout: reserved, user, execution, storage · the fraction settings · on-heap vs off-heap · overhead memory and why the container dies rather than the JVM · spill to disk as designed behaviour rather than failure · the five OOM causes — driver collect, oversized broadcast, skewed partition, too few partitions, and container overhead — each with a different fix · GC pressure and how to see it.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free official documentation · Title: the Spark Tuning Guide memory management section, with The Internals of Apache Spark on UnifiedMemoryManager
Why this one: free, and the internals reference gives you the actual arithmetic of how the regions are sized. Being able to compute how much execution memory a task actually gets is what turns a guess into a diagnosis.

S9 Joins

Where the optimiser's most consequential decision is made.

WHAT IT IS. The physical strategies — broadcast hash, sort-merge, shuffle hash, broadcast nested loop — and the rules by which one is chosen.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because a join is the most expensive common operation, and the right strategy differs by orders of magnitude. Broadcasting a small side eliminates the shuffle entirely; getting the size estimate wrong kills the driver.

DEPENDENCIES. S3, S5. Databases N3 is the same material at single-node scale.

SUBTOPICS. The four strategies and their cost shapes · autoBroadcastJoinThreshold and why it is compared against an estimate · why a broadcast collects to the driver first · sort-merge join and its sort cost · shuffle hash join and when it beats sort-merge · bucketed joins as pre-shuffled data · join hints and when overriding is correct · join reordering · outer joins and why they restrict strategy · the null-key problem — nulls all hash to one partition.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free official documentation · Title: Spark SQL Performance Tuning — join strategy hints, plus The Internals of Spark SQL on JoinSelection
Why this one: free, and JoinSelection is a readable decision tree. Reading the actual selection logic means you can predict the strategy before running the job, which is a genuinely impressive interview move.

S10 Skew

The normal case, not the exception — and the most common real-world Spark problem.

WHAT IT IS. Uneven distribution of records across partitions, so one task processes far more data than the others and the stage cannot finish until it does.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because real key distributions are power-law, not uniform. One customer, one product, one null, one default value — every real dataset has them, and hash partitioning faithfully sends all of them to one place.

DEPENDENCIES. S3, S4 · Probability N4 (heavy tails are the same phenomenon).

SUBTOPICS. Detection in the UI: task duration distribution, max vs median shuffle read · detection in data: key frequency histogram before running · sources of skew — nulls, defaults, sentinel values, celebrity keys, time-of-day clustering · salting and the two-stage aggregation it requires · isolated map join for a single hot key · AQE skew handling and its boundary · why repartition alone does not fix key skew.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free conference talks · Title: Data+AI Summit / Spark Summit talks on skew and performance tuning — free on YouTube
Why this one: free, and skew is the topic where practitioner talks genuinely beat documentation, because they show the actual UI screenshots of a skewed stage, which is what you need to recognise it in three seconds.

THE LAB. Laboratory 05 — skew is half of it.

The diagnostic to internalise In the stage summary, compare the max task duration against the median. A ratio above roughly 5× is skew until proven otherwise. Then check max shuffle read bytes against median. Two numbers, ten seconds, and it is the first thing to look at in any slow stage.

CONNECTS TO. S7 · S9 · Probability N4 · L3 Kafka hot partitions — the identical problem in a different system.

S11 I/O and caching

Where Spark meets storage, and where caching stops helping.

WHAT IT IS. How Spark reads and writes files — pruning, pushdown, split behaviour — and the persistence API for reusing computed data.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because the cheapest work is work not done. Partition pruning, column pruning and predicate pushdown eliminate reads entirely, which beats any amount of downstream optimisation. Caching is the opposite trade: spend memory to avoid recomputation, and it frequently loses.

DEPENDENCIES. S5 · L0 file formats.

SUBTOPICS. Partition pruning vs dynamic partition pruning · column pruning · predicate pushdown and which predicates actually push · file listing cost at scale and why it dominates on cloud storage · split sizes and non-splittable compression · the small-files problem on write · persist storage levels · when caching hurts — evicting execution memory, caching something read once, caching before a filter · checkpointing vs caching and lineage truncation.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free official documentation · Title: Spark SQL data sources documentation and the Parquet format specification, read together
Why this one: both free, and the pairing is the point. Pushdown only works if the format supports it, so reading the format spec tells you what is even possible before you read what Spark attempts.
OBSERVATION · Make caching lose Cache a large DataFrame, then run a job whose shuffle needs memory. Watch execution memory evict your cached blocks, then watch the recomputation. You paid for caching and got nothing.

Then the second case: cache a DataFrame that is used exactly once. Compare against no caching. The cached version is slower — you paid the write cost for no reuse. Two experiments, and "cache it" stops being a reflex.

CONNECTS TO. S8 (cache evicts and is evicted) · L0 formats · Data Engineering N2.

S12 The UI as an instrument

Not a topic. The instrument you use for every other node.

WHAT IT IS. The Spark UI and event log as a diagnostic surface: jobs, stages, task distribution, shuffle metrics, SQL plans with runtime statistics, executor health, and GC time.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because a distributed job has no single place where the story is. The UI is where the narrative is reconstructed — and the interview question is always "how would you find that", not "what is that".

DEPENDENCIES. S2. Pick it up here and use it for everything after.

SUBTOPICS. The stage summary metrics table and reading the percentile columns · shuffle read and write, remote vs local · spill (memory) vs spill (disk) as separate signals · the SQL tab with runtime row counts against estimates · GC time as a fraction of task time · the timeline view for straggler and scheduling gaps · the event log and the history server · what is not in the UI.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free official documentation · Title: the Spark Web UI documentation page
Why this one: free, and it is the only complete reference for what each metric means. Most engineers use the UI for years without reading this page and consequently misread three or four metrics permanently.
The five-number diagnosis, to internalise as a reflex For any slow stage, read these five in this order:

1. Max vs median task duration → skew. 2. Shuffle read size → how much moved. 3. Spill (memory) and spill (disk) → did it fit. 4. GC time as a fraction of task time → memory pressure. 5. Estimated vs actual rows in the SQL tab → did the optimiser plan on a lie.

Those five numbers diagnose the large majority of slow Spark jobs, and being able to recite them in order is a strong signal in an interview.

CONNECTS TO. Everything. Also Infrastructure N5 — this is observability with a domain-specific UI.

05 Laboratory — Shuffle & Skew

The flagship lab of this branch. If you do only one, do this one.

PROBLEM. You are computing revenue per customer over a year of order events. The job is correct and unacceptably slow, and the slowness is not proportional to the data size.

INITIAL SYSTEM. A generator producing order events with a customer key, an amount and a timestamp — starting with a uniform key distribution, roughly 200 million rows, written as Parquet. A simple groupBy(customer).agg(sum(amount)) job on the standalone cluster with AQE off and small executor memory. Establish this baseline and record its stage timings. It will be unremarkable, which is the point.
FAILURE TO INTRODUCE Regenerate the data with a realistic power-law key distribution — one customer holding perhaps 8% of all rows, a handful holding 1% each, a long tail holding the remainder. Change nothing else. The job now takes several times longer, 199 tasks finish in seconds, and one runs for minutes. The data volume is identical.

CONCEPTS THAT EMERGE. In this order, each demanded by the previous observation: the shuffle write and read path → partition as the unit of skew → hash partitioning's faithfulness to the key distribution → task duration distribution as a diagnostic → salting and two-stage aggregation → AQE skew handling and its boundary → why repartition alone changes nothing.

#ExperimentWhat it isolates
1Uniform vs skewed keys, identical row countSkew is independent of volume
2Sweep shuffle.partitions across 50 / 200 / 1000 / 4000More partitions do not split a single key
3repartition(n) before the aggregationRedistributing does not fix key skew — a surprise for most people
4Salt the hot keys, aggregate twice, then combineThe actual fix, and its cost
5Sweep the salt factor 2 / 10 / 50 / 200Over-salting adds a second shuffle for no gain
6Enable AQE and re-run experiments 1 and 4What AQE fixes automatically, and what it does not
7Make one single key enormous, then enable AQEAQE splits partitions, not keys — the boundary
8Add a null-heavy key column and join on itNulls all hash to one partition — the skew nobody plants deliberately
9Kill one executor mid-shuffleFetch failure retries the whole stage, not the task
10Reduce executor memory until spill appears, then furtherWhere skew becomes OOM rather than slowness

WHAT TO OBSERVE. For every run, record: max and median task duration; max and median shuffle read bytes; spill to memory and to disk; total stage time; and the number of tasks in the tail. Plot max and median task duration against experiment number. The plot is the artifact — it shows the fixes working and the boundaries where they stop.

REAL-WORLD CONNECTION. This is the single most common production Spark incident. A celebrity product, a default customer ID used by an upstream system, a null from a failed enrichment, or a test-account key that accumulated a year of traffic. In every case the pipeline was fine until the distribution changed, which is why it fails in production and not in testing.

PORTFOLIO VALUE. High. A repository containing a tunable skew generator, a benchmark harness, a results table and a plot demonstrating each mitigation and its limit is a genuinely uncommon artifact — and it is directly demonstrable in an interview screen-share.

NEXT CONNECTED TOPICS. S7 AQE · S9 joins (skewed joins are worse than skewed aggregations) · S8 memory · L3 Kafka hot partitions.

The data structure this lab forces To find the hot keys before running, you need the top-K most frequent keys over a large dataset without sorting all of it. That is a bounded min-heap, and it is worth implementing rather than calling — because "I profiled the key distribution first" is a much stronger answer than "I noticed the job was slow", and the heap is how you do it cheaply.

06 Laboratory — Catalyst & Plan Reading

Turning the optimiser from a black box into an object you interrogate.

PROBLEM. Two queries that return identical results differ in runtime by more than an order of magnitude, and the difference is invisible in the source code.

INITIAL SYSTEM. A star schema on local mode: one large partitioned fact table in Parquet, three dimension tables of varying size, all generated. A set of analytical queries with filters on both fact and dimension columns. Statistics not collected. AQE off.
FAILURE TO INTRODUCE Write the same logical query three ways: filter before join, filter after join, and filter inside a Python UDF. Two are fast and one is catastrophically slow. Catalyst pushed the filter down in two cases and could not see through the UDF in the third — and nothing in the source code signals which is which until you read the plan.

CONCEPTS THAT EMERGE. The four plan phases → predicate pushdown and column pruning → why a UDF is an optimisation barrier → statistics and cardinality estimation → cost-based join reordering → reading estimated vs actual rows → dynamic partition pruning.

1Diff the parsed, analysed, optimised and physical plans for one queryWhat each phase actually changes

07 Laboratory — Memory & Spill

Five OOMs, five causes, five different fixes.

PROBLEM. The job dies with an out-of-memory error. The standard response — increase executor memory — sometimes works, sometimes does nothing, and sometimes makes it worse. You need to know which case you are in.

INITIAL SYSTEM. The standalone cluster with deliberately small executor memory and a modest dataset, so that every failure reproduces in under a minute. A job with a wide aggregation, a join, and an optional cache step, all parameterised so you can trigger each failure independently.
FAILURE TO INTRODUCE — five separate ones 1. collect() a large result → the driver dies. 2. Join against a slightly-too-large dimension with a raised broadcast threshold → the driver dies again, for a different reason. 3. Aggregate a skewed key → one executor dies. 4. Set shuffle.partitions very low → executors die on partition size. 5. Allocate large off-heap buffers → the container is killed while the JVM reports plenty of heap.

Five identical-looking failures with five completely different fixes.

CONCEPTS THAT EMERGE. Unified memory regions and their arithmetic → execution evicting storage → spill as designed behaviour rather than failure → the difference between JVM heap exhaustion and container overhead → why driver memory is a separate problem → GC pressure as a distinct signal.

#ExperimentWhat it isolates
1Reproduce all five failures and record the exact error textThey are distinguishable if you read them
2For each, apply the wrong fix first (raise executor memory)Which cases it does not help — the important half
3Sweep memoryFraction settings on a shuffle-heavy jobThe execution/storage boundary, moved deliberately
4Cache a large dataset, then run a memory-hungry shuffleExecution evicts storage; measure the recomputation
5Reduce memory gradually and watch spill appear before failureSpill is the warning; OOM is the wall
6Compare spill-to-memory and spill-to-disk metricsThey mean different things and are read differently
7Raise partition count on the OOMing aggregationSmaller partitions as an alternative to more memory
8Enable off-heap and repeat the container-kill caseOverhead memory versus heap
9Track GC time as a fraction of task time across all runsThe silent tax before the visible failure

WHAT TO OBSERVE. The exact exception and which process emitted it; peak execution and storage memory per executor; spill volumes; GC time fraction; and whether the container or the JVM was the thing that died. Build a one-page decision table — symptom to cause to fix. That table is the artifact, and it is the answer to a very common interview question.

REAL-WORLD CONNECTION. The 3am page. The genuinely valuable outcome here is the reflex of reading the error and asking which process died and why before touching any configuration — because raising memory is the response that masks four of the five causes without fixing them.

PORTFOLIO VALUE. High for interviews, moderate as a repository. The decision table is the part worth publishing.

08 Laboratory — Join Strategy

The optimiser's most consequential decision, made observable and overridable.

PROBLEM. A join between a large fact table and a medium dimension is slow. Someone suggests raising the broadcast threshold. It works — until the dimension grows slightly and the driver dies.

INITIAL SYSTEM. A fact table of a few hundred million rows and dimension tables at several sizes spanning the broadcast threshold — well below, just below, just above, far above. Statistics uncollected initially. AQE off.
FAILURE TO INTRODUCE Raise autoBroadcastJoinThreshold so the medium dimension broadcasts. It is dramatically faster. Now grow that dimension by 20%. The driver OOMs — because the threshold is compared against an estimate, the estimate was stale, and a broadcast collects to the driver before distributing. The optimisation that worked yesterday is the outage today, and nothing in the code changed.

CONCEPTS THAT EMERGE. The four join strategies and their cost shapes → how JoinSelection decides → estimates versus reality → why broadcast is driver-mediated → sort-merge's sort cost → bucketing as pre-shuffled data → AQE runtime strategy switching → hints and when overriding is correct.

#ExperimentWhat it isolates
1Join against each dimension size; record the chosen strategy and runtimeThe cost curve of each strategy
2Force each strategy with a hint on the same pairWhat the optimiser avoided, and why
3Grow the dimension past the threshold with stale statisticsThe estimate is the decision input, not the truth
4Bucket both sides on the join key, then re-runA join with no shuffle at all
5Bucket with mismatched bucket countsWhen bucketing silently stops working
6Enable AQE and repeat experiment 3Runtime strategy switching, observed in the plan
7Join on a skewed key, with and without AQE skew handlingSkewed joins are worse than skewed aggregations
8Left outer join on the same pairWhich strategies remain available and why
9Join on a column with many nullsAll nulls to one partition; the fix is to filter or handle them separately
10Three-way join, vary the order manuallyReordering, and its dependence on statistics

WHAT TO OBSERVE. The chosen physical operator in the plan; shuffle bytes for each strategy; driver memory during broadcast; sort time within sort-merge; and estimated versus actual build-side size. Produce a chart of runtime against dimension size, one line per strategy. The crossover points on that chart are the answer to "when would you broadcast?" — expressed as a measured number rather than a rule of thumb.

REAL-WORLD CONNECTION. Broadcast threshold tuning is one of the most commonly copied settings in production and one of the least understood. The failure mode — works fine until a dimension grows — is a real and recurring incident class, and bucketing is the underused technique that removes the problem rather than tuning around it.

PORTFOLIO VALUE. High. The runtime-vs-size chart with strategy crossovers is a genuinely striking artifact and directly answers a standard interview question with your own measurements.
