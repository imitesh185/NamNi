Economics

Economics is the study of how people and societies allocate things that are insufficient for everyone's wants. Its central and most transferable claim is that outcomes are produced by incentives and constraints rather than by intentions — which makes it, structurally, systems thinking applied to human beings.

The single idea. A price is not a number, it is a message. It compresses the dispersed knowledge of millions of people — what is scarce, what is wanted, what is costly to make — into a single figure that coordinates strangers who will never meet. Almost every debate in this domain is about when that message is accurate and what to do when it is not.

00 Why this domain exists, and how to avoid the ideology trap

This is the first domain in the Atlas where the experts genuinely disagree, and that must be handled honestly.

In the computing cluster, disagreement usually means someone is wrong. Here it frequently means the question is genuinely open, or is partly a values question wearing a technical costume. The commander-level skill is to separate the mechanism from the value judgment — to say "this policy will raise prices and reduce shortages; whether that is good depends on who you weight" rather than picking a team.

The failure mode of this domain Adopting a school before understanding the mechanisms. If you can state a policy's effect only in terms of whether you approve of it, you have learned a position rather than a subject. The test of understanding here is being able to construct the strongest version of an argument you disagree with.

N1 Incentives and the margin

Not a topic — the reasoning method the rest of the domain uses.

WHAT IT IS. Three habits: recognising that every choice has a foregone alternative, reasoning about the next unit rather than the total, and predicting behaviour from incentives rather than from stated intentions.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because "should we do more of this?" is almost never answerable from averages. The relevant question is what the next rupee, hour or unit buys, and that is usually different from what the last one bought.

DEPENDENCIES. Systems Thinking N2 (incentives create loops).

SUBTOPICS. Scarcity and trade-offs · opportunity cost including foregone time · sunk cost · marginal cost and benefit · diminishing returns · incentives and moral hazard · unintended consequences · the cobra effect.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free online textbook · Title: CORE Econ, The Economy, units 1–4 — free in full at core-econ.org
Why this one: free, and it is the best modern introductory treatment: it starts from real empirical data and real institutions rather than from idealised markets, and it does not hide the disagreements. It is also written for people who are not going to become economists.

DEEPER (optional) — Marginal Revolution University's free video courses for a different perspective on the same material.
HANDS-ON · Find a policy that produced the opposite of its purpose Choose a real Indian policy with a well-documented perverse outcome and reconstruct the incentive chain yourself before reading any analysis. Good candidates: free electricity for agricultural pumps and groundwater depletion; minimum support prices and the crop mix in Punjab; rent control and housing supply in Mumbai; the plastic ban and what replaced it.

For each, write the chain explicitly: the rule → the incentive it creates for an individual acting reasonably → the aggregate consequence → the second-order response. Then check your chain against published analysis.

The groundwater one is the most instructive. Free power makes the marginal cost of pumping water approximately zero. A farmer facing zero marginal cost pumps until the marginal benefit is zero. Every farmer does the same. The water table falls, so pumps must go deeper, so more power is used — and the policy intended to help farmers has made farming more precarious. Nobody behaved irrationally and nobody intended the outcome.

Then find the leverage point using Systems Thinking N6: it is almost never "tell people to use less".

The realisation to reach: policies do not have effects, incentives do — and the incentive is what an individual faces at the margin, not what the policy document says it intends.

CONNECTS TO. Systems Thinking N5 (tragedy of the commons) · Human Behavior · Indian Politics · Business (incentive design inside firms).

N2 Price as information

The mechanism by which millions of strangers coordinate without a plan.

WHAT IT IS. Supply and demand as a model of how price emerges, how it clears a market, and how it transmits information about scarcity to people who have no idea why it changed.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because no central authority can gather the information needed to allocate an economy — it is dispersed across millions of people and much of it is tacit. Prices aggregate that information automatically, which is why command economies could not price things and slowly misallocated everything.

DEPENDENCIES. N1.

SUBTOPICS. Demand and supply curves · equilibrium · elasticity and why it determines who bears a tax · shifts versus movements · price ceilings and floors · shortages, queues and black markets · the knowledge problem · speculation and its stabilising role.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free online textbook · Title: CORE Econ, The Economy, units 7–8 and 11
Why this one: free, and it treats markets as institutions with specific conditions rather than as a default state of nature — which makes both their power and their failures easier to see.

DEEPER (optional) — Hayek, "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945), free online. Six pages, and the clearest statement of why prices carry information.
HANDS-ON · Trace an onion price spike from field to headline Indian onion and tomato prices spike periodically and dramatically, and every spike is a complete economics lesson. Pick one — 2019–20 onions, or a recent tomato episode — and reconstruct it from free data: Agmarknet publishes daily mandi prices, and the news archives give the timeline.

Build the chain: what happened physically (unseasonal rain in Maharashtra or Karnataka), why the supply curve moved so much for a small crop loss (perishability, no storage, concentrated growing regions), why the demand curve barely moved (onions are a staple with few substitutes — inelastic demand), and therefore why a modest supply shock produced an enormous price move.

Inelastic demand plus inelastic short-run supply equals violent prices. This one insight explains onions, oil, electricity, semiconductors during shortages, and housing in Mumbai.

Then evaluate the policy responses that actually occurred — export bans, stock limits, imports — and trace each one's second-order effect on farmers' planting decisions for the following season. The export ban that protects consumers this year reduces the price farmers expect next year, so less is planted, so next year is worse.

The realisation to reach: the price spike is not the problem, it is the signal that something physical went wrong — and suppressing the signal without addressing the physical cause moves the shortage rather than removing it.

CONNECTS TO. Indian Geography (monsoon, crop regions) · Systems Thinking N3 (planting delay causes the cycle) · Geopolitics (commodity shocks) · Distributed Systems (prices as a gossip protocol — a genuine structural isomorphism).

N3 Market failure

The four conditions under which the price message is systematically wrong.

WHAT IT IS. Situations where voluntary exchange does not produce an efficient outcome: externalities, public goods and commons, information asymmetry, and market power.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because the price mechanism only carries information about costs and benefits that fall on the transacting parties. Anything falling on third parties, or hidden from one party, is simply absent from the message.

DEPENDENCIES. N2.

SUBTOPICS. Externalities, positive and negative · Pigouvian taxes and subsidies · public goods and free riding · the tragedy of the commons and Ostrom's counter-examples · adverse selection and the market for lemons · moral hazard · monopoly, monopsony and network effects · government failure and regulatory capture.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free online textbook · Title: CORE Econ, units 12 and 20 (market failure and the commons)
Why this one: free, and it includes Ostrom's empirical work showing communities successfully managing commons without either privatisation or state control — which is the part usually omitted and which makes the topic far less ideological.

DEEPER (optional) — Akerlof, "The Market for Lemons" (1970), free. It connects directly to your existing game theory note.
HANDS-ON · Price an externality you personally create Choose something you do that imposes a cost on people who did not agree to it: a car or bike commute, an air-conditioner, food delivery packaging, a cloud workload's carbon.

Now put a number on it. Estimate the physical quantity from free data — fuel consumed, kilowatt-hours, kilograms of plastic — then find a published social cost per unit. Compute what you would pay if that cost were included in your price. Then ask the uncomfortable follow-up: would you change your behaviour at that price? That answer is the elasticity that determines whether a carbon tax works.

Then find an information asymmetry in a market you use. India's used two-wheeler market is the textbook case: sellers know the vehicle's history, buyers do not, so buyers discount every vehicle, so owners of genuinely good vehicles withdraw, so average quality falls, so buyers discount further. Reconstruct that spiral numerically and watch a market unravel from information alone. Then identify what actually solves it in practice — warranties, certification, reputation — and note that each is a costly signal, connecting straight to your game theory note.

The realisation to reach: markets fail in specific, identifiable ways, not generally. Naming which failure is present tells you which intervention is even relevant, and most policy argument skips that step entirely.

CONNECTS TO. Game Theory (signalling, Akerlof, Spence) · Systems Thinking N5 (commons) · Indian Politics (regulation) · Geopolitics (global commons) · AI (data as a public good).

N4 The aggregates

How you measure an entire economy — and what every measure leaves out.

WHAT IT IS. GDP, inflation, unemployment and the other national statistics: what they count, how they are constructed, and what they systematically miss.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because policy needs numbers. But every aggregate is a choice about what to count, and in India these choices are particularly consequential because so much activity is informal and therefore hard to observe.

DEPENDENCIES. Mathematics N6 · Data Engineering N5 (this is measurement design, with the same grain and definition problems).

SUBTOPICS. GDP and its three equivalent measurements · what GDP omits — unpaid work, informal activity, depletion, distribution · nominal versus real · price indices and CPI construction · the substitution and quality problems · inflation versus relative price change · unemployment definitions and why they understate in India · informality.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free primary document · Title: the Economic Survey of India, published free by the Ministry of Finance each year — read one chapter, not the whole thing
Why this one: free, official, and it shows the aggregates being used to argue rather than presented as neutral facts. Reading the government's own analysis of its own data teaches both the statistics and how they get deployed.

DEEPER (optional) — Our World in Data (free) for cross-country comparison with sources attached.
HANDS-ON · Compute your own inflation rate and disagree with the official one List the twenty things you actually spend money on, with quantities. Find their prices now and five years ago. Compute your personal weighted inflation rate.

Compare it to official CPI for the same period. It will differ, sometimes substantially, and the reason is instructive: the official basket is not your basket. If you spend heavily on rent, education or healthcare and lightly on food, your experienced inflation diverges sharply from the headline — which is why people frequently insist inflation is higher than reported and are, for themselves, correct.

Then take your own salary over five years and convert it to real terms using both your personal rate and the official one. The two answers to "did I get richer?" can have opposite signs.

Finally, examine what GDP misses by trying to count it: estimate the annual value of unpaid domestic work in your own household at market rates. It is a large number, it is real economic activity, and it is in no national statistic. Then ask what it means that a country's measured growth can rise simply because work moved from homes into markets.

The realisation to reach: aggregates are constructed objects with a defined grain and scope. Knowing what is inside the boundary is the difference between using a statistic and being used by one.

CONNECTS TO. Data Engineering N5 (grain and definitions) · Systems Thinking N7 (boundaries determine conclusions) · Indian Politics (data as a political object) · Mathematics N6.

N5 Money and central banking

Where most of the mystery is, and where almost none of it survives contact with the mechanism.

WHAT IT IS. What money is, how commercial banks create most of it by lending, and how a central bank influences the economy by changing the price of short-term credit.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because an economy needs a medium of exchange whose quantity is neither fixed nor arbitrary. The institutional answer — private banks creating credit, a central bank steering its price — is strange, historically contingent, and worth understanding mechanically because it drives asset prices, employment and the value of your savings.

DEPENDENCIES. N2, N4 · Systems Thinking N3 (transmission has long delays).

SUBTOPICS. Functions of money · commercial bank money creation through lending · the central bank balance sheet · the policy rate and the corridor · monetary transmission and its lags · inflation targeting · quantitative easing · the fiscal deficit and its financing · currency management and the impossible trinity.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free primary documents · Title: the RBI's Monetary Policy Committee resolution and Governor's statement, published free after every meeting
Why this one: free, short, and it is the actual decision with the actual reasoning. Reading four consecutive ones teaches you what the committee is watching, how it weighs conflicting signals, and how it talks about uncertainty — none of which a textbook conveys.

DEEPER (optional) — the Bank of England's "Money creation in the modern economy" (free), which is the clearest official statement that banks create deposits by lending rather than lending out deposits.
HANDS-ON · Follow one rate decision all the way to a real price Take one RBI repo rate change. Read the actual MPC resolution and note the stated reasoning and the vote split — dissents are published, and they tell you where the genuine uncertainty was.

Now trace the transmission empirically, using free data at each step. Did bank lending rates move, and by how much, and after how long? Did deposit rates move by the same amount or less? Did credit growth change? Did housing or vehicle sales respond? Did inflation respond, and when?

You will find the chain is leaky and slow — transmission is partial, delayed by quarters, and much weaker for some borrowers than others. This is Systems Thinking N3 at national scale: a balancing loop with a long delay, which is exactly why central banks overshoot and why they talk so carefully.

Then do the money-creation exercise. Trace a single loan: a bank grants ₹10 lakh and simultaneously creates a ₹10 lakh deposit. No existing money moved. Follow it through the balance sheet using double entry and confirm both sides. Most money in the economy was created this way, by private banks, as a by-product of lending decisions.

The realisation to reach: money is credit, credit is created by lending, and the central bank influences rather than controls it — through a mechanism with delays long enough that the effects of today's decision will be judged in a world nobody can currently see.

CONNECTS TO. Systems Thinking N2–N3 (a delayed balancing loop) · Finance (rates price everything) · Geopolitics (reserve currencies, the impossible trinity) · Indian History (1991 and the BoP crisis).

N6 Trade

Why countries exchange, and who actually pays a tariff.

WHAT IT IS. The theory and accounting of exchange across borders: comparative advantage, the balance of payments, exchange rates, and trade policy.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because comparative advantage is genuinely counter-intuitive — a country can benefit from trading even when it is worse at producing everything — and because the distributional consequences are real, concentrated and politically decisive.

DEPENDENCIES. N1, N2, N4.

SUBTOPICS. Absolute versus comparative advantage · gains from trade and their distribution · the balance of payments identity · current and capital accounts · exchange rate determination · real effective exchange rate · tariffs and incidence · non-tariff barriers · global value chains and why "made in" has stopped meaning much.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free online textbook · Title: CORE Econ, The Economy, unit 18
Why this one: free, and it treats the distribution of gains as central rather than as a footnote — which is the only way to understand why trade is politically contested despite the aggregate argument being strong.
HANDS-ON · Find out who paid the tariff Pick a real Indian tariff change — electronics, solar panels, edible oil, steel. Before looking at any analysis, predict who bears the cost: the foreign exporter, the domestic importer, the domestic consumer, or the domestic producer of the substitute.

Then check with data. Did import prices fall (exporter absorbed it) or did domestic retail prices rise (consumer paid)? Did domestic production actually increase, and how much later? Did downstream industries using that input become less competitive?

Incidence — who actually pays — is determined by elasticity, not by who writes the cheque, which is the same result as N2 and one of the most reliably counter-intuitive findings in economics.

Then take one physical object you own — a phone is ideal — and trace its supply chain across countries: design, chip fabrication, assembly, software, distribution. Assign a rough share of value to each stage. You will find assembly, the stage a "made in" label refers to, captures a small fraction of the value. That single observation reframes most trade-deficit rhetoric.

The realisation to reach: trade statistics count gross flows while value is distributed unevenly along a chain, so trade balances measure something quite different from what they are usually claimed to measure.

CONNECTS TO. Geopolitics (chokepoints, export controls, semiconductors) · World Geography (routes and ports) · Business (global supply chains) · Indian History (1991 liberalisation).

N7 Growth and development

The most consequential question in the domain, and the one with the least settled answer.

WHAT IT IS. Why output per person rises over time, why it differs so enormously between countries, and what if anything policy can do about it.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because the difference between a country where a life is short and constrained and one where it is long and optioned is almost entirely this. It is also where economics is most honest about not knowing — growth theory explains the mechanics but not why institutions differ.

DEPENDENCIES. N1–N6.

SUBTOPICS. Capital, labour and total factor productivity · why productivity is the residual and what that admits · human capital · institutions and property rights · the structural transformation from agriculture to industry to services · why India's path skipped a stage · convergence and its absence · the middle-income trap · state capacity.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free data platform · Title: Our World in Data's economic growth and development sections, used to construct comparisons yourself
Why this one: free, sources every chart, and lets you build the comparison rather than read someone's conclusion about it. For this node, constructing the comparison is the learning.

DEEPER (optional) — CORE Econ units 1, 2 and 21, and the free working papers of the Reserve Bank and NITI Aayog on state-level variation.
HANDS-ON · Two countries, one starting point, two outcomes Pick two countries that were at similar income levels in 1960 and are now far apart: India and South Korea is the canonical pair, but India and China, or Kerala and Bihar within India, work as well and are more immediate.

Using free data, plot GDP per capita, literacy, life expectancy, urbanisation, electricity access and manufacturing share of employment across sixty years. Find the years where the paths diverge, then find what happened in those specific years.

Now construct the causal story yourself, and then attack it. For every explanation you propose — education, industrial policy, institutions, geography, external demand — find a country that had the same thing and did not grow. Almost every single-factor explanation of growth dies to a counter-example, and discovering that yourself is more valuable than any answer.

Then look at India specifically: services grew before manufacturing did, which is historically unusual and has consequences for employment, because services absorb fewer low-skilled workers per rupee of output. Trace that to the current employment debate.

The realisation to reach: this is the domain's honest edge. The mechanics of growth are understood; the reasons some societies build the institutions that produce it are not, and anyone claiming otherwise is selling something.

CONNECTS TO. Indian History (1991, the licence system, colonial extraction) · Indian Geography (agriculture, urbanisation) · Geopolitics (development as power) · Indian Politics (state capacity) · World History (industrial revolution).
