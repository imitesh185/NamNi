Business

A business is a machine that converts inputs into something people will pay more for than it cost. That is the entire subject. Everything below is either about whether the machine works per unit, or about whether someone else can build the same machine next to you.

The single idea. Creating value and capturing value are different things, and most business failure is being good at the first and bad at the second. Airlines create enormous value and capture almost none of it; a toll bridge creates modest value and captures nearly all of it. The difference is structural, not managerial.

00 Why this domain exists, and why it is fast for you

The cheapest large gain available to an engineer.

Most engineers know nothing here, so the distance from zero to genuinely useful is short — and the combination is rare enough to be disproportionately valuable. You already have the two hardest inputs: the ability to read statements and the ability to compute what infrastructure costs. Those two together are most of what business analysis actually requires.

N1 Business models

Where the money comes from, and where it goes on its way to you.

WHAT IT IS. The structure by which a business creates something valuable, delivers it, and retains a share of the value created.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because the same product can be sold in structurally different ways with completely different economics. Selling software as a licence, a subscription, a service, or free with advertising produces four different companies from one artefact.

DEPENDENCIES. Accounting N3 · Economics N2.

SUBTOPICS. The value chain and where margin pools sit · who the customer is versus who the user is · archetypes: manufacturing, retail, subscription, marketplace, advertising, licensing, services · value creation versus value capture · commoditisation of complements.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free primary document · Title: a Draft Red Herring Prospectus for an Indian company whose product you use — free on SEBI's website. Read the "Our Business" and "Industry Overview" sections
Why this one: free, and the DRHP describes the business model in the company's own words while being legally liable for accuracy. It is far more honest than any case study, because misstatement has consequences.
HANDS-ON · Follow ₹100 through a business you used this week Take a transaction you personally made — a food delivery order, a cab ride, a phone recharge, a purchase from a large retailer. Now trace where the money went, using free public filings.

For a ₹400 food delivery order: how much went to the restaurant, the rider, payment processing, the platform's gross take, and what was left after the discount the platform funded? The published financials give you enough to construct this within a reasonable margin.

You will discover that the platform's take rate and its contribution margin are completely different numbers, and that the gap is where the business lives or dies. Then ask the structural question: who in this chain has the power to raise their share, and why? The restaurant with a queue outside has options; the rider does not; the platform's leverage depends entirely on whether the customer would switch.

Then do the same for a business with the opposite shape — a cement company or a paint manufacturer — and notice that the questions change completely, because the constraint moved from customer acquisition to plant utilisation.

The realisation to reach: the money is always somewhere in the chain, and the interesting question is never how much value was created but which party was structurally able to keep it.

CONNECTS TO. Economics N3 (market power) · Geopolitics (value chains) · Systems Thinking N6 (structural position is a leverage point).

N2 Unit economics depth

The single most useful thing in this domain, and the fastest to learn.

WHAT IT IS. The economics of one customer or one unit, isolated from the company's overall size — what it costs to acquire, what it contributes, and how long that takes to pay back.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because a company losing money at scale is either investing or dying, and only the unit level distinguishes them. If one unit is profitable, growth is an investment; if it is not, growth is a faster route to zero.

DEPENDENCIES. Accounting N3–N4 · Finance N1.

SUBTOPICS. Contribution margin and what belongs in it · customer acquisition cost, fully loaded · lifetime value and its dependence on retention · payback period · the LTV/CAC ratio and its abuse · cohort analysis · net revenue retention · fixed versus variable cost and operating leverage · why blended CAC misleads.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free primary documents · Title: the cohort and unit-economics disclosures in Indian consumer-tech DRHPs and quarterly investor presentations — free
Why this one: free, and Indian consumer internet companies are now required to disclose cohort behaviour in unusual detail. These documents are effectively a free advanced course in unit economics using real numbers.
HANDS-ON · Build the model that shows growth killing a company Build a unit economics model for a subscription business: acquisition cost ₹1,000, monthly revenue ₹200, monthly variable cost ₹80, monthly churn 5%.

Compute contribution margin per month, average customer lifetime, lifetime value, and payback period. Then change churn to 8% and recompute. Lifetime value collapses far more than 3% suggests, because lifetime is one divided by churn — a nonlinear relationship. Plot LTV against churn and see the curve.

Now add the cash dimension from Accounting N4: you pay the ₹1,000 acquisition cost today and recover it over months. Model growth at 20% per month and plot the cash balance. It goes deeply negative. Every profitable-per-unit, fast-growing subscription business is a cash-consuming machine, and that is normal rather than a warning sign — provided payback is short enough. Find the payback period at which the business becomes self-funding.

Then find the trap: use blended CAC when half your customers arrive organically, and watch the number flatter you. Split it into paid and organic and recompute. The paid channel is usually far worse than the blended figure implies, and this is the most common way unit economics are misrepresented — often sincerely.

Finally, take a real company's disclosed cohorts and compute whether each cohort spends more or less over time. Rising cohort revenue means the business compounds without new customers, which is the single strongest signal in this domain.

The realisation to reach: "we lose money on every order but we will make it up in volume" is sometimes correct and sometimes fatal, and the unit model is the only thing that tells you which.

CONNECTS TO. Accounting N4 (cash timing) · Infrastructure N7 (your COGS is infrastructure) · Finance N1 (payback is a discounting question) · Probability N4 (customer value is heavy-tailed).

N3 Moats

Why profit persists — or, far more often, why it does not.

WHAT IT IS. Structural characteristics that make a business's advantage durable against competitors who can see exactly what it is doing.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because in the absence of a barrier, high returns attract entrants until returns fall to the cost of capital. Competition is the default; persistent profit is the anomaly that requires explanation.

DEPENDENCIES. N1 · Economics N3 (market power).

SUBTOPICS. Supply-side scale economies · demand-side network effects, and their limits · switching costs · brand as a trust shortcut · counter-positioning · cornered resources · process power · what is not a moat — being first, working harder, better technology alone.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free primary documents · Title: the "Risk Factors" and "Competition" sections of DRHPs, read across three competitors in one industry
Why this one: free, and the risk factors section is where a company is legally obliged to describe what could destroy it. Reading three competitors' risk sections side by side shows you exactly which of them has a structural advantage and which is merely currently ahead.
HANDS-ON · Test a claimed moat by trying to break it Pick three businesses you use and write down what you believe protects each. Then attack each claim: if I had ₹500 crore and no constraints, could I replicate this in two years?

Most claimed moats dissolve under this test. "Better product" usually does. "Great team" always does. What survives tends to be unglamorous: a distribution network that took thirty years to build, a regulatory licence, a switching cost embedded in someone else's workflow, or a scale advantage in a business with high fixed costs.

Then examine network effects carefully, because they are the most over-claimed. For each supposed network business, ask whether the network is global or local. A food delivery network is local — being large in Mumbai does nothing for you in Coimbatore, which is why these markets support several players. A payment network is global. Local network effects produce competitive markets; global ones produce monopolies, and conflating them is the most expensive analytical error in consumer tech.

Finally, look for margin evidence rather than narrative: find a company with sustained high returns on capital over ten years and work backwards to what allowed it. The financial record is a more reliable witness than any strategy document.

The realisation to reach: most businesses have no moat, and most of what is called strategy is operating well in a competitive market — which is respectable, difficult, and completely different from being protected.

CONNECTS TO. Economics N3 · Game Theory (commitment and entry deterrence) · Geopolitics (chokepoints are national moats) · Distributed Systems (network effects are a reinforcing loop).

N4 Pricing

The highest-leverage number in a business, and the least analysed.

WHAT IT IS. Deciding what to charge — based on what a customer is willing to pay rather than on what the thing cost to make.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because price flows directly to profit. A 1% price increase, if volume holds, improves operating profit far more than a 1% cost reduction — often by a multiple. It is also the decision most often made by copying a competitor.

DEPENDENCIES. N2 · Economics N2 (elasticity).

SUBTOPICS. Cost-plus versus value-based pricing · willingness to pay and its distribution · segmentation and price discrimination · versioning, bundling, unbundling · anchoring and decoy pricing · freemium · usage-based versus seat-based · price as a quality signal.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free primary sources · Title: the published price lists of three competitors in one category, reverse-engineered
Why this one: free and immediate. Pricing pages are strategy documents in public — the tiers, the fences between them, and what is deliberately withheld from the lower tier tell you exactly how the company has segmented its customers.
HANDS-ON · Compute what a 1% price rise is actually worth Take a real company's income statement. Increase revenue by 1% with costs unchanged, and compute the percentage change in operating profit. For a business with a 10% operating margin, a 1% price increase raises profit by 10%. Now do a 1% cost reduction and compare. Price wins, usually by a large multiple.

Then design a three-tier structure for something you could sell, and — this is the actual exercise — decide what to deliberately withhold from the cheaper tier. The fence between tiers is the entire design. It must be something the high-willingness-to-pay segment needs and the low one does not, and choosing it wrongly means either cannibalising your own revenue or losing the low tier entirely.

Then examine cloud pricing with your Infrastructure N7 work: why is inbound data free and outbound charged? That is not a cost structure, it is a deliberate fence designed to make data accumulate where it lands — pricing as strategy, visible in public, affecting an entire industry's architecture.

The realisation to reach: price is not derived from cost. Cost sets a floor below which you should not sell; everything above that floor is a decision about who your customer is.

CONNECTS TO. Economics N2 (elasticity), N3 (price discrimination requires market power) · Psychology (anchoring, reference prices) · Infrastructure N7 (cloud pricing as incentive design).

N5 Distribution

The most underrated node in the domain — and usually the real moat.

WHAT IT IS. How a product reaches customers: channels, sales motions, intermediaries and platforms, along with the economics of each.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because a product nobody can reach has no value, and because distribution is frequently harder to replicate than the product itself. Engineers systematically underrate this, which is why technically superior products routinely lose.

DEPENDENCIES. N1, N2, N3.

SUBTOPICS. Direct versus channel · the cost per channel · enterprise sales motions versus product-led growth · marketplaces and aggregation · owning the customer relationship versus owning the supply · India's distribution reality — kirana density, tier 2 and 3 economics, cash on delivery, logistics cost per kilometre.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free primary document · Title: the distribution and supply-chain sections of an Indian FMCG company's annual report — free
Why this one: free, and Indian FMCG distribution is one of the most impressive and least discussed systems in the country. Reading how a company reaches millions of outlets, and what it costs, permanently changes how you assess any consumer business.
HANDS-ON · Find the product that lost despite being better Identify two cases where the technically superior product lost to the better-distributed one. There are many, and reconstructing one honestly is more instructive than any framework.

Then do the Indian version, which is more concrete: compare how a large FMCG company and a direct-to-consumer brand each reach a customer in a tier-3 town. Count the intermediaries, estimate the margin at each stage, and compute the delivered cost per unit. The incumbent's decades-old network is expensive per rupee of revenue and impossible to replicate quickly, which is precisely why it is a moat.

Then examine aggregation: for a marketplace you use, work out who owns the customer relationship. If the customer thinks of the platform rather than the supplier, the platform can raise its take rate and the supplier cannot leave. Whoever owns demand eventually captures the margin, and this dynamic explains most platform-versus-supplier conflict.

The realisation to reach: distribution is not marketing. It is infrastructure, it takes years and capital to build, and it is the reason many excellent products never reach anyone.

CONNECTS TO. Indian Geography (logistics, corridors, tier 2 and 3 markets) · Economics N3 (aggregation creates market power) · Systems Thinking N2 (a reinforcing loop between demand and supply).

N6 Organisation

The machine that runs the machine — and why it shapes the product.

WHAT IT IS. How firms are structured, how incentives inside them drive behaviour, and why organisations exist rather than everything being contracted in a market.

WHY IT EXISTS. Coase's question: if markets are efficient, why do firms exist at all? The answer — transaction costs — explains outsourcing, vertical integration, and why the boundary of a company moves as coordination costs change.

DEPENDENCIES. N1 · Economics N1 (incentives) · Human Behavior.

SUBTOPICS. Transaction costs and the theory of the firm · make versus buy · vertical integration · principal-agent problems inside firms · incentive design and Goodhart's law · Conway's law · span of control and coordination cost · why reorganisations rarely fix what they target.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free paper · Title: Coase, "The Nature of the Firm" (1937), freely available
Why this one: free, short, and it asks a question so basic that nobody had asked it, then answers it in a way that still explains outsourcing, gig work and platform companies ninety years later.

DEEPER (optional) — Parnas (1972) and Conway (1968), both free, read together. Conway explains why your architecture mirrors your org chart; Parnas explains what to do about it.
HANDS-ON · Find Conway's law in a system you know Take a codebase or product you know well and draw its architecture. Separately, draw the org chart of the team that built it. Overlay them.

The module boundaries will follow the team boundaries with uncomfortable fidelity. The awkward integration is between two teams that report to different managers. The service nobody owns is the one whose team was reorganised. Conway's law is not a tendency, it is close to an identity, and seeing it in your own system is more convincing than any restatement.

Then find a Goodhart failure: identify a metric your organisation optimises and work out how it could be satisfied without achieving the underlying goal. Then check whether that is happening. When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure — and this is the same failure as optimising the wrong loss function, with humans as the optimiser.

Finally, apply Coase directly: pick something your company builds internally and something it buys, and articulate why the boundary sits there. Then ask what would have to change for it to move. This is the actual reasoning behind outsourcing, cloud adoption, and every build-versus-buy argument you will ever sit in.

The realisation to reach: the organisation is not the container for the work — it is a constraint on what work is possible, and it will reproduce itself in whatever you build.

CONNECTS TO. Software Engineering N9 (Conway's law) · AI N7 (Goodhart and loss functions) · Human Behavior (incentives, principal-agent) · Systems Thinking N6 (structure over exhortation).
