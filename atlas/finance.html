Finance

Finance is the study of moving value across time and redistributing risk. Every instrument, however elaborate, is some combination of those two operations — and once you see that, the vocabulary stops being intimidating and becomes a taxonomy.

The single idea. A rupee today is not a rupee next year, and a certain rupee is not an uncertain one. The first fact gives you discounting; the second gives you risk premia. Everything else — bonds, equities, options, insurance, valuation, crises — is built from those two adjustments.

00 Why this domain exists

And the honest warning about it.

Finance exists because savings and opportunities sit with different people, and because risk is cheaper when spread. It is genuinely useful. It is also the domain in this Atlas most surrounded by people who profit from your confusion, which makes the mechanism unusually worth owning.

N1 Time value of money

One equation that underlies every other node.

WHAT IT IS. The observation that money available now is worth more than the same amount later, and the arithmetic — compounding forward, discounting backward — that makes cash flows at different times comparable.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because every financial decision compares amounts arriving at different times, and they cannot be compared until they are moved to a common date. The discount rate that performs the move is where all the judgment hides.

DEPENDENCIES. Mathematics N2 (exponentials).

SUBTOPICS. Compound interest · present and future value · net present value · internal rate of return and its failure modes · annuities and perpetuities · real versus nominal rates · the rule of 72 · why the discount rate is an assumption and not a fact.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free course materials · Title: Aswath Damodaran's corporate finance and valuation classes — full lecture videos, slides, spreadsheets and datasets, all free at pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar
Why this one: free, comprehensive, and he publishes his actual working spreadsheets and data. He is also unusually honest about how much of valuation is assumption, which is the single most important thing to learn here.
HANDS-ON · Discover that most of a valuation is a guess about year 30 Build a ten-year discounted cash flow in a spreadsheet: project cash flows, add a terminal value, and discount everything at 10%. Note the answer.

Now change the discount rate to 9%, then 11%. The valuation swings enormously for a one-point change. Then change the terminal growth rate by half a point and watch it swing again.

Now compute what fraction of your total value comes from the terminal value — the lump sum representing everything after year ten. For a typical growth company it is 60–80%. Most of what you just "calculated" is a guess about a period you did not model, discounted by a rate you chose.

Then do the personal version, which is more useful than it sounds. Compute what ₹10,000 invested monthly becomes over 10, 20 and 30 years at 8%, 10% and 12%. The 30-year numbers are startling relative to the 20-year ones — that is the second half of the compounding curve doing nearly all the work, and it is the strongest argument in this Atlas for starting early rather than optimising rates.

The realisation to reach: a DCF is not a measurement. It is your assumptions, made arithmetic — which is why two competent analysts produce values differing by a factor of three and both are being honest.

CONNECTS TO. Mathematics N2 · Economics N5 (the rate comes from monetary policy) · Systems Thinking N2 (compounding is a reinforcing loop) · your own career decisions.

N2 Risk and return

Where Probability N4 becomes money.

WHAT IT IS. The relationship between uncertainty and expected compensation, the mathematics of diversification, and the distinction between risk you are paid to bear and risk you are not.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because investors dislike uncertainty, so uncertain claims must be cheaper to attract buyers. That discount, expressed as a higher expected return, is the risk premium — and understanding what generates it separates investing from gambling.

DEPENDENCIES. N1 · Probability N3–N4, N7.

SUBTOPICS. Volatility as a proxy for risk and its inadequacy · correlation and diversification · systematic versus idiosyncratic risk · why only systematic risk is compensated · the risk-free rate · equity risk premium · drawdown and time to recovery · sequence-of-returns risk.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free data + free lectures · Title: Damodaran's free historical return datasets, used alongside NSE's free historical index data
Why this one: free, and this node only becomes real when computed on actual long-run returns rather than on textbook examples with invented numbers.
HANDS-ON · The average return nobody actually received Get twenty years of Nifty index data, free from NSE. Compute the arithmetic mean annual return. Now compute the compound annual growth rate actually achieved over the period.

The CAGR is lower than the arithmetic mean, always. Volatility itself destroys compounded returns — a 50% loss requires a 100% gain to recover. Quantify the gap for your data. This is the same asymmetry as Probability N7's ruin problem, in a form that affects every saver.

Then run the sequence experiment. Take the same set of annual returns and shuffle their order. For a lump sum invested once, the final value is identical regardless of order. Now do it for someone investing monthly, and again for a retiree withdrawing monthly. Order now matters enormously — the retiree who meets a crash in year one and the one who meets it in year twenty have completely different outcomes from identical returns.

Finally, test diversification honestly: compute correlations between Indian equities, gold, and a bond index during normal periods and then specifically during the worst months. Correlations rise in crises — diversification weakens precisely when it is needed.

The realisation to reach: the average return is not the experienced return, and volatility is not merely discomfort — it is a direct subtraction from what you actually end up with.

CONNECTS TO. Probability N4, N7 · Systems Thinking N4 (nonlinearity) · Psychology (loss aversion) · Business (risk-adjusted decisions).

N3 Instruments

A taxonomy, not a vocabulary test. Every instrument is a claim on cash flows with a priority.

WHAT IT IS. The main families of financial contract — debt, equity, derivatives, pooled vehicles — and what each one claims, in what order, under what conditions.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because different investors want different risk. Splitting a business's uncertain cash flows into a safe senior claim and a risky residual claim lets both a pension fund and a venture investor fund the same enterprise.

DEPENDENCIES. N1, N2 · Accounting N2 (the balance sheet is the priority ladder).

SUBTOPICS. Bonds, coupons, yield to maturity · the inverse price-yield relationship · duration and interest rate risk · credit risk and ratings · equity as the residual claim · dividends and buybacks · dilution · forwards and futures · options and payoff asymmetry · mutual funds, ETFs, expense ratios and fee drag.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free primary documents · Title: an actual bond or NCD prospectus and a mutual fund Scheme Information Document, both free on SEBI's and the issuer's sites
Why this one: free, and the legally required disclosure documents state the terms, the risks and the fees precisely — including the parts the marketing brochure omits. Learning to find the fee table and the risk factors is the practical skill.
HANDS-ON · Compute the fee that eats a third of your retirement Take a real mutual fund's expense ratio from its scheme document — say 1.8% for an active fund and 0.2% for an index fund. Project ₹10,000 monthly for 30 years at 11% gross, subtracting each fee.

The difference is not 1.6%. It is a large fraction of the final corpus, because the fee compounds against you every year on a growing balance. Compute the exact rupee figure. Most people have never done this arithmetic and are shocked by it.

Then do the bond exercise, which builds an intuition almost nobody has. Take a bond paying a fixed coupon. Now suppose market rates rise by 2%. Compute the new price — it must fall, because your fixed coupon is now uncompetitive. Then do the same for a bond with 20 years remaining versus 2 years. The long bond moves far more. You have just derived duration, and simultaneously explained why rising rates damage long-dated debt holders and why banks holding long bonds can fail when rates rise.

Finally, draw an option payoff by hand — profit against underlying price at expiry, for a call and a put. The kink is the entire point: limited loss, unlimited gain is an asymmetry, and asymmetry is what all of derivatives is for.

The realisation to reach: instruments are combinations of three things — a claim, a priority, and an asymmetry. Anything you cannot decompose into those is something you should not buy.

CONNECTS TO. Accounting N2 (priority is the balance sheet order) · Probability N7 (asymmetric payoffs) · Game Theory (options as strategic flexibility) · Economics N5 (rates move all of this).

N4 Valuation

Where accounting, economics and probability all arrive at once.

WHAT IT IS. Estimating what a business or asset is worth — by discounting its expected cash flows, or by comparing it to similar assets.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because price and value are different, and every investment decision is a claim that they differ in a particular direction. Valuation is the discipline of making that claim explicitly enough to be wrong.

DEPENDENCIES. N1, N2 · Accounting N3–N6 · Business (you must understand the business first).

SUBTOPICS. Intrinsic versus relative valuation · free cash flow to firm and to equity · the cost of capital · terminal value and growth assumptions · multiples and comparable selection · why multiples are DCFs with the assumptions hidden · valuing loss-making companies · margin of safety.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free course + free spreadsheets · Title: Damodaran's Valuation class, free video lectures with his published models
Why this one: free, and he values real companies live, including ones he gets wrong and revisits later. Watching an expert be uncertain in public is the fastest cure for false precision.
HANDS-ON · Value a company you know, then find out why you were wrong Pick an Indian company whose product you personally use and understand physically. Pull five years of financials free from its annual reports. Build a DCF: project revenue, margins and capital spending, choose a discount rate, compute a value per share.

Compare to the market price. You will differ, possibly by a lot. Now the actual exercise: work out what the market must be assuming to justify its price. Reverse the DCF — hold the price fixed and solve for the growth rate implied. Is that growth rate plausible? Sometimes it is obviously not, and that is interesting; more often it is entirely plausible and you learn that your disagreement was an assumption, not an insight.

Then do the multiples version: compute the P/E of five companies in the same industry. They differ. Work out what a P/E actually encodes — growth, risk and capital intensity — and you will find a multiple is a compressed DCF with the assumptions hidden inside a single number.

Finally, the humility check: find a valuation you or an analyst made three years ago and score it against what happened.

The realisation to reach: valuation does not produce a number, it produces a set of assumptions you are now accountable for. The output's precision is entirely fake; the discipline of stating the assumptions is entirely real.

CONNECTS TO. Accounting (all inputs come from there) · Business (assumptions come from strategy) · Probability N7 (margin of safety is risk-of-ruin management) · Psychology (anchoring, overconfidence).

N5 Markets and price discovery

How prices form, and in what precise sense markets are efficient.

WHAT IT IS. The mechanics of trading — order books, liquidity, spreads — and the theory of how information becomes price.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because a price is not announced, it is discovered through the actions of participants with different information and beliefs. Understanding the mechanism explains liquidity crises, flash crashes and why "the market thinks" is a loose way of speaking.

DEPENDENCIES. N3 · Economics N2 · Probability N5.

SUBTOPICS. Order books, bid-ask spread, market and limit orders · liquidity and its disappearance under stress · the efficient market hypothesis in its three forms · what "efficient" does and does not claim · why active funds mostly underperform after fees · behavioural critiques · bubbles and reflexivity · insider information and its regulation.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free data + free regulator material · Title: NSE's free market data and investor education material, alongside SEBI's free investor resources
Why this one: free, primary, and the regulator's own material is unusually plain about costs, conflicts and the empirical record of active management — which is not what the industry's marketing says.
HANDS-ON · Watch liquidity vanish, and check whether skill persists Look at a live order book for a large-cap stock and then for a small-cap. Note the spread and the depth. Now look again during the first minutes after market open, and around a major announcement. Liquidity is not a property of an asset; it is a condition that disappears exactly when you most want to sell.

Then run the persistence test on skill. Take the top ten performing equity funds from five years ago — all published free. Where did they rank over the following five years? Repeat for a different starting year. You will find little persistence, which is the empirical core of the efficiency argument, and you derived it yourself rather than accepting it.

Then compute the cost of trading rather than holding: apply a realistic spread plus brokerage plus short-term tax to a strategy trading monthly versus one holding for ten years, at identical gross returns. The gap is large, and it is the strongest practical argument in this node.

Finally, study one Indian bubble with free contemporary reporting and identify the reflexive loop: rising prices attracted buyers, whose buying raised prices, which validated the story. That is a reinforcing loop, and it works identically in reverse.

The realisation to reach: "efficient" does not mean prices are right. It means they already reflect what is widely known — so beating the market requires either better information, a longer horizon, or tolerance for a risk others avoid. Nothing else works.

CONNECTS TO. Systems Thinking N2 (reflexivity) · Psychology (herding, narrative) · Probability N5 (distinguishing skill from luck) · Distributed Systems (an order book is a consensus mechanism).

N6 Leverage and fragility

How finance breaks — and why it breaks the same way every time.

WHAT IT IS. Borrowing to amplify returns, the maturity mismatch at the heart of banking, and the mechanisms by which one institution's failure becomes everyone's.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because leverage is genuinely useful and genuinely amplifies both directions, and because the institution that borrows short and lends long is both economically valuable and inherently vulnerable to a loss of confidence.

DEPENDENCIES. N2, N3 · Probability N4 (fat tails) · Systems Thinking N2, N4.

SUBTOPICS. Leverage and the amplification of returns and losses · margin calls and forced selling · maturity mismatch · bank runs and deposit insurance · contagion and interconnection · procyclical behaviour · why risk models fail together · systemic versus idiosyncratic failure · moral hazard and bailouts.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free official report · Title: the RBI's Financial Stability Report, published free twice a year, plus the free official inquiry reports into a crisis of your choice
Why this one: free, and a regulator's own account of what it is worried about is a far better education in fragility than any commentary. Read two consecutive editions and see what changed.
HANDS-ON · Amplify a small loss into ruin, then trace a real one Model an investment with 4× leverage. A 10% gain becomes roughly 40%. Now model a 25% loss — the equity is gone entirely. Find the exact percentage move that wipes you out at 2×, 4× and 10× leverage, and notice how small it becomes.

Then add the mechanism that turns a loss into a spiral: a margin call. When your equity falls below a threshold you must sell, and if many leveraged holders sell the same asset simultaneously, the price falls further, triggering more calls. Simulate it with a hundred leveraged participants and watch the cascade. Nobody in the simulation behaves irrationally, and everyone is destroyed.

Now trace a real Indian case with free public material — the IL&FS collapse and the NBFC liquidity crisis that followed. Identify the maturity mismatch (short-term commercial paper funding long-term infrastructure), the trigger, the contagion channel to mutual funds and other NBFCs, and the policy response. Then compare its structure to 2008's, which had different instruments and an identical shape.

The realisation to reach: financial crises are not caused by leverage alone. They are caused by leverage plus a maturity mismatch plus correlated positions — and that combination reappears every decade in a new costume, which is why recognising the structure matters more than knowing the history.

CONNECTS TO. Distributed Systems N7 (metastable failure and cascades — structurally identical) · Systems Thinking N4 (dominance shift) · Probability N4 (fat tails) · Economics N5 · World History (1929, 2008).
