Infrastructure & Cloud

This domain is where software stops being a program and becomes a service — something that must exist continuously, survive the failure of its parts, be changed without being stopped, and cost a defensible amount of money. It is the only technical domain in the Atlas where the finance and the engineering are inseparable.

The single idea. Infrastructure is the practice of describing a desired state and letting a control loop maintain it. Almost everything here — orchestration, infrastructure as code, autoscaling, reconciliation — is the same feedback loop from Systems Thinking, implemented in different layers by different tools.
#containers #reconciliation #observability #slo #cost

00 Why this domain exists

Because "it works on my machine" is not a system, and someone is paying by the hour.

The gap between a program and a service contains everything in this domain: how it gets onto a machine, which machine, what happens when that machine dies, how you know it is healthy, how you change it without downtime, and what it costs per request.

N1 Images and containers

Packaging a program together with everything it assumed.

WHAT IT IS. An image is a layered, content-addressed filesystem snapshot plus metadata. A container is a process started from that snapshot with a restricted view of the kernel — as established in OS N6.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because the hardest part of deployment was never the binary — it was the hundred implicit assumptions about what else was installed. An image makes those assumptions explicit and shippable.

DEPENDENCIES. OS N6.

SUBTOPICS. Layers and the union filesystem · content addressing and caching · build context · multi-stage builds · base image choice and attack surface · why layer order determines rebuild time · registries · reproducibility and why "latest" is a bug.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free specification · Title: The OCI Image Specification, read alongside the Docker build documentation
Why this one: the spec is short and reveals that an image is just a tar archive plus a JSON manifest — which permanently demystifies the tooling. Vendor tutorials teach commands; the spec teaches the object.
HANDS-ON · Make the build slow, then make it fast, by moving two lines Write a Dockerfile that copies your whole source tree and then installs dependencies. Build it. Change one character in a source file and rebuild — every dependency reinstalls. Time it.

Now swap the order: copy only the dependency manifest, install, then copy the source. Rebuild after the same one-character change. It is nearly instant. You moved two lines and changed build time by an order of magnitude, because layers are cached by content and invalidated in order.

Then look inside: export the image with docker save and untar it. It is layers of tar files and a JSON document. Nothing else. Count the size of each layer and find the one that is unexpectedly enormous — usually a build tool that was never needed at runtime. Fix it with a multi-stage build and measure the reduction.

The realisation to reach: an image is a cache-keyed pipeline, not a package. Understanding the cache key is the entire skill, and it is the same content-addressing idea that underlies git.

CONNECTS TO. OS N6 · Software Engineering N9 (dependency boundaries) · Security (supply chain).

N2 Orchestration depth

You stop issuing commands and start declaring a desired state.

WHAT IT IS. A control system that continuously compares the actual state of a cluster with a declared desired state and takes action to close the gap.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because imperative deployment does not survive failure. If you ran a process and the machine died, nothing runs it again. If you declared that three copies should exist, something notices there are two and acts. This shift from imperative to declarative is the single most important idea in the domain.

DEPENDENCIES. N1 · Distributed Systems N5 (the cluster state store is a consensus system) · Systems Thinking (feedback loops).

SUBTOPICS. Desired versus actual state · the reconciliation loop · controllers and operators · the scheduler and bin packing · health checks — liveness versus readiness · rolling updates and disruption budgets · why the cluster state lives in etcd · declarative configuration as data.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free documentation · Title: Kubernetes documentation, the "Concepts" section — specifically the controller and scheduler pages
Why this one: free, and the concepts pages are unusually good at explaining the control-loop model. Skip the tutorials until you understand reconciliation, or you will learn commands instead of the idea.

DEEPER (optional) — the Borg paper (Google, free), which is the honest account of what was learned before Kubernetes existed.
HANDS-ON · Fight the control loop and lose Run a local cluster — kind or k3s, both free. Declare that three replicas of a service should exist. Now delete one by hand. Watch a replacement appear within seconds. Delete two. Same result.

Now try to win: delete a replica in a tight loop, as fast as you can. You cannot get below the declared count for long, and you have just experienced a negative feedback loop resisting a disturbance — exactly the structure from your Systems Thinking note, running on your laptop.

Then break it instructively. Configure a liveness probe that always fails. The container is killed, restarted, killed, restarted — a control loop with a bad sensor destroys the thing it is protecting. Then configure a readiness probe that never passes and watch traffic never arrive while the pod stays "healthy". The distinction between the two probes is now permanent knowledge.

Finally, perform a rolling update with a deliberately broken image and watch the rollout stall rather than complete — the control loop refusing to destroy a working state to reach a broken declared one.

The realisation to reach: you are no longer operating the system; you are tuning a controller. Most operational incidents in this world are control-loop pathologies — bad sensors, wrong set points, oscillation — not software bugs.

CONNECTS TO. Systems Thinking (this is a textbook servo loop) · Distributed Systems N5 · Mechanical/Electrical (control theory is the same mathematics) · Economics (central bank policy is a control loop with a long delay).

N3 Infrastructure as code

Making the environment a reviewable, versioned artefact rather than an oral tradition.

WHAT IT IS. Declaring infrastructure in files that are version-controlled, reviewed and applied by a tool that computes the difference between the declaration and reality.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because manually configured environments diverge, and the divergence is invisible until an incident. IaC makes the environment diffable, which makes it reviewable, which makes it reproducible.

DEPENDENCIES. N1, N2.

SUBTOPICS. Declarative versus imperative provisioning · state files and why they exist · drift detection · plan and apply · immutable infrastructure and cattle-not-pets · idempotency of provisioning · secrets management · environment parity.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free documentation · Title: OpenTofu / Terraform documentation, the "State" and "Plan" concept pages
Why this one: free, and state is the concept people get wrong. Understanding why the tool needs a state file — and what breaks when reality and state disagree — is most of the domain.
HANDS-ON · Create drift on purpose and watch it get detected Define something small in code — a container, a network, a local resource. Apply it. Now change it by hand, outside the tool, exactly as a stressed engineer would during an incident.

Run plan again. The tool reports a difference and offers to revert your change. You have just seen the central tension of this node: the code is the source of truth, and any manual fix is a time bomb that will be silently reverted at the next deploy.

Then create the harder failure: delete the resource by hand and run plan. The tool now believes something exists that does not. Repair the state and notice how much of operations is reconciling a model with a reality that changed behind your back — the same problem as stale query planner statistics and stale DNS caches.

The realisation to reach: infrastructure as code does not describe your infrastructure; it describes what you believe about it, and every outage in this space lives in the gap between those two things.

CONNECTS TO. Software Engineering N8 (review and testing) · Databases N3 (models of reality going stale) · Business (audit and compliance).

N4 Cloud networking

Networking N1–N8, re-implemented in software, with a bill attached.

WHAT IT IS. Virtual networks, load balancers, ingress, service discovery and the policy layer that decides which service may talk to which — all software abstractions over the physical network.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because multi-tenant machines need isolated networks, and services that move between machines need stable identities. Everything here re-solves a physical networking problem in software, with the addition that traffic now costs money in one direction.

DEPENDENCIES. Networking N2–N7 · N2.

SUBTOPICS. VPCs, subnets, routing tables and security groups · L4 versus L7 load balancing · ingress and reverse proxies · service discovery and DNS-based routing · NAT gateways · availability zones versus regions · why egress is charged and ingress is not · private connectivity.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free documentation · Title: Your chosen cloud's VPC concepts documentation, read with the Networking page open beside it
Why this one: the value here is in the mapping. Every cloud construct is a familiar physical concept renamed, and reading them side by side collapses a large vocabulary into a small one.
HANDS-ON · Trace a packet through three layers of indirection Deploy a service behind a load balancer in a local cluster. Now answer, with evidence rather than belief: from a client request to the process handling it, how many network hops, address translations and proxies are involved? Capture at each layer and count.

You will typically find three or four hops that did not exist in your mental model — the ingress controller, the cluster's virtual IP, the node's routing rules, the container's namespace. Each adds latency; measure it and compare against the physical baseline from Networking N8.

Then break it on purpose: apply a network policy that denies traffic between two services, and observe the failure mode. It does not produce a clean "denied" — it produces a timeout, which is Distributed Systems N1 arriving in your operational life.

The realisation to reach: every layer of network indirection you add is latency you cannot see and a failure mode that looks like slowness.

CONNECTS TO. Networking · Distributed Systems N1 · Cost (egress is the line item that surprises everyone) · Geopolitics (data residency determines region choice).

N5 Observability depth

Three instruments, three different questions, and one that most teams never build.

WHAT IT IS. The property of a system that lets you answer questions about its internal state from its external outputs — implemented through logs, metrics and traces, each with different costs and blind spots.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because in a distributed system, no single machine has the story. The request that failed touched eight services, and none of them knows it failed. Observability is the reconstruction of a narrative from fragments.

DEPENDENCIES. Distributed Systems N1 · Software Engineering N8.

SUBTOPICS. Logs, metrics and traces and what each is bad at · structured logging · cardinality and why it dominates cost · counters, gauges, histograms · percentiles and why averages are useless here · distributed tracing, spans and context propagation · sampling · monitoring versus observability · the difference between known and unknown unknowns.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free book · Title: Google's Site Reliability Engineering, chapter 6 ("Monitoring Distributed Systems") — free in full at sre.google
Why this one: free, and it is opinionated about what not to monitor, which is far more useful than a list of things you could measure. The four golden signals will structure your thinking permanently.

DEEPER (optional) — the OpenTelemetry documentation for the concrete model of spans and context propagation.
HANDS-ON · Find the slow service without knowing which one it is Build three small services that call each other in a chain, and inject 200 ms of latency into the middle one. Now try to find it using only logs. You will have three log streams with no shared identifier, and correlating them by timestamp will be miserable and unreliable — which is exactly the lesson.

Now add a request ID, propagated through every call and included in every log line. Suddenly you can reconstruct one request's journey. Then add real distributed tracing with spans, and the answer becomes a picture you read in two seconds.

Next, the metrics lesson: record average latency and confirm it looks fine. Record p99 and watch the problem appear. This is Probability N4 in production, and it is why dashboards showing averages hide outages.

Finally, the cost lesson: add a metric label containing user ID. Watch your time-series count explode combinatorially. Cardinality, not volume, is what makes observability expensive — and this single mistake has produced monitoring bills larger than the infrastructure being monitored.

The realisation to reach: logs answer "what happened to this one thing", metrics answer "what is the shape of everything", traces answer "where did the time go". Choosing the wrong instrument makes an easy question unanswerable.

CONNECTS TO. Probability N4 (percentiles) · Software Engineering N8 · Distributed Systems N1 (you are inferring state you cannot observe directly) · Psychology (incident diagnosis under bias).

N6 Reliability

Treating failure as a budget rather than a moral failing.

WHAT IT IS. The practice of setting explicit reliability targets, measuring against them, spending the remainder deliberately on change, and learning from incidents without assigning blame.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because "make it never fail" is not an engineering objective — it has infinite cost. Reliability engineering replaces that with a number, which converts an emotional argument between product and operations into an arithmetic one.

DEPENDENCIES. N5 · Probability N4 · Distributed Systems N7.

SUBTOPICS. SLI, SLO, SLA and the differences · error budgets · the cost curve of additional nines · blameless postmortems · toil and its elimination · graceful degradation · chaos engineering · on-call as a system with human components.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free book · Title: Google's Site Reliability Engineering and The SRE Workbook, both free in full at sre.google — the SLO and error budget chapters
Why this one: free, and it is the origin of the error-budget idea. The workbook is the more practical of the two and contains worked examples you can copy.
HANDS-ON · Compute your own error budget and spend it Take any service you can measure — even a personal project or a public API you poll. Define one SLI precisely: the proportion of requests returning success within 300 ms. Collect a week of real data.

Now set an SLO of 99.9% and compute the error budget: 0.1% of a week is about ten minutes. Look at your actual data and determine whether you already spent it. Most people are shocked by how small the budget is and how much of it a single bad deploy consumes.

Then do the arithmetic that changes how you argue: compute the budget for 99%, 99.9%, 99.99% and 99.999%. The last is five minutes per year — which is less time than it takes a human to notice, wake up and log in. Past a certain point, more nines is not an engineering problem but a staffing and architecture problem, and its cost rises far faster than its benefit.

Finally, write a postmortem for a real failure you caused, using the free SRE workbook template, with the discipline of naming no person and identifying at least three contributing causes.

The realisation to reach: the target is never 100%, and choosing a number less than 100% on purpose is what makes shipping possible at all.

CONNECTS TO. Probability N7 (decisions under uncertainty) · Business (SLAs are contractual and have penalties) · Psychology (blame, hindsight bias, just culture) · Systems Thinking (incidents are emergent, not caused by one thing).

N7 Cost

The node that connects this entire cluster to Value & Exchange.

WHAT IT IS. Understanding what infrastructure actually costs, how pricing models create incentives, and how to express a technical architecture as a unit economic statement.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because in a software business, infrastructure is the main component of cost of goods sold — so architecture decisions are gross margin decisions. An engineer who can connect those two things is disproportionately valuable and disproportionately rare.

DEPENDENCIES. N1–N4 · Accounting (gross margin) · Business (unit economics).

SUBTOPICS. Compute, storage, network and managed-service pricing · why egress is asymmetrically priced and what that is designed to achieve · reserved and spot capacity · the buy-versus-build calculation · cost per request as an engineering metric · the real economics of leaving the cloud · how infrastructure cost shows up in a company's gross margin.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free primary documents · Title: Cloud providers' public pricing pages, read alongside the cost-of-revenue section of a real infrastructure company's annual report on SEC EDGAR
Why this one: both are free, and the pairing is the whole point — the pricing page tells you what you pay, and the filing tells you what that does to a business at scale. No secondary source connects these two as well as reading them together.
HANDS-ON · Price one real workload three ways, then find the trap Take a concrete workload: 100 GB of data, 10 million requests a month, 500 GB of egress. Price it three ways using public pricing — a managed service, containers on rented VMs, and a single large dedicated server.

Build the spreadsheet honestly. You will usually find the managed option is several times more expensive and that egress alone dominates in at least one scenario. Now find why: inbound traffic is free and outbound is charged, which is a deliberate incentive to keep data where it lands. That single asymmetry explains data gravity, multi-cloud difficulty, and a large part of the industry's structure.

Then convert it into the language of Cluster C4: if you charge ₹100 per customer per month and this workload serves 1,000 customers, what is your gross margin under each option? Now double the traffic and recompute — and notice which option's margin improves with scale and which does not.

The realisation to reach: an architecture diagram is a cost structure in disguise, and the choice between managed and self-operated is a choice about where your money goes — vendor margin or engineer salary — not a purely technical one.

CONNECTS TO. Accounting (COGS and gross margin) · Business (unit economics, buy vs build) · Economics (pricing as incentive design) · Geopolitics (cloud concentration and sovereignty).
