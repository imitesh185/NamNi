Data Engineering

The second domain in the Atlas set to genuine depth. Data engineering is not "ETL" and it is not a set of tools — it is the discipline of moving data between systems that disagree about structure, timing and truth, without silently losing or duplicating any of it, at a cost someone is willing to pay.

The single idea. Every pipeline is a distributed system whose failures are silent. A web service that breaks returns a 500 and someone is paged. A pipeline that breaks produces plausible numbers that are wrong, and nobody notices for a quarter. Everything in this domain is a defence against that specific asymmetry.
#columnar #cdc #idempotency #modelling #contracts

00 Why this domain exists

Because the system that records a fact is almost never the system that needs to reason about it.

A transactional database is optimised for writing one row correctly under concurrency. Answering "what happened across all rows last quarter, sliced six ways" is a different workload with opposite requirements, and running it against production will take production down. That single conflict created this entire field.

Environment for this domain Use DuckDB for nearly everything. It is free, single-file, reads Parquet and CSV natively, and is fast enough that you can experiment on tens of millions of rows on a laptop. It removes the cluster from the learning loop, which is the single biggest obstacle to actually doing this domain's hands-on work.

N1 Batch versus streaming

The shape of time in your pipeline — and the distinction that resolves most confusion in this field.

WHAT IT IS. Batch processes a bounded dataset with a known end. Streaming processes an unbounded one that never ends, which forces you to decide when a result is final even though more data may still arrive.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because the world produces events continuously but questions are asked at moments. The gap between those two facts is where event time, windows, watermarks and late data all come from.

DEPENDENCIES. Distributed Systems N2 (there is no global now).

SUBTOPICS. Bounded and unbounded data · event time versus processing time versus ingestion time · tumbling, sliding and session windows · watermarks as a heuristic guess about completeness · late-arriving data · the lambda and kappa architectures · why "real time" almost always means "a few seconds, and we chose that".

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free article · Title: "Streaming 101" and "Streaming 102" (Tyler Akidau), freely available
Why this one: free, and it is the clearest existing treatment of event time versus processing time. It reframes batch as a special case of streaming rather than as a different technology, which permanently simplifies the field.
HANDS-ON · Count events per hour, then receive yesterday's data Generate a day of events with timestamps. Compute a simple hourly count in batch. Correct, easy, done.

Now stream the same events and compute the same counts incrementally — but deliberately deliver 5% of them out of order, some arriving hours after their event time, as a mobile client with poor connectivity genuinely does. Your hourly counts are now wrong, and they were already reported.

Work through the three possible responses and implement each: drop late events (fast, lossy, and the count never matches the batch answer); wait longer before finalising (correct, but every result is delayed by the worst case); restate the earlier window (correct and current, but every downstream consumer must handle a number that changes after publication).

Then measure the completeness curve: what fraction of a given hour's events have arrived after 1 minute, 5 minutes, an hour, a day? That curve is your watermark, and it is a business decision disguised as a technical one.

The realisation to reach: there is no correct answer to "how long do I wait?" — only a trade-off between latency and completeness that someone must choose explicitly, and that someone is usually you.

CONNECTS TO. Distributed Systems N2 · Probability N4 (the lateness distribution is heavy-tailed) · Business (a restated metric destroys trust faster than a late one).

N2 Storage formats depth

The clearest example in the Atlas of layout beating cleverness.

WHAT IT IS. How analytical data is physically arranged on disk — grouped by column rather than by row — and the encoding, compression and statistics that this arrangement makes possible.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because an analytical query touches few columns and many rows, while row storage forces you to read every column of every row. Columnar layout reads only what is needed, compresses far better because similar values sit together, and can skip entire blocks using stored statistics.

DEPENDENCIES. Computing N4 · Databases N2.

SUBTOPICS. Row groups, column chunks and pages · dictionary and run-length encoding · min/max statistics per block · predicate pushdown · column pruning · partitioning schemes · the small-files problem · compaction · open table formats and why they exist — atomic commits over object storage.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free specification + free engine · Title: The Apache Parquet format specification, explored using DuckDB
Why this one: both are free, and Parquet's spec is short enough to read in one sitting. Pairing the spec with an engine that lets you query files directly means you can verify every claim in the spec with a measurement.

DEEPER (optional) — the Apache Iceberg specification, for how atomic commits and time travel are achieved on storage that has no transactions.
HANDS-ON · The same data, ten times smaller and a hundred times faster Take a real dataset of at least ten million rows and twenty columns — government open data works well. Save it as CSV. Note the size. Convert it to Parquet with DuckDB in one line. Note the size again — typically five to ten times smaller, with no information lost.

Now query one column with a filter, over both formats, and time it. The gap will be enormous. Explain it in three parts, verifying each: column pruning (you read 1 column of 20, so 95% less data), compression (similar values adjacent compress far better), and predicate pushdown (min/max statistics let whole row groups be skipped without decompression).

Then prove the last one directly: sort the data by your filter column, rewrite, and re-run the query. It gets dramatically faster again, because the statistics are now selective. Physically sorting data changes query performance without changing a single byte of logical content.

Finally, create the failure mode everyone hits: partition the same dataset into 10,000 tiny files. Query it. It is now slower than the CSV, because per-file overhead dominates. Compact the files and watch it recover. Find the file size where performance peaks — usually a few hundred megabytes.

The realisation to reach: this is Computing N4's stride cliff at yet another scale. Sequential access to what you need, and never reading what you do not, is the whole of storage performance from L1 cache to object storage.

CONNECTS TO. Computing N4 · Databases N2 · Infrastructure N7 (storage and scan cost are billed) · AI (training data formats).

N3 Ingestion and change data capture

Getting data out of a source system without harming it, and without missing anything.

WHAT IT IS. The mechanisms for extracting data from operational systems — periodic queries, event streams, or reading the database's own replication log — and the trade-offs each makes.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because the naive approach — query the production database on a schedule — breaks in three predictable ways: it loads the source, it misses deletes, and it misses intermediate states between polls.

DEPENDENCIES. Databases N4 (the WAL) · Distributed Systems N7 (idempotency).

SUBTOPICS. Full snapshot versus incremental extraction · high-watermark columns and why updated_at is unreliable · hard deletes and the tombstone problem · CDC from the write-ahead log · log-based integration and the log as a universal primitive · schema drift at the source · exactly-once delivery as an illusion, and effectively-once via idempotent writes.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free article · Title: "The Log: What every software engineer should know about real-time data's unifying abstraction" (Jay Kreps), freely available
Why this one: free, and it is the piece that reframes databases, replication, streaming and integration as one idea — an ordered log — rather than four. It will change how you see the previous three domains as well.
HANDS-ON · Lose rows three different ways, then stop losing them Set up a source table and a naive incremental extractor that pulls rows where updated_at > last_run. Run it on a schedule while a writer modifies the source.

Now break it three ways, deliberately, and count the damage each time. Delete a row — your warehouse still contains it, forever, and no query will ever reveal the discrepancy. Update a row twice between polls — you captured only the final state, so any analysis of intermediate states is silently wrong. Have two transactions commit out of timestamp order — one is missed entirely, because your watermark advanced past it.

That third one is the subtle killer, and it is a direct consequence of Distributed Systems N2. Reproduce it deliberately, then reason about how long a safety margin would need to be.

Now do it properly: enable logical replication on PostgreSQL and consume the WAL. Every insert, update and delete appears as an ordered event with no polling and no load on the source. Delete a row and watch the tombstone arrive.

The realisation to reach: a table is a compressed summary of a log, and you have been reading the summary while needing the log. Once seen, this reframes replication, event sourcing, Kafka and CDC as one mechanism.

CONNECTS TO. Databases N4 · Distributed Systems N2, N7 · Accounting (a ledger is a log, and a balance is its summary — the same insight, four hundred years earlier).

N4 Transformation and orchestration

Making a pipeline something you can re-run without fear.

WHAT IT IS. Expressing derived datasets as a dependency graph, and the scheduler that runs each step when its inputs are ready, retries failures, and allows historical reprocessing.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because pipelines fail constantly — upstream is late, a schema changed, a node died — and the only sustainable answer is to make every step safely re-runnable. Idempotency is not a nice property here; it is the entire design constraint.

DEPENDENCIES. N1, N3 · Distributed Systems N7 · Software Engineering N4 (DAGs are graphs).

SUBTOPICS. DAGs and dependency resolution · idempotent writes and partition overwrite · backfills and reprocessing history · ETL versus ELT and why ELT won · incremental versus full refresh · data freshness SLAs · testing transformations · the cost of a full backfill.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free documentation · Title: dbt's documentation on materialisations, incremental models and testing — free
Why this one: free, and dbt encodes the current consensus on transformation practice — idempotency, testing, and lineage as first-class concerns — clearly enough that the concepts transfer even if you never use the tool.
HANDS-ON · Run the same job twice and get the wrong answer Build a three-step pipeline: raw events → daily aggregates → a summary table. Have step two append to its output.

Now run it twice for the same day. Every number doubles. This is the most common bug in the entire field, it produces plausible output, and no error is raised anywhere.

Fix it with a partition overwrite — delete-and-insert the day's partition atomically — and verify that running it five times produces identical results. You have implemented idempotency, and the operational consequence is enormous: retries become safe, so failures stop being emergencies.

Then hit the harder problem. Change the business logic of step two and backfill two years of history. Discover that the source data no longer contains a column you now depend on, that one month's data was already deleted, and that the backfill would cost more compute than a month of normal operation.

Finally, make step one fail deliberately and observe whether steps two and three run anyway on stale input. If they do, you have found the difference between a scheduler and an orchestrator.

The realisation to reach: a pipeline is not code that runs, it is code that must be able to run again, for any date, at any time, and produce the same answer. Every architectural decision here follows from that.

CONNECTS TO. Distributed Systems N7 · Infrastructure N2 (orchestration is reconciliation) · Software Engineering N8 (testing).

N5 Modelling for analytics

Shaping data around questions rather than around transactions.

WHAT IT IS. Organising warehouse data into facts (events, measurements) and dimensions (the entities that describe them), at a deliberately chosen level of detail.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because a normalised transactional schema is optimised for writing consistently and is hostile to the question "revenue by region by month by product". Dimensional modelling optimises for the opposite, accepting redundancy in exchange for query simplicity.

DEPENDENCIES. Databases N6 · N2.

SUBTOPICS. Facts and dimensions · grain, and why choosing it wrong is unrecoverable · star and snowflake schemas · slowly changing dimensions, especially type 2 · conformed dimensions · additive, semi-additive and non-additive measures · surrogate keys · the semantic layer and single metric definitions.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free articles · Title: The Kimball Group's freely published design tips and dimensional modelling techniques on their website
Why this one: free, canonical, and organised as short problem-specific notes rather than a monolith — which suits a domain where you need one pattern at a time.
HANDS-ON · Ask "what was true then?" and watch your model fail Model something with real history — sales by salesperson by region. Build the obvious star schema: a sales fact, a salesperson dimension, a region dimension. Answer "revenue by region last year". Easy.

Now a salesperson moves from the West region to the East. Update the dimension row — the natural thing to do. Re-run last year's report. Every historical sale that person made has silently moved to the East region. Last year's numbers just changed, and nothing warned you.

This is why slowly changing dimensions type 2 exists. Implement it: instead of updating, insert a new row with validity dates and a surrogate key, and have facts reference the key that was current at the time. Re-run the report and watch history hold still.

Then confront grain. Build a fact table at order level, and be asked for revenue by product. You cannot answer without changing the grain to line-item — and every downstream consumer of the order-level table breaks when you do. Grain is the one decision in this node that is not reversible cheaply.

The realisation to reach: analytical models must represent what was believed at a point in time, not just what is currently true — and transactional schemas, which overwrite, throw that information away by default.

CONNECTS TO. Databases N6 · Accounting (a ledger never overwrites, for exactly this reason) · Business (metric definitions are political) · Indian History (the same problem — what did the record mean when it was written).

N6 Quality and contracts

The domain's defining problem: failures that produce plausible numbers.

WHAT IT IS. Automated assertions about data itself — freshness, volume, distribution, schema, referential integrity — plus explicit agreements with upstream producers about what they will not change without warning.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because a broken pipeline that raises an error is a good day. The bad day is a pipeline that runs successfully on data that changed meaning, and reports numbers that are wrong, confidently, to people making decisions.

DEPENDENCIES. N4 · Probability N4–N5 (distribution tests are statistical) · Software Engineering N8.

SUBTOPICS. The four test families: freshness, volume, distribution, schema · uniqueness and referential integrity · anomaly detection and its false-positive problem · data contracts and schema registries · producer versus consumer responsibility · reconciliation against source · circuit-breaking a pipeline versus publishing bad data.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free documentation · Title: Great Expectations documentation on expectation types, and dbt's tests documentation — both free
Why this one: free, and reading the catalogue of possible assertions is the fastest way to discover the failure modes you had not thought to check for. The list itself is the education.
HANDS-ON · Break the data without breaking the pipeline Build a working pipeline with a dashboard on top. Now introduce four faults, one at a time. Every one of them must leave the pipeline green.

Upstream changes a currency from rupees to lakhs. Numbers drop by a factor of 100,000 and the schema is unchanged. A join silently becomes one-to-many. Row counts inflate and revenue triples. An upstream system stops sending one category of event. The pipeline succeeds on a subset. A nullable column starts arriving null. Averages shift and nothing errors.

Now write the assertions that catch each: row-count-within-expected-range, distribution comparison against a trailing window, uniqueness on the join key, and null-rate thresholds. Re-run the four faults and verify each is caught.

Then face the false-positive problem from Probability N2: set your anomaly thresholds tight and count how often they fire on normal variation. A data quality system that cries wolf is worse than none, because it trains people to click through.

The realisation to reach: the pipeline's exit code tells you whether the code worked. Nothing except an explicit assertion tells you whether the data is right — and those are completely different questions.

CONNECTS TO. Probability N2 (alert precision), N5 (distribution tests) · Software Engineering N8 · Business (trust in numbers is the actual product of a data team).

N7 The platform landscape

Warehouse, lake, lakehouse — three answers to one question about coupling.

WHAT IT IS. The architectural families for storing and querying analytical data, distinguished chiefly by whether storage and compute are coupled and whether the format is open.

WHY IT EXISTS. Warehouses gave transactions and performance but locked data into a vendor's format. Lakes gave open formats and cheap storage but lost transactions and performance. Lakehouses are the attempt to have both, via open table formats that add atomic commits over object storage.

DEPENDENCIES. N2 · Databases N1–N4 · Infrastructure N7.

SUBTOPICS. Coupled versus separated storage and compute · MPP query execution · object storage as a substrate and its lack of atomicity · open table formats — snapshots, manifests, time travel · the semantic layer · streaming-first architectures · what each option actually locks you into.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free paper · Title: "Lakehouse: A New Generation of Open Platforms that Unify Data Warehousing and Advanced Analytics" (Armbrust et al., free)
Why this one: free, and it states the historical problem and the trade-offs directly rather than as marketing. Read it as an argument you are allowed to disagree with.
HANDS-ON · Give object storage a transaction it does not have Write a dataset to a folder of Parquet files. Now update it while another process is reading. The reader sees a mixture of old and new files — a torn read, exactly the atomicity problem from Databases N4, on storage that has no transactions at all.

Now solve it the way open table formats do: never modify files in place. Write new files, then atomically swap a small pointer file naming the current set. Readers resolve the pointer once and see a consistent snapshot. You have just rebuilt the core of Iceberg, and it is the same write-temp-then-rename idiom from OS N4.

Then get time travel free: keep old pointer files and you can query the dataset as of yesterday.

The realisation to reach: the same atomic-pointer-swap trick appears at every layer of this Atlas — filesystem rename, database commit, table format snapshot, blue-green deploy. One idea, four disguises.

CONNECTS TO. OS N4 · Databases N4 · Infrastructure N3 (immutability) · Business (vendor lock-in has a price).

N8 Cost, lineage and governance

Who can see what, where a number came from, and what it cost to produce.

WHAT IT IS. The operational surround: tracking which datasets derive from which, controlling access to sensitive fields, and understanding the cost of storage and query.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because platforms make querying easy and therefore make spending easy, and because personal data carries legal obligations that are enforced with fines. Both are engineering constraints, not paperwork.

DEPENDENCIES. N4, N7 · Infrastructure N7.

SUBTOPICS. Column-level lineage · impact analysis before a schema change · access control and column masking · PII classification · retention and the right to erasure · query cost attribution · the economics of separated storage and compute · data residency requirements.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free primary document · Title: India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 — the actual statute, read directly
Why this one: free, and it is short. Summaries of data protection law are consistently worse than the text, and reading the obligations directly makes it obvious why deletion and lineage are engineering features rather than policy documents.
HANDS-ON · Delete one person, completely Take your pipeline and receive a lawful erasure request for a single individual. Now actually do it.

Trace every place that person's data exists: the source table, the raw landing zone, every intermediate dataset, the aggregates, the backups, the object storage snapshots, and — the one everyone forgets — the time-travel history that lets you query yesterday's version.

You will find that the architecture that made your pipeline reliable and re-runnable is precisely what makes deletion nearly impossible. Immutability and the right to erasure are in direct conflict, and resolving it requires a design decision made before the request arrives — usually crypto-shredding or a tokenisation boundary.

Then do the cost half: pick your most expensive query, compute its scan volume, and estimate its cost per run at public prices. Multiply by its schedule. Most people find one dashboard nobody looks at costing more per year than a developer's laptop.

The realisation to reach: governance is not paperwork bolted on afterwards. It is a set of constraints that must be present in the architecture from the first table, because retrofitting them is sometimes genuinely impossible.

CONNECTS TO. Infrastructure N7 · Indian Politics & Government (the DPDP Act) · Geopolitics (data residency and sovereignty) · Business (data as an asset with a carrying cost).
