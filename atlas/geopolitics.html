Geopolitics

Geopolitics is the study of how states pursue interests under constraints they did not choose. The discipline that makes it useful rather than entertaining is simple and demanding: explain every decision by the incentives and constraints of the decider, never by their character.

The single idea. States are not people and do not have feelings. They are institutions with interests — security, resources, market access, regime survival — pursued by people facing domestic constraints. A decision that looks irrational almost always makes sense once you find the constraint you did not know about, and finding it is the entire skill.

00 Why this domain exists

And why it is the domain where confident nonsense is most abundant.

Geopolitics attracts grand narratives because they are satisfying and unfalsifiable. The corrective is the same one used throughout this Atlas: demand a mechanism, demand primary evidence, and check whether the theory predicted anything before the fact.

01 The reconstruction method

The hands-on structure for this entire domain — apply it to any event.

TAKE ONE REAL EVENT
  a summit, a treaty, a sanction, a border incident, an export control

1 · WHAT DOES EACH SIDE WANT?          state the interest, not the rhetoric
2 · WHAT CONSTRAINS EACH SIDE?         geography, resources, alliances,
                                       domestic politics, prior commitments
3 · WHAT ARE THE ALTERNATIVES?         what else could each have done?
4 · WHAT IS THE COST OF EACH OPTION?   including domestic political cost
5 · NOW EXPLAIN THE DECISION           without using anyone's personality
6 · WHAT WOULD FALSIFY YOUR ACCOUNT?   what should we observe next?

Then read a primary document — the speech, the joint statement, the
treaty text — and check your reconstruction against it.
The test of understanding in this domain Construct the strongest version of the position you find least sympathetic. If you cannot state why a state's behaviour is rational from where it sits, you do not yet understand the situation — you have only chosen a side.

N1 Forms of power

What "power" decomposes into, and why the components are not interchangeable.

WHAT IT IS. The distinct capabilities states use to influence outcomes — military, economic, technological, institutional and persuasive.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because these do not convert freely into one another. A state can be militarily powerful and economically dependent, or economically large and institutionally weak. Most analytical errors come from treating power as a single quantity.

DEPENDENCIES. World History N7.

SUBTOPICS. Military capability and its limits · economic size versus economic leverage · technological control · institutional position — voting weights, standard-setting, veto power · soft power and its overstatement · alliances as force multipliers and as entanglements · the difference between power and influence.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free official data · Title: SIPRI's free military expenditure and arms transfer databases, alongside World Bank and IMF free economic data
Why this one: free, and building your own comparison table across power dimensions immediately shows which states are strong on one axis and weak on another — which is precisely the insight the single-quantity framing hides.
HANDS-ON · Build a power table and find the mismatches Pick six countries. Build a table with columns for military spending, GDP, energy self-sufficiency, semiconductor capability, reserve currency status, permanent Security Council membership and food self-sufficiency. Fill it from free data.

The mismatches are the interesting part. Find a state strong militarily and dependent on imported energy. Find one economically large without a reserve currency. Find one with technological depth and no institutional voting weight. Each mismatch is a vulnerability and predicts where that state will invest and what it will fear.

Then use it to predict: for each mismatch, name the policy you would expect that state to pursue. Then check whether it is pursuing it. Your hit rate will be higher than you expect, which is the point — structural position predicts behaviour better than declared intention does.

Then examine conversion difficulty: economic size does not automatically become military capability or institutional influence, and the conversion takes decades. That lag explains a great deal about why rising powers are frustrated and established powers are complacent.

The realisation to reach: power is a vector rather than a scalar, and the interesting analysis is always about the components that are out of balance.

CONNECTS TO. Economics N6 · Business N3 (structural advantage) · World History N7.

N2 Geography as constraint

Chokepoints, borders and the things no state can change about itself.

WHAT IT IS. The permanent physical facts a state must work with — its coastline, its neighbours, its resource endowment, and the routes its trade must take.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because these change on geological timescales while politics changes on decadal ones. Geography does not decide outcomes, but it sets the menu of options and the price of each.

DEPENDENCIES. World Geography N5–N6.

SUBTOPICS. Maritime chokepoints and transit volumes · land borders and their defensibility · landlocked states and transit dependence · buffer states · sea lines of communication · the difference between a chokepoint that can be bypassed at cost and one that cannot · submarine cables and digital chokepoints · Arctic route changes.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free analysis + live data · Title: the EIA's free world oil transit chokepoints analysis and free marine traffic visualisations
Why this one: free, and it publishes actual transit volumes. Watching live ship traffic funnel through a strait converts an abstraction into an observable fact.
HANDS-ON · Reconstruct one state's entire strategy from its map Pick a country and derive its likely strategic priorities from geography alone, before reading anything about its policy. Coastline length, ports, navigable rivers, defensible borders, energy and food self-sufficiency, and which chokepoints its trade must cross.

Then check against its actual published strategy documents, which are free. Your predictions will be substantially correct, and where they are wrong you will have found the non-geographic factor — history, ideology, domestic politics — which is exactly where the interesting analysis is.

Do this for India specifically: a long peninsula astride Indian Ocean sea lanes, two difficult land borders, heavy energy import dependence, and a diaspora and trade concentration in the Gulf. Now derive the naval priority, the energy diversification effort, the Gulf relationships and the continental-versus-maritime tension. Then read India's actual maritime and defence documents.

Then examine a landlocked state's dependence and note that transit rights are a permanent negotiation with a neighbour who knows it.

The realisation to reach: a very large share of foreign policy is predictable from a map plus a resource table — and knowing that lets you evaluate claims about intentions against structural facts.

CONNECTS TO. World Geography N6 · Cars N6 (freight) · Networking N3 (cable routes) · Business N5 (supply chains).

N3 Money as power

Currency, sanctions and financial infrastructure — the most consequential and least visible node.

WHAT IT IS. The use of currency dominance, payment systems and capital access as instruments of state power.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because most international trade is invoiced and settled in a small number of currencies through a small number of systems. Whoever controls those systems can exclude others from them, which is coercive power exercised without any military action.

DEPENDENCIES. Economics N5–N6 · Finance.

SUBTOPICS. Why the dollar is the reserve currency and what that requires · the Triffin dilemma · the payment messaging layer and exclusion from it · secondary sanctions and extraterritorial reach · reserve accumulation as insurance · de-dollarisation attempts and why they are slow · currency swap lines · the impossible trinity.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free official data · Title: the IMF's free COFER data on reserve currency composition and BIS free statistics on international currency use
Why this one: free, and it quantifies currency dominance precisely. The gap between "de-dollarisation" rhetoric and the actual reserve composition trend is instructive, and you can measure it rather than argue about it.
HANDS-ON · Measure de-dollarisation instead of arguing about it Pull IMF COFER data on the currency composition of global reserves over twenty years. Plot the dollar's share. It has declined, slowly, and remains dominant by a wide margin. Then find what replaced the lost share — it is distributed across several currencies rather than concentrated in one.

The data supports "gradual diversification" and not "imminent replacement", and being able to state that with a chart is worth more than any amount of confident commentary.

Then work out the mechanism of sanctions: trace what actually happens when a bank is excluded from dollar clearing. It cannot settle dollar transactions, so counterparties will not deal with it, so it is effectively cut from most international trade regardless of its own country's laws. That is network effect as coercion, and it is the same structure as Business N3's moats.

Then examine the cost to the issuer: reserve currency status requires supplying the world with your currency, which requires running deficits — the Triffin dilemma. The privilege has a structural price.

Then look at India's position: reserve levels, the rupee's internationalisation attempts, and the payment arrangements built for specific trades. Each is a response to a documented vulnerability.

The realisation to reach: financial infrastructure is a chokepoint exactly like a strait, and exclusion from it is a coercive act that requires no military capability at all.

CONNECTS TO. Economics N5–N6 · Finance N6 · Business N3 (network effects) · Distributed Systems (a payment network's topology is its power).

N4 Deterrence and escalation

Where game theory earns its place.

WHAT IT IS. The logic of preventing an action by making its costs credibly unacceptable, and the dynamics of escalation between states.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because deterrence is a communication problem under uncertainty: your threat must be believed, which requires it to be credible, which requires it to be costly to abandon. Most deterrence failures are credibility failures rather than capability failures.

DEPENDENCIES. N1 · Game theory · World History N7.

SUBTOPICS. Deterrence by punishment and by denial · credibility and commitment · second-strike survivability as the load-bearing element · the security dilemma · escalation ladders and thresholds · the stability-instability paradox · red lines and the cost of not enforcing one · signalling and misperception · India's and Pakistan's differing doctrines.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Your own note plus free declassified documents · Title: your Game Theory & Signalling note, applied to declassified crisis documents from the National Security Archive
Why this one: you already hold the theory — commitment beats options, and a claim is worth what it would cost a weaker type to fake — and the declassified record lets you test it against real crises.
HANDS-ON · Work out why a costly, irreversible commitment is stronger than a flexible one Take the security dilemma first: a state increases its defences for purely defensive reasons; its neighbour cannot distinguish defensive from offensive intent and responds; both are now less secure while both acted reasonably. Simulate it as a simple model and watch the spiral. This is a reinforcing loop from Systems Thinking N2, and no participant needs to be aggressive for it to run.

Then examine credibility. Why does a state deliberately tie its own hands — stationing troops it cannot withdraw, making public commitments it cannot retract? Because reducing your own options makes your threat believable. That is Schelling's insight and it inverts ordinary intuition about flexibility.

Then apply the reconstruction method from above to one real crisis using declassified documents. Identify what each side wanted, what constrained it, and where misperception occurred. You will find that both sides frequently misread signals that seemed unambiguous to the sender.

Then compare India's no-first-use posture with Pakistan's declared willingness to use tactical weapons first. Work out what each doctrine is trying to deter and what it commits its holder to. They are responses to different asymmetries, and both are internally coherent.

The realisation to reach: deterrence rests on credibility rather than capability, and credibility is purchased by giving up flexibility — which is why the strongest commitments are the ones that would be most costly to break.

CONNECTS TO. Game theory · Systems Thinking N2 (arms races) · World History N6–N7 · Human Behavior N4 (trust without enforcement).

N5 Technology as leverage

The current front, and the one closest to your professional domain.

WHAT IT IS. The use of control over critical technologies — semiconductors, compute, standards, data — as instruments of state power.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because some technologies have extremely concentrated supply chains with no near-term substitute. Where concentration and non-substitutability coincide, you have a chokepoint — and it behaves exactly like a strait.

DEPENDENCIES. N2, N3 · AI N6 · Electronics N4.

SUBTOPICS. The semiconductor supply chain and where its chokepoints actually are · EUV lithography and single-supplier concentration · export controls as an instrument · compute as a strategic resource · standard-setting bodies and why they matter · data localisation and sovereignty · critical minerals and processing concentration · the difference between mining and refining leverage.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free primary documents · Title: published export control regulations and the free annual reports of key supply chain firms
Why this one: free, and export control texts specify exactly which capabilities are being restricted, in technical detail. Reading the actual thresholds tells you precisely what the controlling state believes is strategically decisive.
HANDS-ON · Map the chip supply chain and find the single points of failure Take a modern processor and map its production chain: design tools, intellectual property cores, fabrication, lithography equipment, photoresists and specialty chemicals, packaging, testing. For each stage, find how many firms and countries are genuinely capable.

Several stages have one or two suppliers worldwide. EUV lithography has effectively one, and no state can substitute it in under a decade at enormous cost. Identify every such stage in your map — those are the chokepoints, and they are where policy acts.

Then read an actual export control document and find the technical thresholds. They are specific: performance limits, feature sizes, interconnect bandwidths. The thresholds tell you exactly what capability is considered decisive, which is far more informative than commentary about intentions.

Then compute the substitution timeline: what would it take, in years and capital, to replicate one chokepoint stage domestically? For fabrication the answers are enormous, which is why this leverage is durable in a way that most economic leverage is not.

Then place India: identify where India sits in this chain today, where its stated ambitions are, and what the realistic constraints are. Design and software capability are strong; fabrication is not, and the reasons are capital intensity, ecosystem depth and water and power reliability.

The realisation to reach: technological leverage follows the same chokepoint logic as geography — concentration plus non-substitutability — which means you already know how to analyse it.

CONNECTS TO. AI N6 · Electronics N4 · World Geography N5–N6 · Business N3 · Infrastructure (compute as strategic infrastructure).

N6 India's position

Applying every previous node to one country's actual situation.

WHAT IT IS. India's structural position — its geography, dependencies, relationships and constraints — and the strategy that follows from them.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because this is where the domain becomes personally relevant, and because India's position involves a genuinely difficult set of trade-offs that are frequently discussed badly.

DEPENDENCIES. N1–N5 · Indian Politics · Indian Geography.

SUBTOPICS. Two difficult land borders and a long maritime frontier · energy import dependence and its diversification · the Gulf relationship — energy, remittances, diaspora · strategic autonomy and what it costs · defence procurement dependencies · the China relationship's economic and security contradiction · Indian Ocean naval position · relations with neighbours and their asymmetries · trade policy and its tensions.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free official documents · Title: Ministry of External Affairs annual reports, joint statements, and India's published strategic and maritime doctrine documents — all free
Why this one: free, and a joint statement is a negotiated text where every phrase was argued over. Comparing two consecutive years' statements with the same country reveals what changed with unusual precision.
HANDS-ON · Diff two joint statements and find what changed Take joint statements issued by India with the same country in two different years, several years apart. Compare them line by line.

Note what was added, what was dropped, and where the language strengthened or weakened. Diplomatic texts are negotiated word by word, so a phrase appearing or vanishing is a signal, not an accident. This is a genuinely powerful analytical technique and it costs nothing.

Then run the full reconstruction method on one real Indian decision: an abstention at the UN, a trade negotiation position, a defence procurement choice, or a border infrastructure programme. Identify the interest, the constraints — energy dependence, domestic politics, existing supplier relationships, neighbour reactions — and the alternatives with their costs. Explain it without reference to anyone's personality.

Then examine the central contradiction directly: India's largest goods trade relationship and its most serious security concern involve the same country. Work out what strategies are available when economic interdependence and security competition point in opposite directions, and what each costs. There is no clean answer, and being able to state the trade-off precisely is the objective.

Then compute the energy exposure: import dependence as a percentage, the concentration of suppliers, and the chokepoints that supply crosses. Then identify the diversification and strategic reserve measures taken, and assess whether they match the exposure.

The realisation to reach: India's foreign policy positions are legible as responses to a specific structural position — and once you can reconstruct them from constraints, you can evaluate them rather than merely support or oppose them.

CONNECTS TO. Indian Politics (domestic constraints on foreign policy) · Indian Geography · Economics N6 · Business (supply chain exposure) · Indian History N6 (non-alignment's legacy).
