Psychology

Psychology studies the machinery producing your experience — and it is the domain where you are the least reliable possible observer, because the instrument being studied is the one doing the studying. That is not a metaphor; it is the central methodological problem of the field.

The single idea. Your mind is not a camera and a filing cabinet. It is a prediction machine that constructs experience and reconstructs memory, optimising for speed and usefulness rather than accuracy. Almost every finding below is a consequence of that architecture.

01 The replication problem, stated up front

This must come before the content, not after it.

A large fraction of famous psychology findings failed to replicate when carefully retested. Some effects were far smaller than reported; some do not appear to exist. This is not a reason to dismiss the field — it is a reason to hold every specific finding loosely and every mechanism more firmly.

Trust levelExamples
Robust — replicated widely, large effectsThe forgetting curve · spacing and testing effects · anchoring · loss aversion · working memory limits · sleep deprivation effects
Contested — mixed or shrinkingEgo depletion · many priming effects · power posing · growth mindset interventions at scale
Treat with suspicionSingle dramatic studies · findings from small samples with surprising results · anything that became a bestselling book before replication
The rule for this domain A surprising psychology result reported once is a hypothesis, not a fact. Ask the sample size, the effect size, and whether it has been independently replicated — three questions that eliminate most of the noise.

N1 Perception and attention

Experience is constructed, not received.

WHAT IT IS. How sensory input becomes experience — and the fact that far more of it is generated by prediction than transmitted from the senses.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because sensory data is noisy, ambiguous and arrives with delay. A system that waited for complete information would be too slow to catch anything. So the brain predicts and uses the senses mainly to correct the prediction.

DEPENDENCIES. Biology N5.

SUBTOPICS. Bottom-up and top-down processing · predictive processing and prediction error · attention as selection and as precision-weighting · inattentional and change blindness · perceptual illusions as evidence of construction · adaptation · the blind spot and filling-in.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Your own note · Title: Predictive Processing overview
Why this one: already written and already framed around the mechanism rather than a list of illusions.

DEEPER (optional) — MIT OCW 9.00SC Introduction to Psychology, free, for the classical experimental grounding.
HANDS-ON · Find your own blind spot and watch your brain lie about it Every eye has a blind spot where the optic nerve exits — no photoreceptors at all. Map yours: close one eye, fix on a mark, and move a second mark horizontally until it vanishes. It will.

Now the important part. You do not perceive a hole. You perceive continuous background, because the brain fills it in with what it predicts should be there. Do it against a patterned surface and watch the pattern continue through a region receiving no data at all. You have just caught your visual system fabricating, confidently, in real time.

Then do a change-blindness demonstration — freely available online — and fail to notice a large change you were looking directly at. Then reason about why: attention is a limited resource allocated by prediction, and unattended regions are not stored in detail.

Then connect it to your work: this is exactly why you miss the obvious bug in code you wrote, and why a second reader finds it in seconds. Your prediction of what the code says overrides what it says.

The realisation to reach: you do not see the world. You see your brain's best current model of the world, corrected occasionally by data — which is also, precisely, why eyewitness testimony is weak evidence.

CONNECTS TO. Biology N5 · AI (perception as inference) · Software Engineering N8 (why you cannot see your own bugs) · World Philosophy (epistemology).

N2 Memory and learning

The most immediately actionable node in the Atlas, and the one whose findings are most robust.

WHAT IT IS. How information is encoded, retained, forgotten and retrieved — and the specific interventions that reliably improve retention.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because memory is reconstructive rather than reproductive, and because the strategies that feel effective while studying are largely the ones that do not work.

DEPENDENCIES. N1 · Biology N5.

SUBTOPICS. Working memory and its limits · encoding and depth of processing · the forgetting curve · the spacing effect · the testing effect and retrieval practice · interleaving · desirable difficulties · why re-reading and highlighting fail · reconstruction and memory distortion · transfer.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Your own note · Title: Metacognition overview
Why this one: already written, and it covers the crucial part — that confidence is computed from fluency rather than from competence, which is why ineffective study methods feel best.
HANDS-ON · Prove to yourself that re-reading does not work Take two chunks of material of similar difficulty from any domain in this Atlas. Study chunk A by re-reading it four times. Study chunk B by reading it once, then closing it and writing down everything you can recall, three times.

Rate your confidence in each immediately. You will feel more confident about A — re-reading is fluent and comfortable. Now test yourself on both after two days. B will win, usually by a large margin.

The method that felt worse worked better, and the method that felt better failed. That inversion is the single most useful finding in this domain, it replicates robustly, and having produced it yourself makes it impossible to ignore.

Then demonstrate reconstruction: write down a detailed memory of an event from years ago. Then find a photo, a message, or another person who was there. You will find confident, specific details that are simply wrong. Memory is not retrieval from storage; it is rebuilding from fragments plus expectation — which is N1 applied to the past.

The realisation to reach: your sense of how well you know something is generated by how easily it comes to mind, which is a measurement of familiarity rather than of knowledge.

CONNECTS TO. Your metacognition note · every other domain in this Atlas (this is how you learn them) · AI N2 (overfitting versus generalisation is the same distinction).

N3 Judgment and decision-making

Where Probability meets the machinery that ignores it.

WHAT IT IS. The heuristics people use to judge probability, value and causation, and the systematic errors those heuristics produce.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because exact reasoning is slow and expensive, so the mind uses fast approximations that are correct in typical environments and wrong in specific, predictable ways. The biases are not defects; they are the cost of the shortcuts.

DEPENDENCIES. N1, N2 · Probability N2, N7.

SUBTOPICS. System 1 and System 2 as a useful simplification · availability · representativeness and base rate neglect · anchoring · confirmation bias · hindsight bias · loss aversion and prospect theory · framing · overconfidence and calibration · the outside view · when intuition is trustworthy.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free paper · Title: Kahneman & Tversky, "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases" (1974), freely available
Why this one: free, short, and the original. Reading the source is better than the many downstream summaries, and it lets you assess the evidence directly rather than accept a list.

DEEPER (optional) — Kahneman and Klein's joint paper "Conditions for Intuitive Expertise: A Failure to Disagree" (free), which is the mature version: intuition is reliable when the environment is regular and feedback is fast, and unreliable otherwise.
HANDS-ON · Calibrate yourself and discover you are overconfident Write twenty factual questions with numeric answers you do not know — populations, distances, dates. For each, give a range you are 90% confident contains the true answer. Then check.

If you are calibrated, about eighteen ranges should contain the answer. Most people get twelve to fifteen. You are systematically overconfident, and you now have your personal number.

Then repeat the exercise a week later, deliberately widening your intervals. Calibration improves quickly with feedback, which is one of the few genuinely trainable things in this node.

Then find anchoring in your own decisions: before your next significant estimate — a project timeline, a price, a capacity plan — write down the first number that came to mind, then your considered answer. The gap between them is usually small, and that is the anchor operating.

Then run the outside view: for your next estimate, ask "how long did similar things actually take?" rather than "how long will this take?". The reference-class answer is almost always worse than the inside-view answer and almost always closer to the truth.

The realisation to reach: knowing a bias exists does not remove it. What works is a procedure — a checklist, a reference class, a written prediction — that operates outside the biased judgment rather than trying to correct it from within.

CONNECTS TO. Probability N2, N7 · Finance N5 · Business (estimation) · Infrastructure N6 (postmortems and hindsight bias) · World Philosophy (rationality).

N4 Motivation and emotion

What actually moves behaviour, as opposed to what people say moves it.

WHAT IT IS. The systems generating drive, reward, aversion and feeling — and their relationship to behaviour, which is looser than intuition suggests.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because behaviour requires something to select among options, and emotion is largely that selection mechanism — a fast evaluation of situations against goals, not an interference with reason.

DEPENDENCIES. N1 · Biology N5.

SUBTOPICS. Reward prediction error and dopamine — signalling surprise rather than pleasure · intrinsic and extrinsic motivation · habit formation, cue-routine-reward · delay discounting · emotion as appraisal · the affect-as-information effect · stress and performance · why willpower models are contested.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Your own note plus a free course · Title: your predictive processing note on reward prediction error, with MIT OCW 9.00SC for the classical material
Why this one: the prediction-error framing unifies motivation with perception, and you have already built it.
HANDS-ON · Find the reward that stopped rewarding Identify something you once found strongly rewarding that now produces little — a game, a notification, a food, a milestone at work. Then explain it with prediction error: dopamine signals the difference between expected and received reward, so a reliably delivered reward eventually generates almost no signal.

Anticipation, not receipt, is where the signal lives — which explains variable-ratio reward schedules, why slot machines and infinite feeds are designed as they are, and why a predictable outcome feels flat even when it is objectively good.

Then map a habit of your own into cue, routine and reward, and try to change it by altering only the cue rather than exercising resolve. Cue-level intervention is far more effective than intention, and it is a structural leverage point rather than a parameter one.

Then measure delay discounting on yourself: would you take ₹1,000 today or ₹1,100 in a month? Then ₹1,000 in twelve months or ₹1,100 in thirteen? Most people switch preference despite the identical one-month gap and interest rate. That inconsistency is real, it is measurable in you, and it is why commitment devices work.

The realisation to reach: motivation is not a quantity you have more or less of. It is a signal generated by a specific mechanism, and you can arrange your environment to produce it rather than attempting to summon it.

CONNECTS TO. Your predictive processing note · Systems Thinking N6 · Business (incentive design) · Economics N1 · Finance N1 (discounting, in a different sense).

N5 Individual differences

The node that requires the most statistical care.

WHAT IT IS. How and why people differ stably — personality traits, cognitive ability — and what the evidence does and does not support.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because differences are real and predictive at the population level, and because this is the area where statistical illiteracy causes the most harm. Group averages say almost nothing about individuals, and forgetting that is how legitimate research becomes prejudice.

DEPENDENCIES. N1–N4 · Mathematics N6.

SUBTOPICS. The Big Five and why it survived factor analysis · why MBTI did not · cognitive ability and its predictive validity and limits · heritability, correctly defined as population variance explained · gene-environment interaction · the overlap of distributions · regression to the mean · measurement error.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free reference · Title: peer-reviewed open-access reviews of the Big Five, read alongside Mathematics N6
Why this one: free, and this node is unreadable without statistics. The statistics are the content here — everything else is a misuse waiting to happen.
HANDS-ON · Draw two overlapping distributions and stop misusing group differences Take any two groups with a reported average difference on some trait — the effect size will be published. Now plot the two full distributions with that separation, rather than the two means.

The overlap is enormous. For a typical published effect size, a large majority of each group falls within the other's range. Compute the probability that a randomly chosen member of the lower-average group exceeds a randomly chosen member of the higher-average group. It will be far closer to 50% than intuition suggests.

A real group difference in means is almost useless for predicting any individual, and seeing the overlapping curves makes that permanent in a way that a verbal caution never does.

Then take a personality test twice, a month apart, and compare. Big Five results are reasonably stable; type-based results frequently flip categories, because forcing a continuous distribution into types puts a boundary through the middle of the population.

Then examine regression to the mean: find any extreme performance — a great quarter, a terrible exam, a rookie season — and look at what followed. It moved towards average, with no intervention required. Then note how often an intervention gets credit for that.

The realisation to reach: this node's findings are population-level and probabilistic, and every harmful misuse of them consists of applying a distribution to a person.

CONNECTS TO. Mathematics N6 · Business (hiring and its weak predictive validity) · Human Behavior · Indian Politics (policy on group differences).

N6 Social psychology

How behaviour changes when other people are present — including imagined ones.

WHAT IT IS. The study of influence, conformity, group dynamics, identity and attribution.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because behaviour is strongly situational, and people systematically underweight situation when explaining others while overweighting it when explaining themselves. That single asymmetry causes an enormous amount of interpersonal misjudgement.

DEPENDENCIES. N3, N4.

SUBTOPICS. The fundamental attribution error · conformity and the classic experiments, with their replication status noted · obedience research and its ethical and methodological criticism · social proof · in-group and out-group formation · minimal group experiments · diffusion of responsibility · norms as invisible infrastructure.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free course · Title: MIT OCW 9.00SC, the social psychology unit — free
Why this one: free, and it presents the famous experiments alongside their subsequent methodological criticism, which is essential in a subfield where the most famous studies are also the most contested.
HANDS-ON · Catch yourself committing the attribution error For one week, whenever someone's behaviour irritates you, write two explanations: one about their character, one about their situation. Then do the same for your own irritating behaviour that week.

You will find a consistent asymmetry: others are late because they are inconsiderate; you were late because of traffic. That is the fundamental attribution error, measured in your own log rather than read about.

It is also the single most useful correction available for working with people, because situational explanations suggest fixable causes while character explanations suggest none.

Then observe norms by finding their edges — not by violating them uncomfortably, but by noticing. Watch how far apart people stand in a queue, who speaks first in a meeting, how loudly people talk in different spaces. Then observe the same in a different setting: another city, another generation, another workplace. The variation reveals that these were choices rather than physics.

Then examine in-group formation: notice how quickly arbitrary groupings — team, floor, language, project — produce genuine preference. The minimal group findings say this happens with groupings assigned by coin flip.

The realisation to reach: most behaviour you attribute to what people are is better explained by the situation they are in — which is systems thinking applied to people, and it makes you both more accurate and more tolerable.

CONNECTS TO. Human Behavior · Systems Thinking N7 (bounded rationality) · Communication · Business N6 (organisational behaviour) · Indian Politics (identity).
