Mathematics

Mathematics is the domain where more self-directed learners are lost than anywhere else in this Atlas — not because it is hard, but because they treat it as a journey with a beginning. It is not. It is a toolbox, and the correct behaviour is to walk over, take one tool, and go back to the problem that sent you.

The single rule for this domain. Never study mathematics for its own sake unless you have decided that mathematics is the goal. Take a branch when another domain has made you unable to proceed without it. The pull table below tells you which branch each domain will eventually demand, so you can recognise the moment when it arrives.

00 The toolbox principle

Why "start from the beginning and go forward" fails here specifically.

Mathematics is taught in a sequence because a curriculum needs one, not because the subject has one. The dependency graph is much sparser than the textbook order implies: you can do a great deal of probability with no calculus, all of discrete mathematics with no algebra beyond school level, and most of linear algebra without either.

The reason this matters is motivational rather than logical. A branch taken because a real problem demanded it is retained. A branch taken because it was chapter three is not — and by chapter nine you will have forgotten chapter three and concluded you are bad at mathematics.

What commander-level does not include Real analysis · measure theory · abstract algebra · topology · differential equations beyond the idea · proof technique as a craft. These are the mathematics of mathematicians, and none of them is on the path to understanding the other 35 domains.

N1 Fluency

The only part of this domain that genuinely must come first.

WHAT IT IS. Comfortable manipulation of numbers and symbols: ratios, percentages, powers, logarithms, rearranging an equation without anxiety, and reasoning about units.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because every other branch assumes it silently. Most adults who believe they "cannot do maths" have a gap here and not in the advanced material — and the gap is usually specific and small.

DEPENDENCIES. None.

SUBTOPICS. Fractions and ratios · percentage change and its asymmetry · exponents and logarithms · orders of magnitude · scientific notation · dimensional analysis · estimation.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free interactive practice · Title: Khan Academy, Algebra 1 and 2 — used diagnostically, not sequentially
Why this one: free, and it has a diagnostic that finds your actual gaps rather than making you restart from the beginning. Take the diagnostic, fix only what it flags, and leave.

DEEPER (optional) — none needed. This node has a floor and you cross it.
HANDS-ON · Estimate things nobody has told you Fermi estimation. Answer these with no lookups, showing your reasoning chain, then check: how many litres of milk does Mumbai consume per day? How many piano tuners work in India? How much does one hour of your laptop's electricity cost?

Then the version that matters professionally: how many bytes does one day of logging produce for a service handling 1,000 requests per second? How many servers would that require? What is that per month at public prices?

Check your answers. You will typically land within a factor of ten, which is far better than most people expect and is enough precision to make almost every real decision.

The realisation to reach: arithmetic fluency is not about accuracy, it is about being willing to compute at all. The people who seem quantitatively sharp are mostly people who reach for a number while everyone else is still reaching for an opinion.

CONNECTS TO. Every domain. Especially Infrastructure N7 (cost), Business (unit economics), Physics (dimensional analysis catches errors).

N2 Functions and growth

The branch that quietly governs how you reason about scale.

WHAT IT IS. The idea of one quantity determined by another, the standard families of such relationships, and — the part that matters most — how differently they behave as inputs grow.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because human intuition is linear and reality frequently is not. Exponential growth, logarithmic returns and power laws all defeat untrained intuition in ways that have consequences in engineering, finance and epidemiology alike.

DEPENDENCIES. N1.

SUBTOPICS. Functions, domain and range · linear and affine · polynomial · exponential and doubling time · logarithmic and diminishing returns · power laws · asymptotic comparison · log scales and when to use them.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free video series · Title: 3Blue1Brown, "Exponential growth and epidemics" plus the log-scale explainer
Why this one: free, and it demonstrates the failure of linear intuition on a case where the stakes were real and public, which fixes the idea more firmly than an abstract treatment.
HANDS-ON · Find where the loser overtakes the winner Plot four functions on the same axes: n, n log n, n², and 1000n. Find every crossover point.

The interesting one is the last: 1000n beats n² only above n = 1000, which means an "asymptotically worse" algorithm wins for every input below that. Now connect it to Computing N7, where you measured exactly this and were surprised.

Then switch to log axes and watch an exponential become a straight line. Take a real dataset — Covid case counts, Moore's law, compound interest, your own service's traffic — and plot it both ways. On linear axes exponential growth looks flat and then vertical; on log axes it looks like a line with a slope you can read off and extrapolate.

Finally, the estimate that changes how you hear news: use the rule of 70 to convert any growth rate into a doubling time in your head. 7% growth doubles in a decade. Apply it to GDP, to a savings account, and to a database that is "only growing 5% a month".

The realisation to reach: the shape of a curve is a claim about the future, and choosing the axes is choosing what people will believe.

CONNECTS TO. Computing N7 · Economics (growth, compounding) · Finance · Systems Thinking (exponential behaviour comes from reinforcing loops) · Probability N4.

N3 Discrete mathematics

If you take only one branch for your professional work, take this one.

WHAT IT IS. The mathematics of countable, separate things: logic, sets, counting, graphs and induction. It is the native mathematics of computing, and almost none of it requires calculus.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because computers manipulate discrete structures. Every data structure is a set or a graph, every program is a logical statement, every complexity argument is a counting argument, and every recursive algorithm is an inductive proof in disguise.

DEPENDENCIES. N1.

SUBTOPICS. Propositional logic, implication and contrapositive · quantifiers · sets, relations, functions · counting, permutations, combinations · the pigeonhole principle · graphs, trees, paths, cycles · traversal · induction and strong induction · recurrence relations.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free textbook + course · Title: Mathematics for Computer Science (Lehman, Leighton, Meyer) — the free MIT 6.042 textbook
Why this one: free, written specifically for people who will use mathematics in computing rather than prove theorems about it, and every chapter is motivated by a computational problem. Chapters on logic, induction, counting and graphs are the ones to take.
HANDS-ON · Find the illegal step in a proof that 1 = 2 Look up the classic "proof" that 1 = 2 and find the exact line where it fails — it is always a division by something that is secretly zero. Then do the same for the "proof" that all horses are the same colour, which fails at the base case of an induction, and for a convincing geometric argument that 64 = 65.

This is the fastest way to internalise that a proof is a chain where every link must hold, and an argument that feels compelling can be broken at exactly one point. It transfers directly to evaluating arguments in statistics, in geopolitics, and in code review.

Then build a graph of something you own. Parse the import statements of a codebase, or the links between your own notes, and construct the graph. Now ask real questions of it: is it acyclic? What is the longest dependency chain? Which node, if removed, disconnects the most? Which nodes form a cycle that should not exist?

You will find a circular dependency you did not know about. That discovery — a real defect found by a graph algorithm you implemented — is worth more than fifty textbook graph exercises.

The realisation to reach: graphs are not a topic, they are a lens. Once you have it, dependency management, social networks, routing, citation, supply chains and family trees stop being different subjects.

CONNECTS TO. Software Engineering N3–N4 · Databases N1 (relations are sets) · Networking N3 (routing is shortest path) · Distributed Systems N2 (happens-before is a partial order) · World Philosophy (formal logic).

N4 Linear algebra

Data as geometry — and the branch that AI made unavoidable.

WHAT IT IS. The study of vectors, the spaces they live in, and the transformations that map one space to another. A matrix is not a grid of numbers; it is a function that moves space around.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because an enormous class of problems can be expressed as "apply a linear transformation to a large collection of vectors", and computers are extremely fast at exactly that. Graphics, machine learning, search, compression and physics all reduce to it.

DEPENDENCIES. N1, N2.

SUBTOPICS. Vectors as points and as directions · linear combinations, span, basis · matrices as transformations · matrix multiplication as composition · the dot product as similarity · projection · determinant as area scaling · eigenvectors as directions that survive a transformation · SVD and PCA as finding structure.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free video series · Title: 3Blue1Brown, Essence of Linear Algebra (15 episodes)
Why this one: free, and it is genuinely the best explanation of this subject in any medium. It teaches the geometric meaning first, so the algebra becomes a notation for something you can already see. Watch all of it — it is short, and it makes everything else in the branch easy.

DEEPER (optional) — MIT OCW 18.06 (Gilbert Strang), free, when you need the computational machinery rather than the intuition.
HANDS-ON · Compress a photograph and watch meaning survive Load a greyscale photograph as a matrix of numbers. Compute its singular value decomposition, then reconstruct the image using only the largest 5, 20, 50 and 200 singular values. Plot all five images side by side.

At 5 you see blurred structure. At 20 the image is recognisable. At 50 it is nearly perfect — using a small fraction of the original numbers. You have just discovered, by measurement, that real data is far lower-dimensional than it appears, and that is the single idea underneath compression, embeddings, recommendation systems and dimensionality reduction.

Then plot the singular values themselves on a log scale. The steep drop-off is the redundancy, quantified. Do the same for a photograph of noise and watch the curve stay flat — noise is genuinely high-dimensional, and that is what distinguishes it from signal.

Finally, the intuition exercise: take a 2D grid of points, apply a matrix, and plot before and after. Try a rotation, a shear, a projection onto a line. Find a matrix whose determinant is zero and watch the plane collapse onto a line — information destroyed, irreversibly. That is what "singular" means.

The realisation to reach: a matrix is a verb, not a noun.

CONNECTS TO. AI (embeddings are vectors, attention is dot products) · Physics · Computing N4 (why matrix layout affects speed) · Statistics (regression is projection).

N5 Calculus

Change and accumulation — needed less often than its reputation suggests.

WHAT IT IS. Two linked ideas: the derivative, which is an instantaneous rate of change, and the integral, which is accumulation over a range. They are inverses of one another.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because most physical laws are statements about rates. It matters to you for three specific things: understanding physics, understanding optimisation in AI, and understanding stocks and flows in systems thinking. If none of those is active, this branch can wait.

DEPENDENCIES. N2.

SUBTOPICS. Limits informally · the derivative as slope and as rate · rules of differentiation · optimisation via stationary points · the integral as area and as accumulation · the fundamental theorem · partial derivatives and the gradient (only if AI).

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free video series · Title: 3Blue1Brown, Essence of Calculus
Why this one: free, and it derives the rules rather than presenting them, so nothing has to be memorised. Twelve short episodes replace a semester of manipulation you will never need.
HANDS-ON · Break a derivative with floating point Compute a derivative numerically: (f(x+h) - f(x)) / h, and make h smaller and smaller — 0.1, 0.01, down to 10⁻¹⁵. Plot the error against h.

The error falls as expected, reaches a minimum, and then rises again. Below a certain h, subtracting two nearly identical floating point numbers destroys all your significant digits — this is Computing N1 arriving inside a calculus problem.

Mathematics says smaller h is always better; the machine says there is an optimum. Find it experimentally, then reason about why it sits near the square root of machine epsilon.

Then do the accumulation half concretely: take a real time series — your electricity meter, a bank statement, a service's request rate — and move between rate and total in both directions. Integrate a rate to get a total; differentiate a total to recover the rate. You are now fluent in the stock-and-flow distinction that most people cannot do, and which is the foundation of Systems Thinking.

The realisation to reach: continuous mathematics is an idealisation running on discrete hardware, and knowing where the idealisation breaks is more valuable than being fast at the algebra.

CONNECTS TO. Physics · AI (gradient descent) · Systems Thinking (stocks and flows are integrals and derivatives) · Computing N1 (numerical error) · Economics (marginal analysis is differentiation).

N6 Statistics

Distinct from probability: probability reasons forward from a model, statistics reasons backward from data.

WHAT IT IS. Summarising data, estimating unknown quantities from samples, quantifying uncertainty in those estimates, and — hardest of all — reasoning about cause.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because almost every claim you will encounter about the world is a statistical claim, and most of them are wrong in one of about six recognisable ways. This branch is largely defensive.

DEPENDENCIES. Probability N3–N5.

SUBTOPICS. Descriptive statistics and what they hide · sampling and bias · estimation and standard error · confidence intervals and their correct interpretation · hypothesis testing, p-values and their misuse · multiple comparisons · regression · confounding · correlation versus causation · the basics of causal inference.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free book · Title: Think Stats (Allen Downey), free at Green Tea Press
Why this one: free, computational, and built on real datasets — so every claim is one you verify by running code rather than accept. It also treats exploratory analysis seriously, which most introductions skip.

DEEPER (optional) — Causal Inference: The Mixtape (Scott Cunningham), free online, when you want to move from "these are correlated" to "does this cause that".
HANDS-ON · Four datasets, identical statistics, completely different truths Load Anscombe's quartet — four small datasets with the same mean, variance, correlation and regression line. Compute all four summaries and confirm they match to two decimal places. Then plot them. One is linear, one is curved, one has a single outlier dragging the line, one is a vertical cluster plus a lone point.

Every summary statistic agreed. Every dataset means something different. Then look at the Datasaurus Dozen, where the same trick is done with a dinosaur.

Now the harder phenomenon: construct Simpson's paradox with real data. Find a case where a treatment appears better in every subgroup but worse overall — or take the Berkeley admissions data, where each department admitted women at an equal or higher rate while the university as a whole appeared biased against them. Reproduce the reversal numerically and explain it.

The aggregate and the subgroups tell opposite stories, and both are arithmetically correct. Which one is right depends entirely on a causal question that the data alone cannot answer.

The realisation to reach: statistics cannot tell you what caused what. It can only tell you what is consistent with what, and the causal claim always comes from outside the data — from a model of the world you brought with you.

CONNECTS TO. Probability N5 · Psychology (the replication crisis) · Economics (empirical work) · Data Engineering N6 (distribution tests) · World Philosophy (induction, evidence).
