Communication

Communication is the discipline of getting an idea from your head into someone else's with acceptable fidelity. It is a transmission problem across a lossy, noisy channel with an intelligent receiver who has their own model of the world — which makes it more like networking than most people expect, and considerably harder.

The single idea. The meaning of your message is what the receiver decodes, not what you encoded. That single reframing does more work than any technique: it moves the responsibility for being understood onto you, and it makes the receiver's existing model the first thing you must account for.
#audience #structure #argument #clarity

00 Why this domain exists

And why it is the highest-return soft domain for a technical person.

Technical work is bounded by whether it is understood, funded and adopted — all communication outcomes. The gap between engineers with identical ability and very different influence is almost entirely this domain, and unlike most social skills it is explicitly learnable and measurable.

N1 Audience

The node everything else depends on, and the one that is usually skipped.

WHAT IT IS. Building an explicit model of the receiver — what they already know, what they care about, what they will do with the information, and what they fear.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because the same content requires completely different construction for different receivers, and because the curse of knowledge makes it genuinely hard to remember not knowing something. Expertise systematically degrades your ability to explain.

DEPENDENCIES. Psychology N1, N3.

SUBTOPICS. The curse of knowledge · prior knowledge and where to start · what the receiver will do with this · framing and how it changes the same facts · the difference between informing, persuading and instructing · reading the room.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free lecture · Title: Larry McEnerney, "The Craft of Writing Effectively" (University of Chicago) — free on YouTube
Why this one: free, and it makes one argument with unusual force: writing is not about conveying your thinking, it is about changing what a specific community of readers values. It is uncomfortable and correct, and it reframes this entire domain in ninety minutes.
HANDS-ON · Explain one thing to three audiences and watch it change completely Take something you understand deeply — say why database isolation levels matter. Now write three explanations, each about 200 words: one for a fellow engineer, one for a product manager, one for your grandmother.

The engineer version can use vocabulary. The product manager version must be about consequences — what breaks and what it costs. The third must be an analogy with no jargon at all.

The third one is where you discover whether you actually understand it. If you cannot write it, your knowledge is stored as vocabulary rather than as mechanism — which is the same test used throughout this Atlas.

Then test them: give the third version to someone genuinely non-technical and ask them to explain it back. Where they falter is where your explanation failed, not where they did. Measure the failure at your end, always.

Then find your own curse of knowledge: list the assumptions each version makes that you did not notice making. There will be more than you expect.

The realisation to reach: there is no such thing as a clear explanation in the abstract. There is only an explanation that works for a specific receiver, and you must choose one before you write a word.

CONNECTS TO. Psychology N1 · Business (selling anything) · Teaching · Software Engineering (documentation and design docs).

N2 Structure

The order in which things arrive determines whether they are understood.

WHAT IT IS. The organisation of a message — what comes first, how it is grouped, and how the receiver is told what shape to expect.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because working memory is small. A receiver who does not know where they are going must hold everything in suspension, and they will not. Structure is a working-memory subsidy.

DEPENDENCIES. N1 · Psychology N2.

SUBTOPICS. Conclusion-first versus narrative order and when each is right · the pyramid principle · grouping and the limits of a list · signposting · narrative structure and why it is memorable · the situation-complication-question-answer pattern · why chronology is usually the wrong order.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free primary documents · Title: well-regarded engineering design documents and incident postmortems published openly by companies — free
Why this one: free, and reading ten real ones teaches structure faster than any book, because you see the same organising pattern solve the same problem repeatedly under real constraints.
HANDS-ON · Reverse-outline something you wrote Take a document you wrote — an email, a design doc, a message that went badly. Now write one sentence summarising each paragraph, in order. That list is your actual structure, as opposed to your intended one.

You will typically find one of three faults: the conclusion arrives last when the reader needed it first; two paragraphs make the same point; or a paragraph does two unrelated jobs. Fix the outline, then rewrite from it. Editing the outline is an order of magnitude cheaper than editing the prose.

Then run the conclusion-first experiment: rewrite one document to state the ask and the answer in the first two sentences, with reasoning after. Send it. Compare the response rate and the number of clarifying questions against your usual. For anything where the reader is busy, the difference is dramatic.

Then find where narrative order is correct instead: when the reader will resist the conclusion, arriving at it too early triggers defence. In that case the situation-complication-question-answer order works better, because the reader reaches the conclusion themselves.

The realisation to reach: structure is not presentation. It determines whether the content survives contact with a limited, distracted reader — and the correct structure depends on whether they are busy or resistant.

CONNECTS TO. Psychology N2 (working memory) · Software Engineering N9 (decomposition is the same skill) · Business (proposals).

N3 Argument

Constructing a case, and recognising a weak one — the goal you named directly.

WHAT IT IS. The structure of reasoned claims: what is being asserted, what evidence supports it, and the unstated assumption connecting the two.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because most bad arguments are not factually wrong — they are structurally broken, and the break is almost always in the unstated warrant linking evidence to claim. Learning to locate that link is the single most useful analytical move available.

DEPENDENCIES. N1, N2 · Mathematics N3 (logic) · Probability N2.

SUBTOPICS. Claim, evidence, warrant · validity versus soundness · necessary and sufficient conditions · common fallacies as named failure modes · steelmanning · burden of proof · falsifiability · distinguishing empirical, definitional and value disagreements.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free reference · Title: the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entries on argument, fallacies and informal logic — free
Why this one: free, rigorous, and it treats fallacies as structural defects rather than as a list of Latin names to deploy in disputes.
HANDS-ON · Find the missing sentence in a real argument Take an opinion piece, a technical blog post, or a proposal you disagree with. Extract its structure: the claim, the evidence, and — the crucial one — the warrant, the unstated assumption that makes the evidence relevant to the claim.

The warrant is almost never written down. Once you surface it, you can usually see immediately whether the argument holds, and you will frequently find that the real disagreement is about the warrant while both sides argue about the evidence.

Then steelman it: write the strongest version of the position you disagree with — better than its author wrote it. This is uncomfortable and it is the actual test of understanding. If you cannot, you do not yet understand the position well enough to reject it.

Then classify the disagreement into one of three types: empirical (we disagree about facts, resolvable with evidence), definitional (we are using a word differently, resolvable by agreeing terms), or values (we want different things, not resolvable by argument at all). Most unproductive arguments are values disputes conducted as if they were empirical ones, and naming the type ends more disputes than winning them does.

The realisation to reach: spotting a weak argument is not about detecting fallacies by name. It is about locating the unstated link and asking whether it holds.

CONNECTS TO. World Philosophy · Mathematics N3 · Probability N2 · Economics (separating mechanism from value judgment) · Geopolitics (evaluating claims).

N4 Writing

The highest-leverage medium, and a thinking instrument disguised as an output format.

WHAT IT IS. Producing text that a reader can absorb with minimum effort and maximum retention.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because writing scales without you present, persists, and — most importantly — exposes the vagueness you can tolerate inside your own head. It is the highest-resolution debugger available for thinking.

DEPENDENCIES. N1–N3.

SUBTOPICS. Clarity as a property of the reader's experience · concrete over abstract · active voice and its exceptions · sentence length variation · cutting · the first draft as thinking · revision as a separate activity · why jargon is usually concealment · technical writing conventions.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free essay · Title: George Orwell, "Politics and the English Language" (1946), freely available
Why this one: free, short, and it establishes the connection this domain most needs: vague writing is usually vague thinking, and sometimes deliberate concealment. Its six rules remain the most useful checklist in existence.

DEEPER (optional) — Paul Graham's essays on writing, free, for the modern version of the same argument.
HANDS-ON · Cut a piece by half and find it improved Take something you wrote of about 500 words. Cut it to 250 without losing any substance. It will feel impossible for the first ten minutes and then become easy.

Inventory what you removed: hedging ("I think perhaps"), throat-clearing openings, restatement, and abstract nouns that could be verbs. Almost none of it carried meaning, and the shorter version is strictly better — which tells you something permanent about your first drafts.

Then run the concreteness test: highlight every abstract noun in a paragraph and try to replace each with something you could photograph. "We should improve system reliability" becomes "the checkout page should not fail during a sale". The second is arguable, testable and memorable; the first is none of those.

Then the honesty test, which is the real one: write an explanation of something you believe you understand, then read it looking for sentences you could not defend under questioning. Every hedge marks a spot where you know less than you thought. Writing found the gap that reading never would.

The realisation to reach: writing is not a way of recording thought. It is a way of discovering what you actually think, and the discomfort of editing is the discovery happening.

CONNECTS TO. Every domain (writing is how you consolidate) · Software Engineering (design docs, postmortems) · World Philosophy (clarity and meaning).

N5 Speaking

Real time, unrevisable, and with an audience whose state you can read.

WHAT IT IS. Delivering ideas aloud — in presentations, meetings and explanations — under time pressure and without the ability to edit.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because most consequential decisions are made in rooms, and because speaking offers something writing cannot: live feedback. You can see incomprehension arriving and adjust, which is a genuine advantage if you are watching for it.

DEPENDENCIES. N1–N4.

SUBTOPICS. Preparation versus scripting · the opening thirty seconds · pace and pausing · reading incomprehension in a room · handling questions, including hostile ones · saying "I don't know" well · visual aids as support rather than script · nerves as arousal rather than fear.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free practice structure · Title: Toastmasters' freely published meeting format and evaluation criteria, usable to structure your own practice with a colleague
Why this one: free, and the value is entirely in the structured feedback loop rather than in the organisation. Speaking improves only with recorded practice and specific feedback, and the format supplies both.
HANDS-ON · Record yourself and watch it once Record a five-minute explanation of something technical. Watch it back once — it is unpleasant and it is the fastest improvement available.

Count three specific things: filler words, sentences that never resolved, and how long you spoke before saying anything the listener could use. Most people take over a minute to reach content, and cutting that alone transforms how you are received.

Then practise the pause. When you would say "um", say nothing. It feels like an eternity to you and is barely noticed by listeners — record it and verify. Silence reads as confidence; filler reads as uncertainty, regardless of the actual content.

Then run the live-feedback drill: explain something to one person and watch their face specifically for the moment comprehension stops. It is visible, it happens earlier than you expect, and continuing past it is the single most common failure in technical explanation. Stop there and ask a question instead.

Then practise "I don't know" three times in real conversations. Notice that credibility rises rather than falls — because it makes your other claims informative.

The realisation to reach: speaking well is mostly subtraction — less filler, less preamble, fewer points, more silence — plus one addition: actually watching the listener.

CONNECTS TO. Conversation · Human Behavior N3 (status in a room) · Business (pitching) · Psychology N1 (attention).

N6 The line: persuasion versus manipulation

The node that keeps this domain honest.

WHAT IT IS. The distinction between influencing someone through means they would endorse if they saw them, and influencing them through means that depend on concealment.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because the techniques are largely the same and only the relationship to the receiver's interest differs. Without an explicit test, this domain drifts.

DEPENDENCIES. N1–N5 · World Philosophy (ethics).

SUBTOPICS. The transparency test — would this still work if they knew you were doing it? · informed versus exploited emotion · dark patterns · framing as unavoidable versus framing as deception · selective evidence · why the receiver's interest is the deciding variable.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free reference · Title: published dark pattern taxonomies and regulatory guidance — freely available
Why this one: free, and a catalogue of documented manipulative patterns is the most concrete available definition of the line. Recognising them protects you as a consumer and constrains you as a builder.
HANDS-ON · Apply the transparency test to your own work Take three interfaces or messages you have built or written. For each, ask: if the receiver could see exactly what I was doing and why, would this still work?

A clear explanation passes — knowing it is clear does not reduce its effect. A well-structured argument passes. An artificial countdown timer fails. A pre-ticked checkbox fails. A deliberately buried opt-out fails.

The test is not whether it works. It is whether it works because of concealment. That single question resolves nearly every case without needing an ethical theory.

Then find three dark patterns in apps you use this week and identify precisely which piece of information is being withheld or which cognitive limitation is being exploited. You will find them quickly, and you will not be able to stop seeing them afterwards.

The realisation to reach: persuasion and manipulation use overlapping tools. The distinguishing feature is whether the influence survives the receiver knowing about it — and that is a testable property, not a matter of intention.

CONNECTS TO. World Philosophy (ethics) · Business N4 (pricing and dark patterns) · AI N8 (persuasive systems at scale) · Human Behavior (the same boundary, restated).
