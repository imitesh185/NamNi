World History

History is not a sequence of events to be memorised in order. It is the record of how the present came to be arranged this way — and the only way it stays in memory is if you enter it through a question you already wanted answered.

The single idea. Chronological order is the worst possible order for learning history. It front-loads the least relevant material and buries the payoff. Work backwards from a present arrangement that puzzles you until it stops being puzzling, and the chronology assembles itself as a by-product.

00 Why this domain exists

And the depth setting, which is unusual here.

Gated byGates
World GeographyGeopolitics · Indian History · Economics N7 · Religion · World Philosophy
Depth setting: mechanisms over dates You need almost no dates. You need the causal mechanisms that recur — how states form, why empires overextend, what trade does to societies that meet, how industrialisation changes power, and why alliances convert local disputes into general wars. A person who knows five such mechanisms understands more history than one who knows five hundred dates.

01 The backwards method

How to use this domain without drowning.

1 · START FROM A PRESENT PUZZLE
    "Why is the Middle East drawn in straight lines?"
    "Why is Taiwan separate from China?"
    "Why does the dollar dominate?"
    "Why is South Korea rich and North Korea not?"

2 · ASK WHAT DECISION PRODUCED IT
    Find the specific arrangement, treaty, war or policy

3 · ASK WHAT CONSTRAINED THE DECIDERS
    Geography, resources, domestic politics, prior commitments

4 · WALK BACK ONE MORE STEP
    What produced those constraints?

5 · STOP WHEN IT STOPS SURPRISING YOU
    That is the correct depth. Not earlier, not further.
The discipline that makes this work Read at least one primary source per topic — a treaty, a speech, a dispatch, a contemporary newspaper. Primary sources are free on Project Gutenberg, national archives and Internet Archive, and they consistently contradict the tidy narrative. They also show you that people did not know how it would turn out, which is the single most useful corrective to hindsight.

N1 Agriculture and the state

Why states exist at all, and why they appeared where they did.

WHAT IT IS. The transition to agriculture, the surplus it created, and the emergence of hierarchy, writing, taxation and organised coercion.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because a state requires a storable, seizable, countable surplus. Grain is all three; foraging returns are none of them. States formed around cereal agriculture specifically, and that constraint explains their geographic distribution.

DEPENDENCIES. World Geography N4.

SUBTOPICS. Domestication and its independent origins · surplus and storage · the appearance of hierarchy · writing as an accounting technology · taxation and legibility · early states as coercive grain collectors · why the earliest writing is inventory rather than literature.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free lecture series · Title: open-access university lecture materials on early state formation, alongside images of actual cuneiform tablets from museum collections — free
Why this one: free, and looking at the earliest writing and finding that it records quantities of barley makes the argument better than any summary. Writing was invented for accounting, not for poetry.
HANDS-ON · Ask why states never formed in certain places Map the earliest state formations — Mesopotamia, Nile, Indus, Yellow River, Mesoamerica, Andes — and identify what they share. Cereal or tuber agriculture, reliable water, concentrated fertile land.

Now ask the inverse question, which is more instructive: where did states not form for a very long time, and why? Highland regions, dense forests, steppes and swamps resisted state control for millennia — because the surplus there was hard to find, hard to seize and easy to hide, and because people could move.

Statelessness was frequently a choice, not a failure — and that reframing is one of the genuinely surprising results in this domain.

Then look at the earliest tablets: they are receipts. Writing appears as a technology for tracking who owes what. Then connect it to Accounting N1 — double entry is the same problem solved again, four thousand years later.

The realisation to reach: the state is not a natural or inevitable form. It is a technology that requires specific material preconditions, and it appeared exactly where those existed.

CONNECTS TO. Accounting (writing as accounting) · World Geography N4 · Indian History (Indus, Gangetic states) · Economics (taxation and legibility).

N2 Empires

How large states hold together, and the arithmetic that eventually breaks them.

WHAT IT IS. The classical empires, their administrative technologies, and the recurring dynamics of expansion, consolidation and fragmentation.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because empires face a recurring problem: the cost of controlling territory rises with distance while the revenue from it does not. That arithmetic, plus the difficulty of succession, explains most imperial trajectories without needing any individual's biography.

DEPENDENCIES. N1.

SUBTOPICS. Direct rule versus indirect rule · roads, posts and administrative technology · sovereignty versus suzerainty · tax-farming and its incentive problems · succession as the recurring failure point · overextension · steppe nomads and settled empires as a persistent dynamic · why empires fragment along pre-existing regional lines.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free primary sources · Title: imperial edicts, administrative documents and travellers' accounts — freely available in translation on Project Gutenberg and Internet Archive
Why this one: free, and administrative documents reveal what an empire actually did day to day. An edict about grain storage teaches more about imperial capacity than a battle account.
HANDS-ON · Compute the cost of an empire's own communication Pick an empire and compute how long a message took from capital to frontier, using the fastest transport available. Roman roads, the Mauryan network, the Mongol relay system, or a sailing empire.

Then compute the round trip. A frontier governor asking for instructions and receiving them might wait months. That single number sets the maximum degree of central control possible — beyond it, delegation is not a choice, and delegated power becomes independent power.

Then apply Distributed Systems N1 deliberately: an empire is a distributed system with enormous latency and unreliable messaging, and its administrative structures are consistency mechanisms. Indirect rule is eventual consistency; direct rule attempts something stronger and costs far more.

Then examine succession: count how many empires fragmented at a succession rather than at a defeat. The proportion is high, and it identifies the single point of failure in the design.

The realisation to reach: empires are bounded by communication latency and by the succession problem, and both are structural. The individuals matter far less than the arithmetic.

CONNECTS TO. Distributed Systems N1 (latency and delegation) · Indian History (Mauryan, Mughal) · Geopolitics · Business N6 (span of control).

N3 Connection

Trade, disease and the consequences of societies meeting.

WHAT IT IS. Long-distance exchange networks, what moved along them, and the transformative effects — intended and otherwise — of connecting previously separate populations.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because trade routes carried far more than goods: technologies, religions, crops, ideas and pathogens. The unintended cargo frequently mattered more than the intended one.

DEPENDENCIES. N2 · World Geography N6.

SUBTOPICS. Silk routes and Indian Ocean trade · the monsoon and sailing seasons · what India exported and imported historically · the spice trade and its margins · the Columbian exchange and crop transfer · disease and immunological asymmetry · the transmission of technologies and religions along trade routes.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free primary sources · Title: travellers' accounts — Ibn Battuta, Marco Polo, Chinese pilgrim accounts of India, and the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea — all freely available in translation
Why this one: free, and the Periplus in particular is a first-century merchant's practical handbook listing ports, goods and prices. It is a commercial document, not a literary one, which makes it unusually reliable about what actually moved.
HANDS-ON · Track one crop across the world and find it in your kitchen Pick a food you consider fundamentally Indian: chilli, potato, tomato, groundnut, cashew, papaya. All of them arrived from the Americas after 1500.

Trace the route and the timeline for one of them. Then find what Indian cuisine used before it — for heat before chilli, long pepper and black pepper. A cuisine that feels ancient and essential is substantially a product of a five-hundred-year-old exchange, and this is one of the most reliably surprising facts available in conversation.

Then run the reverse: what did India export that transformed elsewhere? Cotton textiles, which dominated world trade for centuries and whose disruption is central to industrialisation. Sugar. Spices whose price differentials funded the voyages that reshaped the world.

Then read the Periplus and find Indian ports listed by name with their goods. Several are identifiable today. You are reading a working merchant's notes from two thousand years ago about places you could visit.

The realisation to reach: what feels ancient and native is usually the residue of an exchange — and tracing one item makes the entire network concrete in a way a map of trade routes never does.

CONNECTS TO. Indian History · Indian Geography N6 (cuisine) · Economics N6 (trade) · Biology N6 (disease) · Geopolitics.

N4 Industry

The largest discontinuity in the record, and the origin of the modern power distribution.

WHAT IT IS. The transition to mechanised production powered by fossil energy, and its consequences for wealth, population and power.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because for the first time output per person rose sustainably rather than being reabsorbed by population growth. Everything about the present distribution of national wealth and power traces to who industrialised, when, and who was prevented from doing so.

DEPENDENCIES. N3 · Physics N3 · Economics N7.

SUBTOPICS. Why Britain first, and the contested explanations · coal, and the energy transition · the steam engine and thermodynamics arriving after the machine · textiles and mechanisation · the demographic transition · urbanisation · why the divergence between world regions widened so sharply · later industrialisations and their different paths.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free data + free primary sources · Title: Our World in Data's long-run GDP and energy series, alongside contemporary accounts of factory conditions from parliamentary inquiries — freely available
Why this one: both free, and pairing the aggregate curve with first-hand testimony prevents the two standard errors: treating industrialisation as pure progress, or as pure catastrophe.
HANDS-ON · Plot the hockey stick, then find who it happened to Plot world GDP per capita over two thousand years using free long-run data. It is flat, then it turns vertical. Nothing in human history matches that discontinuity.

Now disaggregate it by region and watch the divergence open. Then locate India on that chart and find when its share of world manufacturing output fell — the timing coincides with mechanised British textile production and the policies accompanying it, which is the single most consequential fact for Indian History.

Then examine the energy argument: plot energy consumption per capita against GDP per capita. The relationship is tight. Industrialisation is fundamentally an energy transition — from muscle and wood to coal — and every subsequent development question is partly an energy question.

Then read one parliamentary inquiry into factory conditions. The aggregate curve and the testimony describe the same period and feel like different worlds. Both are true, and holding both is the mature position.

The realisation to reach: industrialisation was an energy revolution with enormous aggregate gains and severe local costs, and the current distribution of national power is largely a record of who went through it first.

CONNECTS TO. Physics N3 · Economics N7 · Indian History (deindustrialisation) · Geopolitics (the origin of the current hierarchy) · Mechanical N4.

N5 Colonialism

The mechanism, stated precisely enough to be argued about.

WHAT IT IS. The establishment of political control over distant territories and the economic arrangements that followed.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because industrialisation created both the capability — steam, rifles, quinine, telegraph — and the demand for raw materials and captive markets. The mechanisms are specific and documentable, which is what makes this discussable rather than merely contested.

DEPENDENCIES. N4.

SUBTOPICS. The technological preconditions · chartered companies and the transition to state rule · extraction mechanisms: land revenue, monopolies, forced cultivation, home charges · the drain debate and its evidence · deindustrialisation of existing manufactures · railways built for extraction · arbitrary borders and their persistence · decolonisation and the states it produced.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free primary documents · Title: colonial administrative records, revenue settlements, and the reports of official commissions — freely available in digitised archives
Why this one: free, and the administration documented its own extraction in detail because it needed to account for it. Revenue records and trade statistics are the strongest available evidence and were produced by the extracting party itself.
HANDS-ON · Find the mechanism in the accounts Take one specific extraction mechanism and trace it in primary data. Home charges is the clearest: expenses charged to Indian revenues for administration, pensions, and military costs incurred elsewhere. Find the figures in official accounts and compute them as a share of revenue and of trade surplus.

Then trace the loop: India ran an export surplus, but the proceeds settled obligations in London rather than returning as imports or investment. That is the drain, stated as a balance of payments mechanism rather than as an accusation — and it can be argued about on the numbers, which is the point.

Then examine deindustrialisation with trade data: India's share of world textile exports before and after British mechanisation, alongside the tariff structures applied in each direction. The asymmetry is visible in the figures.

Then look at the railway map and ask what it was optimised for. Lines run from interior production areas to ports, with weak lateral connectivity — an extraction topology rather than an integration one, and its consequences persist in today's corridors.

Then read one voice from each side in primary sources — a colonial administrator's defence and an Indian nationalist economist's critique. Both are free. Being able to state each position in its strongest form is the commander-level outcome.

The realisation to reach: colonialism is best understood as a set of specific, documented economic mechanisms rather than as a moral verdict — because the mechanisms are what explain the outcomes, and they are what recur.

CONNECTS TO. Indian History · Economics N6, N7 · Geopolitics (borders and their persistence) · Indian Geography N5 (railway topology).

N6 The world wars

How local disputes became general wars, and what the settlements produced.

WHAT IT IS. The two general wars of the twentieth century, their causes and their consequences for the arrangement of the world.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because the mechanisms are recurrent and worth owning: alliance structures that convert local disputes into general ones, mobilisation timetables that compress decision time, and settlements that create the conditions for the next conflict.

DEPENDENCIES. N4, N5.

SUBTOPICS. The alliance system and chain-ganging · mobilisation schedules and the loss of decision time · industrial war and attrition · the Versailles settlement and the transfer problem · economic causes of the interwar collapse · total war and civilian mobilisation · the Holocaust · the atomic bomb and what it changed · decolonisation accelerated by European exhaustion · India's role and what it cost.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free primary text · Title: Keynes, The Economic Consequences of the Peace (1919), free at Project Gutenberg
Why this one: free, and it is a contemporary prediction that the reparations settlement was economically unpayable and politically dangerous — written before the consequences arrived. Reading a correct prediction made in real time is a completely different experience from reading hindsight.
HANDS-ON · Trace one month in 1914 and find where decisions stopped being possible Take the five weeks between the assassination in Sarajevo and general war. Build a day-by-day timeline from freely available primary documents — telegrams, ultimatums, mobilisation orders.

Find the point at which the decisions became structurally constrained. Mobilisation schedules meant that delaying while an adversary mobilised was believed to be fatal, so the incentive was to move first, and each move forced the next. Individually rational decisions, taken under time pressure created by a technology of railway timetables, produced an outcome nobody chose.

That is a commitment and signalling problem, and it is the same structure as an escalating retry storm — local rationality producing systemic failure.

Then read Keynes on the reparations settlement and identify the mechanism he predicted: a country cannot pay large reparations without running an export surplus, which requires the recipients to accept imports, which they were unwilling to do. The transfer problem — an economic impossibility with political consequences.

Then find India in these wars: the size of its contribution, what was promised, and what followed. It is a direct thread into Indian History.

The realisation to reach: general wars are usually produced by structures — alliances, timetables, commitments — rather than by decisions, which is why the participants frequently reported feeling they had no choice.

CONNECTS TO. Game theory (commitment, escalation) · Distributed Systems N7 (cascade failure) · Economics (the transfer problem) · Indian History · Geopolitics.

N7 The Cold War and after

The arrangement that produced the present one.

WHAT IT IS. The bipolar confrontation, its economic and ideological dimensions, its end, and the order that followed.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because the present international system, including India's position in it, was shaped in this period — and because the reason it stayed cold is a mechanism worth understanding precisely.

DEPENDENCIES. N6.

SUBTOPICS. Bipolarity and spheres · nuclear deterrence and why survivable second-strike is the load-bearing part · the Cuban missile crisis as a commitment problem · proxy wars · the command economy's information problem · non-alignment and India's position · the Soviet collapse and the role of the oil price · post-1991 unipolarity and its erosion · China's rise.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free declassified primary sources · Title: the National Security Archive and the Wilson Center's Digital Archive — declassified documents from multiple governments, free
Why this one: free, and these archives contain the actual cables, transcripts and internal assessments. Reading what decision-makers believed at the time — including how wrong they frequently were — is the strongest available corrective to hindsight.
HANDS-ON · Work out why nobody used the weapons Take deterrence seriously as a mechanism. Work out why a first strike was unattractive, and you will arrive at the answer yourself: because the other side retained a survivable capacity to retaliate. Submarines, dispersal and hardening exist for this reason.

Deterrence rests on second-strike survivability, not on arsenal size — which is why missile defence and counterforce accuracy are destabilising rather than reassuring, since both threaten the other side's confidence in retaliation. That inversion of intuition is the core of the node.

Then read the declassified Cuban missile crisis materials. Note how much was unknown to the decision makers, how close several moments came, and how the resolution involved a concession kept quiet for decades. The tidy narrative and the documents disagree.

Then trace the Soviet economic collapse: plot oil prices through the 1980s against Soviet hard currency earnings, and add the Afghanistan cost. Then add the knowledge problem — a command economy could not price its way to efficient allocation, and forty years of small misallocations compounded.

Then place India: read the non-alignment position in primary speeches and identify what it was optimising for. It is a strategic choice with costs and benefits, and stating both is the objective.

The realisation to reach: the Cold War stayed cold because of a specific technical property of the weapons, and it ended because of a specific economic property of one system — not because of the intentions of the participants.

CONNECTS TO. Geopolitics (this is its immediate prehistory) · Economics N2 (the knowledge problem) · Game theory (deterrence) · Indian History (non-alignment, 1971, 1991).
