Networking

The internet is not a network. It is an agreement between hundreds of thousands of independently owned networks, none of which trusts the others, to forward packets on a best-effort basis with no promises whatsoever. Every reliable thing you have ever used was built on top of that unreliability by software.

The single idea. Networking is the study of what you can build on a channel that may lose, duplicate, reorder or delay anything you send, and that is controlled by parties with their own interests. Reliability, ordering, identity and privacy are all constructed — none are given.
#layering #tcp #dns #tls #latency

00 Why this domain exists

And why it is the domain most often "known" and least often understood.

Most engineers can name the layers and could not explain why a page loads slowly, why a connection hangs rather than failing, or why moving a server to another continent destroys throughput even though bandwidth is unchanged. Those three questions are the real content of this domain.

N1 Layering

The organising idea, and the honest account of where it leaks.

WHAT IT IS. The decomposition of communication into levels, each providing a service to the one above and using the one below, with each layer's data becoming opaque payload for the layer beneath.

WHY IT EXISTS. So that Ethernet does not need to know about video calls and video calls do not need to know about copper. It is the same information-hiding argument as software design, applied to an industry rather than a codebase.

DEPENDENCIES. None beyond Computing N1.

SUBTOPICS. The OSI model as vocabulary and the TCP/IP model as reality · encapsulation · headers and payloads · MTU and fragmentation · where layering leaks — NAT, deep packet inspection, QUIC deliberately collapsing layers.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free online textbook · Title: Computer Networks: A Systems Approach (Peterson & Davie), chapter 1 — free at systemsapproach.org
Why this one: the authors released it free, it is the standard text, and it teaches layering as a set of engineering choices with costs rather than as a diagram to memorise.
HANDS-ON · Peel one packet apart Install Wireshark, capture traffic, and load a single small page. Find one packet and expand every layer in the tree view. You will see your HTTP request wrapped in TLS, wrapped in TCP, wrapped in IP, wrapped in Ethernet — each with its own header, each unaware of the others' meaning.

Now count the bytes. For a tiny request, the headers can exceed the payload. Compute the overhead ratio, then repeat with a large download and watch the ratio collapse.

The realisation to reach: every layer charges rent in bytes, and the design of every protocol above is shaped by trying to avoid paying it repeatedly.

CONNECTS TO. Software Engineering N7 (serialization) · Software Engineering N9 (abstraction boundaries and their leaks).

N2 Link layer and local delivery

How a frame reaches the machine next to you, and how machines find each other with no help.

WHAT IT IS. Getting bits between two directly connected devices: framing, addressing by MAC, contention for a shared medium, and switching.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because everything above assumes a "next hop" exists and works. This is where the abstraction of a link is manufactured out of a physical signal.

DEPENDENCIES. N1 · Electronics (signals and modulation).

SUBTOPICS. MAC addresses · Ethernet framing · collision domains and why switches replaced hubs · ARP · VLANs · Wi-Fi as a shared, lossy medium and why it behaves differently from cable.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free online textbook · Title: Computer Networks: A Systems Approach, chapter 2
Why this one: it derives Ethernet from the problem of sharing one wire, which makes collision detection and backoff feel inevitable rather than arbitrary.
HANDS-ON · Watch two machines introduce themselves Clear your ARP cache, then ping a device on your local network while capturing in Wireshark. Before any ping appears you will see an ARP broadcast — "who has this IP address?" — shouted to every device on the segment, and one reply.

Now notice the security consequence without being told it: anyone on the segment could have answered. Nothing in the protocol verifies the claim. Look up ARP spoofing and see that the attack is simply using the protocol as designed.

Then compare Wi-Fi to Ethernet: ping continuously over both and plot the round-trip times. Cable is tight and boring; Wi-Fi has a long tail — the same heavy-tail shape from Probability N4, arriving from contention for a shared medium.

The realisation to reach: protocols designed for a trusted local wire became the foundation of a hostile global network, and most network security work is retrofitting trust onto designs that assumed it.

CONNECTS TO. Electrical & Electronics · Probability N4 (Wi-Fi latency tails) · Security.

N3 IP and routing

How a packet crosses networks owned by people who have never met.

WHAT IT IS. A universal addressing scheme plus a best-effort forwarding service, in which each router makes an independent local decision about where to send a packet next.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because a single global network under one authority was never politically or technically possible. IP is the minimum agreement that lets independent networks interconnect — it promises almost nothing, which is exactly why everyone could accept it.

DEPENDENCIES. N2.

SUBTOPICS. IPv4 and IPv6 addressing · subnets and CIDR · routing tables and longest-prefix match · interior routing versus BGP between organisations · NAT and what it broke · TTL · ICMP · why the internet has no central map.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free online textbook · Title: Computer Networks: A Systems Approach, chapters 3–4
Why this one: its treatment of BGP explains the internet as a political system with economic incentives, which is the part that makes outages and route leaks comprehensible.
HANDS-ON · Map the path, then find the lie Run traceroute (or tracert) to a server on another continent. You get a list of hops with round-trip times. Look up who owns each hop — you will cross several companies, and the path is a commercial arrangement as much as a technical one.

Now find something odd: some hops show lower latency than earlier ones, or no response at all. Work out why — a router that deprioritises replies to itself is still forwarding your traffic perfectly. Traceroute measures how routers treat traceroute, not how they treat your data.

Then trace to the same destination from a phone on mobile data and compare paths. Different network, different commercial relationships, different route — same destination.

The realisation to reach: there is no route. There is only a sequence of independent local decisions, any of which can change mid-conversation, made by parties optimising for their own costs.

CONNECTS TO. Geopolitics (submarine cables, routing sovereignty, national shutdowns) · Distributed Systems (partial connectivity) · Economics (peering and transit as a market).

N4 TCP and UDP depth

Constructing an ordered, reliable stream on a service that guarantees none of those things.

WHAT IT IS. Two transport protocols. UDP adds almost nothing to IP — ports and a checksum. TCP adds connection setup, retransmission, ordering, flow control and congestion control, and is one of the most consequential pieces of engineering in the world.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because applications want a pipe and the network provides a lottery. TCP is the translation, and its congestion control is additionally responsible for the fact that the internet does not collapse under its own load.

DEPENDENCIES. N3 · Probability N6 (queueing).

SUBTOPICS. Ports and sockets · the three-way handshake · sequence numbers and acknowledgements · retransmission and timeouts · flow control versus congestion control · slow start and congestion avoidance · bufferbloat · head-of-line blocking · why QUIC was built over UDP.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free online book · Title: High Performance Browser Networking (Ilya Grigorik), free in full at hpbn.co — chapters 1–2
Why this one: it is free, and it is the only widely available treatment that connects TCP's mechanisms to observable application slowness, which is how you will actually encounter this domain professionally.

DEEPER (optional) — Beej's Guide to Network Programming (free) to implement it, then the QUIC design documents for how the trade-offs were revisited after thirty years.
HANDS-ON · Add 100 ms and watch throughput die Download a large file from a server and record the throughput. Now add artificial latency — on Linux, tc qdisc add dev eth0 root netem delay 100ms — and download the same file again. Bandwidth has not changed at all. Throughput collapses anyway.

Work out why from a Wireshark capture: TCP will not send more than a window's worth of unacknowledged data, so throughput is bounded by window ÷ round-trip time. Compute that number and check it against your measurement. You have just derived the bandwidth-delay product from an experiment.

Then add 1% packet loss instead of latency, and watch throughput collapse far more violently than 1% would suggest. Look at the congestion window in the capture — it halves on every loss and climbs back slowly. TCP interprets loss as congestion, so a lossy wireless link is treated as a busy network.

Finally, open a second and third parallel connection and watch total throughput rise — which is exactly why browsers and download managers do it, and why the fairness story is more complicated than it first appears.

The realisation to reach: latency and bandwidth are not interchangeable, and you cannot buy your way out of distance. This single experiment explains CDNs, edge computing, and why your database should be near your application.

CONNECTS TO. Distributed Systems (timeouts, backpressure — TCP is a feedback control system) · Systems Thinking (congestion control is a textbook negative feedback loop) · Infrastructure (CDNs exist because of this node).

N5 DNS

A globally distributed, hierarchical, cached database that almost nobody thinks about until it fails.

WHAT IT IS. The system that turns human-readable names into addresses, by delegating authority down a tree and caching aggressively at every level.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because addresses change and humans cannot remember numbers — but more importantly because it provides a layer of indirection that makes load balancing, failover and migration possible at all.

DEPENDENCIES. N3, N4.

SUBTOPICS. The name hierarchy and delegation · root, TLD and authoritative servers · resolvers and recursion · record types · TTL and cache behaviour · why DNS changes "take time" · DNS as a load balancing and failover mechanism · DNS over HTTPS and the privacy argument.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free tool plus documentation · Title: dig with the +trace option, alongside the DNS chapter of Computer Networks: A Systems Approach
Why this one: dig +trace shows you the entire delegation chain in one command. Watching resolution happen live teaches the hierarchy in two minutes.
HANDS-ON · Walk the tree from the root Run dig +trace www.google.com. Read the output as a story: your resolver asks a root server, which says "I do not know, ask the .com servers"; those say "ask Google's servers"; those finally answer. Three delegations, no central database.

Now query the same name repeatedly and watch the TTL count down in the cached answer. Then query an authoritative server directly and see the TTL reset. You have just observed the caching layer that makes the whole system survivable.

Then do the experiment that explains a class of production incidents: find a domain with a very low TTL and one with a very high TTL, and reason about what happens to each during a failover. A long TTL is a cheap system that fails slowly; a short TTL is an expensive system that fails fast. There is no third option.

The realisation to reach: DNS is not a lookup service, it is a cache coherence problem spanning the entire planet — and every "it works for me but not for you" incident is a cache in a state you cannot see.

CONNECTS TO. Distributed Systems (caching, eventual consistency, TTL as a consistency knob) · Infrastructure (service discovery, blue-green deploys) · Geopolitics (name seizure and censorship).

N6 TLS

Privacy and identity on a wire you must assume is hostile.

WHAT IT IS. A protocol layer that provides confidentiality, integrity and — most importantly — authentication of the other party, using asymmetric cryptography to establish a shared symmetric key.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because every packet crosses machines owned by strangers. Encryption alone is insufficient: without identity you may have a perfectly private conversation with an attacker.

DEPENDENCIES. N4 · basic number theory intuition, not the mathematics.

SUBTOPICS. Symmetric versus asymmetric cryptography and why both are needed · the handshake · certificates and chains of trust · certificate authorities as a political structure · revocation · forward secrecy · why self-signed certificates fail · TLS 1.3's round-trip reduction.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free online book · Title: High Performance Browser Networking, chapter 4 (Transport Layer Security), free at hpbn.co
Why this one: free, current, and it explains the handshake in terms of round trips and cost, which connects cryptography to the latency node rather than leaving it as a separate subject.
HANDS-ON · Watch a stranger prove who they are Capture a TLS handshake in Wireshark. Before any encrypted data flows, you can read the certificate in plaintext — including the chain up to a root authority your machine already trusts.

Now inspect your own machine's trust store and count the root authorities. There will be more than a hundred, from many countries. Any one of them can issue a certificate for any domain. Sit with that: your security model depends on all of them behaving, and some have historically not.

Then generate a self-signed certificate, serve a page with it, and watch the browser refuse. The encryption is identical — what is missing is a chain to something you already trusted. Then add your own certificate to the trust store and watch the warning vanish, which demonstrates exactly what the warning was ever about.

The realisation to reach: encryption is easy; trust is the hard part, and it is outsourced to a list of companies you never chose.

CONNECTS TO. Geopolitics (certificate authorities and state interception) · Mathematics (number theory) · Business (the CA industry) · Human Behavior (trust as delegated verification).

N7 HTTP

The application protocol that ate every other application protocol.

WHAT IT IS. A request/response protocol for transferring representations of resources — plus, by historical accident, the substrate on which nearly all networked applications are now built.

WHY IT EXISTS. It was designed for documents. It became universal because it passes through firewalls, is text-debuggable, and has a caching model good enough to build the web on.

DEPENDENCIES. N4, N6.

SUBTOPICS. Methods, status codes, headers · statelessness and how cookies work around it · caching, ETags, conditional requests · content negotiation · HTTP/1.1 head-of-line blocking · HTTP/2 multiplexing · HTTP/3 over QUIC · REST as a set of constraints, and how loosely the term is used.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free online book · Title: High Performance Browser Networking, chapters 9–12
Why this one: it treats HTTP versions as responses to measured problems, so the reason for each redesign is visible rather than asserted.
HANDS-ON · Speak HTTP with your own hands, then stop believing in caching Open a raw TCP connection with nc or openssl s_client and type an HTTP request by hand. A page comes back. The protocol you have used every day for years turns out to be something you can type.

Then investigate caching properly: request a resource twice with Wireshark open. Look for the conditional request and the 304 Not Modified — the body was not re-sent. Now change the cache headers on your own small server and find combinations that produce genuinely wrong behaviour, such as a stale response after a deploy.

Finally, load a page with 50 small images over HTTP/1.1 and over HTTP/2 with 200 ms of added latency. The difference is dramatic, and the reason is head-of-line blocking — a queueing problem, not a bandwidth one.

The realisation to reach: most web performance work is latency and queueing work wearing different clothes, which is why Probability N6 keeps reappearing in this domain.

CONNECTS TO. Software Engineering N7 · Infrastructure (load balancers, CDNs, caching tiers) · Data Engineering (REST and streaming APIs for ingestion).

N8 Latency and physics

The one constraint in this Atlas that no amount of engineering will ever remove.

WHAT IT IS. The recognition that distance costs time, that this cost is bounded below by the speed of light in glass, and that this floor shapes the architecture of every global system.

WHY IT EXISTS. Bandwidth can be bought — lay more fibre. Latency cannot. This asymmetry is the most important economic fact in distributed computing.

DEPENDENCIES. N4 · Physics (speed of light, refractive index).

SUBTOPICS. Propagation, transmission, queueing and processing delay · the bandwidth-delay product · why round trips matter more than payload size · CDNs and edge placement · the latency numbers every engineer should know · why the network is simply the slowest tier of Computing N4's hierarchy.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free online book · Title: High Performance Browser Networking, chapter 1 ("Primer on Latency and Bandwidth")
Why this one: short, free, and it establishes the numbers you will reason with for the rest of your career in about twenty minutes.
HANDS-ON · Measure the speed of light with ping Ping servers at increasing distances — same city, same country, another continent. Record round-trip times. Now look up the great-circle distance to each, and compute the theoretical minimum round trip: distance × 2 ÷ (speed of light in fibre, roughly 200,000 km/s).

Plot measured against theoretical. You will find you are typically within a factor of two or three of a fundamental physical limit — and that no amount of money, hardware or optimisation could close that gap.

Then apply it: compute the minimum possible round trip between Mumbai and Virginia. Now count how many sequential round trips a naive page load makes — DNS, TCP handshake, TLS handshake, HTTP request — and multiply. You have just explained why a "fast" application feels slow overseas, and why every protocol redesign of the last decade has been an attempt to delete a round trip.

The realisation to reach: the correct unit for network design is not megabits per second but round trips, because one is purchasable and the other is governed by physics.

CONNECTS TO. Physics · Computing N4 (the same hierarchy, one level further out) · Distributed Systems (why synchronous cross-region calls are a design error) · Geopolitics (submarine cable routes determine national latency) · Finance (co-location in trading).
