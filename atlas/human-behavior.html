Human Behavior

Where psychology studies the individual mind, this domain studies what happens when minds are embedded in incentives, norms and other people. It is the meeting point of three of your existing tracks — game theory, systems thinking and metacognition — and it is the most practically useful of the five social domains.

The single idea. Watch what people do, not what they say — and then ask what they are responding to. Behaviour is a rational response to a situation, incentive structure and information set that you probably cannot see. Finding that structure explains far more than judging the person does.

The boundary, stated plainly Everything here is aimed at prediction and comprehension — understanding why people do what they do so you can work with them and not be surprised. The moment a technique's purpose is to make someone act against their own interest without their knowledge, it has left this Atlas. That line is not decorative; it is the difference between social intelligence and manipulation.

N1 Revealed preference

The gap between what people say they want and what they choose.

WHAT IT IS. The principle that choices under real constraints reveal preferences more reliably than statements do.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because stated preferences are cheap, socially performed, and often sincere but inaccurate. People genuinely do not have reliable introspective access to why they choose what they choose — a finding that connects directly to Psychology N1.

DEPENDENCIES. Psychology N3, N4 · Economics N1.

SUBTOPICS. Stated versus revealed preference · why surveys mislead · social desirability bias · the intention-action gap · costly signals as credible preference evidence · why price is the strongest preference signal available.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Your own note · Title: Game Theory & Signalling overview
Why this one: already written, and its core claim — a claim is worth only what it would cost a weaker type to fake — is exactly the mechanism that makes revealed preference informative and stated preference not.
HANDS-ON · Audit your own stated versus revealed preferences List five things you say are important to you. Now find the evidence: your calendar for the past month, your bank statement, your screen-time report, your browser history.

Compare the allocation of your time and money against your stated priorities. There will be at least one large discrepancy, and the discomfort of finding it is the point — you are now treating yourself as a subject whose statements are weak evidence.

Then apply it outward, carefully rather than cynically. When an organisation states a value, look at what it funds, what it measures, and what it promotes people for. Those three signals are costly and therefore informative; a values statement is free and therefore is not.

Then find where the principle fails: revealed preference is only valid under real choice. Someone who works long hours may be revealing a preference for work, or may face no alternative. Constraint and preference look identical in the data, and distinguishing them requires knowing the choice set.

The realisation to reach: people are not lying when their words and actions diverge. They are usually reporting a genuine self-image while responding to a situation — and both are real data about different things.

CONNECTS TO. Game theory (costly signals) · Economics N1 · Business (what customers do versus say in research) · Data Engineering N6 (measuring behaviour rather than opinion).

N2 Norms

The rules nobody wrote down and everybody enforces.

WHAT IT IS. Shared expectations about behaviour, sustained by mutual expectation and low-level social sanction rather than by any authority.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because formal rules cannot cover everything and enforcement is expensive. Norms are decentralised coordination — cheap, powerful, and largely invisible until violated.

DEPENDENCIES. N1 · Psychology N6.

SUBTOPICS. Descriptive versus injunctive norms · norm enforcement and third-party punishment · pluralistic ignorance — everyone privately disagreeing while publicly conforming · norm cascades and rapid change · why norms vary enormously across contexts · queue behaviour, punctuality and personal space as observable examples.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free lecture materials · Title: Elinor Ostrom's freely available lectures and papers on self-governed commons
Why this one: free, and Ostrom documented real communities enforcing norms successfully without state or market — which shows norms as functioning institutions rather than as vague social pressure.
HANDS-ON · Map the unwritten rules of one room Pick a setting you enter regularly — an office, a train, a temple, a gym, a family gathering. Spend twenty minutes writing down every rule that is enforced but never stated.

Who speaks first. How long a pause before someone fills it. What you may ask about and what you may not. Where people stand. Who is served first. What volume is acceptable. What clothing signals belonging.

Then compare the same setting across two contexts — the office in Mumbai versus one in another city or country, a family gathering on each side of a family. The differences prove these were choices, not physics, and that alone reduces a great deal of unnecessary friction and judgement.

Then look for pluralistic ignorance: find a norm most people privately dislike but publicly follow because they believe others endorse it. Meetings that everyone finds useless, ceremonies nobody enjoys, working hours nobody wants. These persist because each person's compliance is evidence to everyone else, and they can collapse very suddenly when that belief breaks.

The realisation to reach: norms are load-bearing infrastructure. Violating one is not merely rude — it imposes real coordination costs, which is why enforcement feels disproportionate.

CONNECTS TO. Systems Thinking (norms are structure) · Economics N3 (Ostrom on commons) · Etiquette · Indian Philosophical Traditions (dharma as norm) · Geopolitics (international norms).

N3 Status

The motive people are least willing to name and most reliably act on.

WHAT IT IS. Relative standing within a group, the competition for it, and its effect on behaviour — including behaviour that is materially costly.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because in ancestral groups status determined access to nearly everything, so sensitivity to it is deep and largely automatic. It explains a great deal of otherwise puzzling behaviour, particularly the refusal of materially good deals that lower standing.

DEPENDENCIES. N1, N2 · Biology N7.

SUBTOPICS. Dominance and prestige as two distinct routes · positional versus absolute goods · conspicuous consumption as costly signalling · status threat and defensiveness · why criticism is heard as status attack · zero-sum versus positive-sum status games · humility as a status move.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free primary text · Title: Veblen, The Theory of the Leisure Class (1899), free at Project Gutenberg — the chapters on conspicuous consumption
Why this one: free, and it identified positional consumption over a century ago in terms that describe the present with uncomfortable accuracy.
HANDS-ON · Find the positional good you are buying List your last ten significant purchases. For each, ask whether the value would change if nobody else could ever know you had it.

Some are unaffected — a mattress, a water filter. Others lose much of their value. The second category is positional, and its value comes from relative standing rather than from the object. This is not a moral finding; it is a mechanism, and it explains why positional markets are unwinnable — everyone can improve absolutely while nobody improves relatively.

Then observe status threat in a technical setting, which is where it does the most damage: watch what happens in a code review or design discussion when a suggestion is framed as a correction versus as a question. The identical content produces different responses, because one implies a standing difference and the other does not.

Then distinguish the two routes: identify one person you defer to because they can affect your outcomes (dominance) and one you defer to because they know things (prestige). The behaviours they produce in you are different, and confusing the two is the source of much bad management.

The realisation to reach: status is a real motive operating in you, not merely in others, and a great deal of unproductive conflict is status contest wearing a content-shaped disguise.

CONNECTS TO. Economics (positional goods) · Business (why reorganisations are resisted) · Communication (framing to avoid status threat) · Geopolitics (prestige projects) · Indian Politics.

N4 Trust and cooperation

Why strangers manage not to defect, most of the time.

WHAT IT IS. The conditions under which cooperation emerges and survives among self-interested parties, and the mechanisms — repetition, reputation, punishment, institutions — that sustain it.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because defection often pays in a single interaction and yet cooperation is everywhere. Explaining that gap is one of the genuine achievements of social science, and the answer — repetition plus reputation — is mechanically precise.

DEPENDENCIES. N1, N2 · Game Theory · Probability.

SUBTOPICS. The prisoner's dilemma · one-shot versus repeated games · tit-for-tat and its robustness · the shadow of the future · reputation systems · third-party punishment · trust as transaction-cost reduction · institutions as substitutes for personal trust · why low-trust societies pay a measurable economic penalty.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free interactive · Title: "The Evolution of Trust" (Nicky Case) — free, playable in a browser
Why this one: free, and it is a genuinely superb piece of interactive teaching: you run repeated-game tournaments yourself and watch which strategies survive under varying noise and repetition. Thirty minutes here beats a term of reading.
HANDS-ON · Run the tournament, then add noise Play through "The Evolution of Trust" and note which strategy wins under repeated interaction. Then change one parameter: add noise, so that actions are occasionally misperceived.

Pure tit-for-tat now collapses — one misread action starts an endless retaliation spiral. The strategies that survive are those with forgiveness built in. In a noisy world, occasionally forgiving an apparent defection is not weakness, it is the optimal policy, and you have just derived that rather than been told it.

Then reduce the number of repetitions towards one and watch cooperation vanish entirely. That is why behaviour differs between a neighbourhood shop and a highway dhaba, between a long-term colleague and a one-off contractor, and why anonymity changes people.

Then find the institutional substitute: identify three situations where you trust a stranger completely — a taxi, an online payment, a restaurant meal. In each, locate the institution doing the work: ratings, escrow, licensing, legal recourse. You are not trusting the person; you are trusting a system, and that substitution is what allows a large economy to function.

The realisation to reach: cooperation is not a character trait. It is an equilibrium produced by repetition, visibility and consequence — and it appears or disappears as those conditions change.

CONNECTS TO. Game theory · Economics N3 · Distributed Systems (Byzantine fault tolerance is this problem with machines) · Geopolitics (treaties without enforcement) · Indian Politics (institutions).

N5 Groups

Identity, coordination and the ease with which lines get drawn.

WHAT IT IS. How collective identity forms, how groups make decisions, and how intergroup conflict arises from remarkably thin distinctions.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because humans coordinate through group membership, and the machinery producing cohesion inside a group simultaneously produces hostility across boundaries. The two are the same mechanism.

DEPENDENCIES. N2, N3, N4 · Psychology N6.

SUBTOPICS. In-group favouritism and minimal group findings · social identity · groupthink and its preconditions · diffusion of responsibility · polarisation within groups · coordination versus collective action problems · why diverse groups outperform on some tasks and underperform on others.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free interactive · Title: "Parable of the Polygons" (Vi Hart & Nicky Case) — free, browser-based
Why this one: free, and it demonstrates in five minutes that large-scale segregation emerges from very mild individual preferences — an emergent outcome nobody chose, which is systems thinking applied to society and one of the most important social science results there is.
HANDS-ON · Produce segregation from tolerant individuals Run "Parable of the Polygons". Set individual preference to something very mild — each agent simply prefers not to be an extreme minority in its immediate neighbourhood. Run it.

The result is severe segregation, from agents that are individually almost entirely tolerant. Nobody in the simulation wanted this outcome, and no agent behaved badly. Then try to reverse it: you will find that removing the preference does not undo existing segregation, because the configuration is stable — hysteresis, in a social system.

Then observe minimal groups directly: notice how fast arbitrary divisions in your own environment produce genuine preference — teams, floors, projects, alumni, language. Track your own reaction to someone identified as in-group versus out-group on a dimension you consider unimportant.

Then look for groupthink preconditions in a real decision you witnessed: high cohesion, a directive leader who stated a preference early, insulation from outside input, and time pressure. When those four are present, the outcome is predictable from structure regardless of the individuals involved.

The realisation to reach: collective outcomes are frequently produced by aggregation rather than by intent — which means blaming individuals for emergent patterns both misdiagnoses the cause and fails to fix it.

CONNECTS TO. Systems Thinking N4 · Indian Politics (identity politics) · Geopolitics (nationalism) · Business N6 (team dynamics) · Religion (group cohesion).

N6 Culture

Behaviour transmitted rather than invented, and why it is stickier than anyone expects.

WHAT IT IS. The transmission of norms, practices and beliefs across generations, and the systematic variation in them across societies.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because individual learning is slow and dangerous, so humans copy — heavily, and with a bias towards copying the successful and the prestigious. That copying makes culture a genuine evolutionary system with variation, heritability and differential success.

DEPENDENCIES. N2, N5 · Biology N7.

SUBTOPICS. Cultural transmission and prestige bias · why practices persist without their original justification · dimensions of cultural variation, treated cautiously · tight versus loose cultures · individualism and collectivism as a spectrum with real measurement problems · WEIRD samples and the generalisation problem in psychology · cultural change and its rate.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free paper · Title: Henrich, Heine & Norenzayan, "The weirdest people in the world?" — freely available
Why this one: free, and it demonstrates that a large share of psychology's findings were established on unrepresentative populations. Reading it retroactively adjusts your confidence in everything in Psychology, which is why it belongs here.
HANDS-ON · Find a practice whose reason has been lost Identify three practices in your own family or region that persist without a current justification — food combinations, timing rules, ritual sequences, prohibitions.

For each, investigate the original function. Many have one: food rules that map onto preservation without refrigeration, seasonal timing that maps onto agricultural cycles, purity practices that map onto disease transmission before germ theory. The practice was transmitted intact while the explanation was lost, and it often worked anyway.

Then find one that did not survive the investigation — a practice whose original function no longer exists or never existed. Both categories are informative, and being able to tell them apart is the actual skill.

Then run the WEIRD check on yourself: take any psychological finding you believe is universal and ask what population it was demonstrated on. Then look for cross-cultural replication. Several famous effects — including some visual illusions — vary substantially across cultures, which is genuinely startling and adjusts your priors permanently.

The realisation to reach: culture is accumulated, unattributed knowledge — much of it useful, some of it obsolete, and almost none of it accompanied by its reasoning. Dismissing it wholesale and accepting it wholesale are both errors.

CONNECTS TO. Biology N7 (cultural evolution) · Religion · Indian Philosophical Traditions · Psychology (the generalisation problem) · Etiquette (culture as executable rules).
