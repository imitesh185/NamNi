World Philosophy

Philosophy is what remains when a question cannot yet be settled by measurement — and it is the domain that audits every other domain in this Atlas. Every field rests on philosophical commitments about evidence, causation, categories and value, and most fields do not examine them.

The single idea. Philosophy is not a set of conclusions; it is the practice of making hidden assumptions explicit and then examining them. Its output is rarely a settled answer. It is a sharper question, a distinction that dissolves a confusion, or the discovery that two people arguing were never disagreeing about the same thing.

00 Why this domain exists, and why it is last

It is placed at the end deliberately, and not because it is least important.

Philosophy is far more useful once you have material to apply it to. Having worked through the other 35 domains, you now hold specific commitments worth examining: what makes a model good, what evidence justifies belief, whether a group average tells you anything about a person, what a benchmark measures, and whether a system that produces fluent text understands anything.

N1 Logic and argument

The machinery, already partly held from Communication N3.

WHAT IT IS. The formal study of what follows from what — validity, soundness, and the structures of inference.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because an argument can be persuasive and invalid, or valid and unsound. Separating those cases requires machinery that intuition does not supply.

DEPENDENCIES. Mathematics N3.

SUBTOPICS. Validity versus soundness · propositional and predicate logic · quantifiers · necessary and sufficient conditions · conditionals and their frequent misreading · deduction, induction and abduction · formal and informal fallacies · the limits of formalisation.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free textbook · Title: forall x — a free, open-access introductory logic textbook
Why this one: free, short, and genuinely introductory. Chapters on validity and conditionals are enough for commander-level and can be worked through in a few sittings.
HANDS-ON · Find out how badly people handle conditionals Try the Wason selection task, which is freely described online. Four cards, a conditional rule, and the question of which cards must be turned over to test it. Most people, including most educated people, get it wrong.

Then try the same logical problem in a social framing — checking whether someone under age is drinking alcohol. Suddenly almost everyone gets it right, despite the identical logical structure. That gap is a genuine finding about human reasoning and it connects directly to Psychology N3.

Then correct a specific error you almost certainly make: "if P then Q" does not entail "if Q then P", and it does not entail "if not P then not Q". Only the contrapositive follows. Find three real claims — technical, political, commercial — that commit exactly this error. They are extremely common.

Then practise the necessary/sufficient distinction on real requirements: is passing this test necessary for correctness, sufficient for it, or neither? Most testing arguments confuse the two.

The realisation to reach: logical form is not intuitive, human reasoning is content-dependent rather than form-dependent, and knowing this makes you check structure deliberately rather than trusting that it feels right.

CONNECTS TO. Mathematics N3 · Communication N3 · Psychology N3 · Indian Philosophy N2 (the Nyaya parallel).

N2 Epistemology

What justifies belief — the node most directly useful to everything else you do.

WHAT IT IS. The study of knowledge, justification and evidence, including the question of what more is required beyond true belief.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because you constantly decide what to believe on incomplete evidence, and you have no direct access to whether your beliefs are true — only to whether they are justified. Everything in this Atlas is a claim you accepted for reasons, and this node examines the reasons.

DEPENDENCIES. N1 · Probability N2.

SUBTOPICS. Knowledge as justified true belief and the Gettier problem · sources of justification · testimony and when to trust it · the problem of induction · scepticism and its varieties · Bayesian epistemology · disagreement among peers · epistemic humility and calibration.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free reference · Title: the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entries on epistemology, the problem of induction and testimony — free
Why this one: free, peer-reviewed, and written by specialists. The SEP is one of the best free resources of any kind on the internet and it is the primary resource for this entire domain.
HANDS-ON · Audit your own strongest belief Take a belief you hold with high confidence in a domain from this Atlas. Now trace its justification honestly. Did you verify it, or did you accept testimony from a source you trust?

Almost everything you know is testimony. You have not verified the speed of light, the existence of the countries you have not visited, or most of the mechanisms in this Atlas. That is not a defect — testimony is a legitimate and indispensable source — but it means your epistemology is largely a theory of whom to trust, and it is worth having that theory explicitly.

Then confront induction directly. Every empirical generalisation you hold — every "this always happens" — rests on the assumption that the future resembles the past, and that assumption cannot be justified without circularity. Hume identified this and nobody has satisfactorily solved it. Read the argument and try to escape it; the attempt is the education.

Then apply Bayesian epistemology as a practice rather than a theory: for one contested claim, write your prior, specify what evidence would move you and by roughly how much, then go and find the evidence. Specifying in advance what would change your mind is the single most useful epistemic habit available, and it is precisely what you do in your projects track with written predictions.

The realisation to reach: your knowledge is overwhelmingly borrowed, resting on an unjustifiable but unavoidable assumption — and knowing that produces appropriate confidence rather than paralysis.

CONNECTS TO. Probability N2, N5 · Psychology N3 · AI N8 · Indian Philosophy N2 (pramanas) · every domain's evidence standards.

N3 Philosophy of science

What makes an explanation scientific — a question you now have strong opinions about.

WHAT IT IS. The study of scientific method, theory change, explanation and the demarcation between science and non-science.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because "scientific" carries enormous authority and the criterion is contested. Falsifiability is the popular answer and it is insufficient — real theories are defended by adjusting auxiliary assumptions rather than abandoned on a single failed prediction, and sometimes that is correct practice.

DEPENDENCIES. N2 · Mathematics N6 · Psychology's replication section.

SUBTOPICS. Induction in science · falsifiability and its limits · the Duhem-Quine problem · Kuhn's paradigms and normal science · Lakatos's research programmes · underdetermination · explanation and causation · models and idealisation · the replication crisis as a live case.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free reference · Title: the SEP entries on scientific method, Kuhn, and the demarcation problem — free
Why this one: free, and it presents the positions as an ongoing argument rather than as a resolved history, which is accurate.
HANDS-ON · Try to falsify something and discover you cannot cleanly Take a real theory you rely on and try to specify a single observation that would falsify it. You will find you cannot, cleanly. Any failed prediction can be attributed to the instrument, the conditions, an auxiliary assumption, or an unmodelled factor.

That is the Duhem-Quine problem: you never test a hypothesis alone, you test it together with everything else you assumed. This is exactly what happens in engineering debugging — a failing test does not tell you which of your assumptions was wrong — and recognising it as a general epistemological structure is useful.

Then examine a real case: the replication crisis from Psychology. Was the original work unscientific? It used accepted methods and passed peer review. The failure was structural — publication incentives, flexible analysis choices, small samples — rather than a failure of any individual's method. That is Kuhn's and Lakatos's territory: science as an institution with incentives, not as a pure method.

Then apply it to your own field: benchmark contamination in AI is the same structure. A measure becomes a target and stops measuring what it measured — Goodhart's law operating on the scientific record itself.

Then take a position on demarcation and defend it: is falsifiability necessary, sufficient, neither?

The realisation to reach: science is an institution with incentives, not a method that runs itself — and its reliability comes from error-correcting structures rather than from any single criterion.

CONNECTS TO. Psychology (replication) · AI N7 · Mathematics N6 · Software Engineering N8 (debugging as hypothesis testing) · Business N6 (Goodhart).

N4 Metaphysics and mind

What exists, and the question your professional domain has made urgent.

WHAT IT IS. Questions about the fundamental nature of reality — causation, identity, free will — and the relationship between mental and physical.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because these questions are presupposed by every empirical domain and settled by none. You have already run into personal identity in Indian Philosophy N5, and consciousness is now a live practical question because of AI.

DEPENDENCIES. N1, N2 · Biology N5 · AI N8.

SUBTOPICS. Causation and what it means beyond correlation · identity over time and the ship of Theseus · personal identity and the bundle view · the mind-body problem · functionalism · the hard problem of consciousness · intentionality and whether syntax can produce semantics · free will, determinism and compatibilism.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free reference and free paper · Title: the SEP entry on personal identity, plus Nagel's "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" — freely available
Why this one: both free, and Nagel's paper is short and states the hard problem more clearly than any summary. It is also the paper you need in order to discuss machine consciousness without talking nonsense.
HANDS-ON · Ask what makes a running system the same system Take a service you operate. Over five years every server has been replaced, the code fully rewritten, the database migrated, the team changed. Is it the same system?

You will find you have no principled answer, and that identity here is a matter of continuity of function and of what people call it rather than of any persisting substance. That is the ship of Theseus, and it is the Buddhist analysis of the self arriving through your own infrastructure.

Then take the hard problem seriously using your AI knowledge. You know exactly what a transformer does — matrix multiplications and attention weights. You also know it produces fluent, contextually appropriate language. Now state precisely what would settle whether anything is experienced. Nagel's point is that no amount of functional description answers the question of what it is like — and once you see why, most confident public claims in both directions become visibly unsupported.

Then examine free will using Psychology N4: if behaviour follows from prior causes, in what sense do you choose? Read the compatibilist response — that freedom means acting from your own desires without external compulsion, which is compatible with those desires having causes. Decide whether you find it satisfying.

The realisation to reach: your engineering intuitions about identity, function and causation are philosophical positions you adopted without examining, and this node is where you find out which ones you hold.

CONNECTS TO. AI N8 · Indian Philosophy N5 · Biology N5 · Software Engineering N1 (identity) · Psychology N1.

N5 Ethics

What should be done, and why the three main answers keep disagreeing.

WHAT IT IS. The systematic study of right action — consequentialism, deontology, virtue ethics — and the meta-question of what makes any moral claim true.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because you make consequential choices affecting others, and "I'll decide case by case" is itself an ethical position with known failure modes — principally that it is unstable under self-interest.

DEPENDENCIES. N1, N2 · Indian Philosophy N7.

SUBTOPICS. Consequentialism and the demandingness and measurement problems · deontology and conflicting duties · virtue ethics and character · the is-ought gap · moral realism and anti-realism · moral luck · trolley problems and what they are actually for · applied ethics in technology.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free primary text · Title: Mill's Utilitarianism and Kant's Groundwork — both free at Project Gutenberg, both short
Why this one: free, and they are the strongest statements of two incompatible positions by the people who held them. Reading both and finding both partly persuasive is the correct outcome, and it is more instructive than a survey that presents them neutrally.
HANDS-ON · Find your own inconsistency, then decide what to do about it Take five real decisions you have made — professional and personal. For each, identify which ethical framework your reasoning actually used.

You will find you are inconsistent — consequentialist about some things, rule-based about others, and the switch frequently correlates with which answer you preferred. That correlation is the uncomfortable finding, and noticing it is the entire value of the exercise.

Then apply it professionally, where it is concrete: you are asked to build something with a dark pattern, or to ship with a known privacy risk, or to optimise a metric you know is a poor proxy for the actual goal. Work each through all three frameworks and see where they agree and diverge. Where all three agree, the case is easy and you should act. Where they diverge, you have found a genuine dilemma rather than a failure of nerve.

Then confront the is-ought gap: no set of facts entails a value. Your economics knowledge can tell you a policy's effects; it cannot tell you whether to want them. Separating those is the discipline that makes technical people useful in policy discussions rather than merely opinionated.

The realisation to reach: you already have an ethics; it is implicit, inconsistent and self-serving at the margins. Making it explicit does not resolve the disagreements between frameworks — it means you notice when you are switching to get the answer you wanted.

CONNECTS TO. Communication N6 (the persuasion line) · AI N8 · Business (decisions with externalities) · Indian Philosophy N7 · Economics (positive versus normative).

N6 Political philosophy

Who may rule, on what grounds, and what they may do.

WHAT IT IS. The justification of political authority, theories of justice, and the proper scope of state power.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because the state's authority requires justification — it coerces, and coercion demands an argument. Every position you hold about policy rests on a theory of justice you may never have stated.

DEPENDENCIES. N5 · Indian Politics · Economics.

SUBTOPICS. Social contract theories and their differences · consent and its problems · Rawls's original position and the veil of ignorance · libertarian entitlement theory · the capability approach · liberty, positive and negative · equality of what · rights and their grounding · democracy's justification and its limits.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free reference · Title: the SEP entries on Rawls, Nozick and the capability approach — free
Why this one: free, and reading three incompatible theories of justice side by side is far more useful than reading one. The capability approach is particularly relevant to Indian policy debate and is frequently omitted from Western surveys.
HANDS-ON · Design a society without knowing your place in it Run Rawls's thought experiment properly. Design the rules for a society — its tax structure, its healthcare arrangement, its education system, its property rules — without knowing which position in it you will occupy. Not your caste, income, health, region, gender or ability.

Write your rules before reading Rawls's conclusion. Then compare. Most people, reasoning under genuine uncertainty about their own position, choose considerably more redistribution than they endorse when they know their position — which is either an argument for the method or a finding about self-interest, and deciding which is the philosophical work.

Then read Nozick's objection: if a distribution arose from voluntary transactions from a just starting point, on what grounds is it unjust? The strongest version of that argument is genuinely difficult, and constructing it is required before you dismiss it.

Then apply the capability approach to a real Indian policy: instead of asking about income transferred, ask what a person is actually able to do and be — which reframes education, health and infrastructure as freedom-expanding rather than as welfare. Compare how the three frameworks would evaluate the same programme.

Then connect to Indian Politics N1: the Constitution's Directive Principles are recognisably a substantive theory of justice placed deliberately outside judicial enforcement. Ask why.

The realisation to reach: your political positions rest on a theory of justice you can now name — and naming it makes disagreement with others tractable, because you can locate whether you differ on facts, on the theory, or on its application.

CONNECTS TO. Indian Politics N1 · Economics N3, N7 · Human Behavior N4 · Religion N6.

N7 Meaning

The last node in the Atlas, and the one it cannot answer for you.

WHAT IT IS. Questions of value and purpose in a universe that does not supply them — and the major responses to that situation.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because every other domain tells you how things work and none tells you what is worth doing. This Atlas has spent 35 domains building capability and has said nothing about what to point it at — which is a gap, and it is deliberate, because that question is not answerable from a map.

DEPENDENCIES. N4, N5 · Indian Philosophy · Religion.

SUBTOPICS. The absurd and Camus's response · existentialist freedom and responsibility · Stoic acceptance and the dichotomy of control, with its limits · Aristotelian flourishing · meaning as constructed versus discovered · the relationship between meaning and mortality · why the question resists a general answer.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free primary texts · Title: Epictetus's Enchiridion and Marcus Aurelius's Meditations — both free, both short — read alongside the SEP entry on the meaning of life
Why this one: free, brief, and the Stoic texts are practical rather than systematic. Reading them with the SEP's critical treatment prevents the common error of taking Stoicism as self-help while ignoring its metaphysical commitments and its real limitations.
HANDS-ON · Find where the dichotomy of control fails Apply the Stoic dichotomy honestly for a week: for each thing that troubles you, classify it as within your control or not, and direct effort only at the former.

It works remarkably well for a large class of anxieties, and the improvement is real and quick. Then find where it fails, because it does. Injustice done to others is not within your control and it is not obviously correct to be tranquil about it; a philosophy of acceptance can become a philosophy of acquiescence. Locate that boundary for yourself rather than accepting either the enthusiastic or the dismissive account.

Then examine the absurd directly: the human demand for meaning meeting a universe that does not supply it. Camus's response — neither denial nor despair, but continuing in full awareness of the situation — is one option. Existentialist meaning-creation is another. Religious frameworks supply a third. Compare them on their own terms and notice that this is the one question in this Atlas where "here is the mechanism" does not help.

Then do the closing exercise, which is the only one in the Atlas with no correct answer: you now hold a map of 36 domains. What is it for? Write the answer. Then revisit it in a year and see what changed.

The realisation to reach: capability is not direction. The Atlas can tell you how the world works and what to learn next, and it is structurally incapable of telling you what to want — which is the one question you have to answer yourself.

CONNECTS TO. Indian Philosophy N7 · Religion N1 · Psychology N4 · Biology N7 (no purpose in the mechanism) · the entire Atlas, retrospectively.
