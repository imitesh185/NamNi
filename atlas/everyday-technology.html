Everyday Technology

This is the highest completion-rate route in the entire Atlas, and it is deliberately structured differently from every other domain. It has no topic tree, because it is not a topic — it is a method: pick one object, follow one signal through it end to end, and let the object decide which physics, electronics and computing you need.

Why this works when textbooks fail. A chapter on displays teaches you about displays. Tracing a remote button to a pixel changing colour forces you through infrared, microcontrollers, protocols, processors, video decoding, panel drivers, polarisation and colour perception — because you needed each one to continue. The device supplies the motivation that a syllabus cannot.

00 Why this domain has a different shape

It is an integrator, not a subject.

Everyday Technology sits downstream of Physics, Electronics, Mechanical and Computing. Its job is to make you use all four at once on something you can hold — which is how they become permanent rather than remembered.

Gated byGates
Nothing strictly — it pulls from Physics, Electronics and Computing as neededCars & Transportation · confidence in every unfamiliar physical system
Safety, and it is not optional Do not open anything containing mains voltage or a large capacitor. CRT televisions and microwave ovens can hold a lethal charge for days after unplugging. Trace their behaviour from the outside and from teardown videos instead — the understanding is identical, and free teardowns of every device below already exist.

01 The trace method

Six steps, applied to any device, forever.

1 · CHOOSE ONE STIMULUS      a button press, a plug-in, a spoken word
2 · PREDICT THE PATH         write down what you think happens, in order
3 · IDENTIFY THE BOUNDARIES  where does the signal change form?
                             (light→electrical→digital→network→light)
4 · RESEARCH EACH BOUNDARY   only the ones you could not explain
5 · FIND THE MEASUREMENT     what could you observe to confirm it?
6 · FIND THE FAILURE         what breaks, and what symptom does it produce?
Step 6 is the one people skip Failure modes are where understanding is proven. If you can predict the symptom from the fault, you understand the mechanism; if you can only describe the normal path, you have memorised a diagram.

Why this one: free, and the IR protocol is small enough to understand completely in an hour — which gives you one fully-owned link in the chain, and that is what makes the rest feel tractable.
HANDS-ON · See invisible light, then decode it Point your TV remote at your phone's front camera and press a button. You will see the IR LED flashing — the camera sensor detects near-infrared that your eye cannot. Most phone rear cameras now filter it out; front cameras usually do not.

You have just observed a wavelength outside human vision, using a device you own. Now go further: with an Arduino and a three-rupee IR receiver module, capture the actual bit pattern from each button. Print the timings. You will find a header pulse, then bits encoded as pulse gaps, then an address and a command, then the command's logical inverse as an error check.

Then do something with it: use an IR LED to transmit the captured code and control the TV from your own hardware. You have now read and written a real-world protocol, and the concepts — framing, addressing, checksums, carrier modulation — are the same ones from Networking, arriving through a device in your living room.

Then find the failure modes and explain each: why the remote fails in bright sunlight (IR noise swamping the 38 kHz carrier), why it needs line of sight but bounces off walls, and why pressing a button on one brand's remote sometimes affects another brand's device.

The realisation to reach: the remote is a complete communication system — transmitter, encoding, carrier, medium, receiver, error detection — small enough to understand entirely. Everything larger is this with more layers.

T2 The phone

The densest object most people own.

SMARTPHONE
├── SENSING ......... touch (capacitive grid), accelerometer & gyro (MEMS),
│                     magnetometer, camera (CMOS sensor, Bayer filter), mic
├── COMPUTE ......... SoC: CPU cores, GPU, NPU, ISP, modem; RAM; flash
├── RADIO ........... cellular, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, NFC, GNSS — all EM waves
│                     at different frequencies with different protocols
├── POWER ........... lithium-ion cell, charge controller, power management
└── OUTPUT .......... OLED/LCD panel, haptic motor, speakers
HANDS-ON · Make the sensors tell you what they see Install a free sensor-logging app that exposes raw values. Now investigate three things.

The accelerometer at rest reads about 9.8, not zero. It measures proper acceleration, so sitting still it reads gravity; in free fall it would read zero. Drop the phone onto a cushion and watch the trace confirm it. That is the equivalence principle, observable on a phone.

The magnetometer is confused by your desk. Rotate the phone near a laptop, a speaker or steel furniture and watch the compass heading distort. Then reason about why navigation apps fuse magnetometer, gyroscope and GPS rather than trusting any one — which is Bayesian sensor fusion, running in your pocket.

GPS needs relativity. Look up the satellite clock corrections: special relativity slows the orbiting clocks, general relativity speeds them up, and the net correction is tens of microseconds per day. Without it, positions would drift by kilometres daily. A theoretical physics result from 1915 is a required software patch in your phone.

The realisation to reach: a phone is not one device. It is roughly a dozen independent physical measurement systems, fused probabilistically, and the fusion is where most of the cleverness is.

T3 The microwave oven

A physics demonstration disguised as an appliance.

A magnetron generates ~2.45 GHz electromagnetic radiation. Water molecules are polar, so they rotate to follow the oscillating field, and that rotation is heat. The metal mesh in the door has holes far smaller than the 12 cm wavelength, so the radiation cannot escape while visible light passes freely.

HANDS-ON · Measure the speed of light with cheese Remove the turntable. Place a wide plate of chocolate, cheese or marshmallows inside and heat briefly until the first melted spots appear. They will be evenly spaced.

Those spots are the antinodes of a standing wave. Measure the distance between them — that is half a wavelength. Multiply by two, then by the frequency printed on the oven's label (usually 2450 MHz). You get the speed of light, within a few percent, from a plate of melted cheese.

Then explain the turntable: without it, food sits permanently at nodes or antinodes and heats unevenly. The turntable exists because standing waves have fixed positions and food does not.

Then reason about why metal sparks (conductors concentrate field at sharp edges), why the door mesh works (aperture smaller than wavelength), and why a microwave heats water but not a dry ceramic plate (polarity, not temperature).

The realisation to reach: wavelength has a physical size you can measure with food, and the entire design of the appliance follows from that one number.

T4 The refrigerator

A heat pump, and the clearest everyday demonstration of the second law.

A compressor raises refrigerant pressure and temperature; the condenser coils outside dump that heat to the room; an expansion valve drops the pressure, making the refrigerant very cold; the evaporator inside absorbs heat from the food. Heat is moved from cold to hot, which requires work — that is the compressor, and that is why your kitchen gets warmer overall.

HANDS-ON · Prove your fridge heats your kitchen Feel the coils or the side panel of a running refrigerator — they are warm. Now reason it through: the fridge removes heat Q from inside and does work W to do it, so it rejects Q + W to the room. A refrigerator with its door open is a room heater, which is genuinely surprising to most people and follows directly from Physics N3.

Then find the same machine reversed: an air conditioner is a refrigerator whose cold side faces the room and whose hot side faces outside. A heat pump water heater is the same machine again. Three appliances, one mechanism, differing only in which side you point where.

Then compute the coefficient of performance from the appliance's rating label — heat moved divided by electricity consumed. It exceeds 1, often reaching 3 or 4, which sounds like a violation until you remember you are moving heat rather than creating it.

The realisation to reach: refrigeration does not create cold. It moves heat, at a cost, and the cost is bounded below by the temperature difference you are fighting.

T5 The Wi-Fi router

Where Networking becomes a physical object on a shelf.

A router is a small computer with several radios and a switch. It runs DHCP to assign addresses, NAT to share one public address, a DNS forwarder, a firewall, and radios at 2.4 and 5 GHz with different range and bandwidth characteristics.

HANDS-ON · Explain your own dead zone Use a free Wi-Fi analyser app to map signal strength through your home. Plot it. Then explain every weak spot physically: 2.4 GHz penetrates walls better but is crowded and slower; 5 GHz is fast and clean but stopped by concrete. Find the wall that kills your signal and check what it is made of.

Then look at channel occupancy — you will see your neighbours' networks overlapping yours. In the 2.4 GHz band only three channels genuinely do not overlap, which is why that band is congested everywhere. This is a shared medium contention problem, made visible.

Then log into the router's admin page and find the DHCP lease table, the NAT table and the firewall rules. Every abstraction from Networking N3 and N5 is there as a concrete list you can read. Then run traceroute and identify which hop is the router itself.

Then create a failure deliberately: set a static IP that collides with the DHCP range and observe the symptom. Predicting the symptom from the fault is the test.

The realisation to reach: your home network is the entire Networking domain in miniature, with every layer physically present and inspectable.

T6 The phone charger

The smallest device worth tracing, and a negotiation you never see.

Mains AC is rectified to DC, chopped at high frequency by a switching transistor, passed through a small transformer, rectified again, smoothed and regulated. It is small precisely because it switches fast — from Faraday's law, higher frequency means fewer turns and less iron.

HANDS-ON · Watch two devices negotiate a contract Use a cheap USB power meter — a few hundred rupees — between charger and phone. Watch voltage and current during a full charge cycle.

You will see the voltage change mid-charge, stepping between 5V, 9V and higher. That is USB Power Delivery: the two devices negotiate over a data line, agreeing a voltage neither one chose alone. Your charger and your phone are running a protocol with an offer, a request and an acceptance — it is a handshake, exactly like TCP's.

Then watch the current taper as the battery fills. Lithium-ion charges at constant current then constant voltage, and the tapering tail is why the last 20% takes disproportionately long. Now you know why fast charging claims are always quoted to 50% or 80%.

Then compare cables: the same charger and phone will negotiate lower power over a cheap cable, because the cable's own resistance and its identification chip participate in the negotiation.

The realisation to reach: even the dumbest-looking object in your house contains a protocol, a negotiation and a control loop.

:: The build — phone to TV

The project you originally described, placed where it belongs.

You wanted to build middleware connecting a phone to a television. That project is an excellent vehicle because it forces you across almost every boundary in this Atlas. Here is the ladder, in the order the problems arrive:

RungWhat you buildWhat it forces
1Discover the TV on your networkmDNS/SSDP, UDP multicast, service discovery
2Read its capabilitiesDIAL or UPnP device description, XML over HTTP
3Launch an app on it remotelyHTTP as a control protocol
4Send a media URL and play itMedia formats, codecs, what the TV can decode
5Control playback and read stateStateful protocol, partial failure when the TV sleeps
6Stream from the phone rather than a URLRunning an HTTP server on the phone, bandwidth and latency
7Handle the TV disappearing mid-streamTimeouts, retries, idempotent resume
8Add IR control for the non-networked TVBack to T1 — encode NEC frames and transmit
Why this is a good project and a todo app is not Every rung produces a genuine failure that demands a concept you do not yet have. The TV is an uncooperative, undocumented, occasionally asleep peer on an unreliable network — which makes it a real distributed systems problem disguised as a home automation toy.
