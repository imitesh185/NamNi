Indian Philosophical Traditions

This is philosophy, not devotion. These traditions produced formal logic, systematic epistemology, sophisticated theories of language and meaning, and centuries of rigorous debate conducted under rules — and they are routinely presented as vague spirituality, which is both inaccurate and a waste of the material.

The single idea. The organising question is: what can be known, and by what means? The pramana framework — a formal theory of valid sources of knowledge — sits underneath every school's disagreements, and once you have it, the debates become legible as epistemology rather than as competing assertions.
#pramana #darshanas #vedanta #anatta

00 Why this domain exists

Three returns, and one correction.

The correction first: these traditions include atheistic schools, materialist schools, formal logic, atomism and detailed philosophy of language. Treating "Indian philosophy" as a synonym for mysticism omits most of it.

N1 Sources and authority

Who gets to say what is true, and what may be revised.

WHAT IT IS. The distinction between shruti — texts held to be revealed and unauthored — and smriti — texts of remembered human authorship — and what follows for revision.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because the classification determines authority. Smriti can be revised as circumstances change; shruti in principle cannot — which makes the classification of a text a question with real consequences for law, custom and reform.

DEPENDENCIES. Religion N2.

SUBTOPICS. Shruti and smriti · the Vedic corpus and its layers · the Upanishads as the philosophical turn · the astika/nastika distinction and what it actually marks · the Charvaka materialist school and what its survival in refutations tells you · commentarial tradition as a genre of philosophy.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free reference · Title: the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entries on Indian epistemology and the individual schools — free
Why this one: free, peer-reviewed, and it treats these traditions as philosophy with arguments to be assessed. It is the single best free entry point and it avoids both mystification and dismissal.
HANDS-ON · Notice what the astika/nastika distinction actually marks Look up how schools are classified as astika or nastika. The line is not theism versus atheism — it is acceptance or rejection of Vedic authority.

Samkhya, one of the six orthodox schools, is essentially atheistic. Mimamsa is concerned with ritual and is not centrally theistic either. Meanwhile Buddhism and Jainism are classified nastika for rejecting Vedic authority, not for any position on gods.

The classification is about epistemic authority, not about God — and noticing that corrects a very common misunderstanding and immediately makes the landscape clearer.

Then investigate Charvaka, the materialist school. Almost none of its own texts survive; we know it largely from opponents quoting it in order to refute it. Consider what that tells you about how intellectual traditions preserve and lose material, and how much caution is required when a position is known only through its critics.

The realisation to reach: the fundamental fault line in this tradition is epistemological — what counts as a valid source of knowledge — rather than theological.

CONNECTS TO. World Philosophy (epistemology, authority) · Religion N2 · Indian Politics (personal law and textual authority).

N2 Pramanas

A formal theory of valid knowledge sources — the node that makes everything else legible.

WHAT IT IS. The systematic account of how knowledge is validly acquired: perception, inference, comparison, testimony, postulation and non-apprehension — with schools accepting different subsets.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because before disputing conclusions, these traditions disputed methods. Schools differ primarily in which pramanas they accept, and their substantive disagreements largely follow from that — which is an unusually clean structure for a philosophical tradition.

DEPENDENCIES. N1 · Mathematics N3 (logic).

SUBTOPICS. Pratyaksha (perception) · anumana (inference) and its formal five-membered structure · upamana (comparison) · shabda (testimony) and the conditions for reliable testimony · arthapatti (postulation) · anupalabdhi (non-apprehension) · which schools accept how many and why · the Nyaya analysis of fallacies.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free reference · Title: the Stanford Encyclopedia entry on the Nyaya school and Indian epistemology — free
Why this one: free, and Nyaya's treatment of inference and fallacy is genuinely comparable to formal logic elsewhere. Reading it beside Communication N3 shows two traditions arriving at overlapping analyses of bad argument.
HANDS-ON · Run a modern claim through the five-membered inference Learn the Nyaya structure of inference: the thesis, the reason, the general rule with an example, the application, and the conclusion. The classic instance is the hill, the smoke, and the kitchen where smoke and fire were observed together.

Now run a modern claim through it — a technical claim from your own work, or a statistical claim from the news. Making the general rule and the supporting example explicit forces out the unstated warrant, which is exactly the move from Communication N3. Two traditions, separated by two thousand years and a continent, identified the same weak point in arguments.

Then examine the treatment of testimony as a formal knowledge source. Nyaya specifies conditions: the speaker must be reliable, must know the subject, and must intend to communicate truthfully. That is a theory of when to trust a source, and it is directly applicable to evaluating claims today — including claims produced by a language model, which satisfies none of the conditions.

Then compare which schools accept which pramanas and predict where they will disagree. The predictions largely hold, which is the satisfying part.

The realisation to reach: this is epistemology with formal machinery, and its analysis of inference and testimony is directly usable on problems you face now.

CONNECTS TO. Communication N3 · Mathematics N3 · World Philosophy (epistemology) · AI N8 (testimony without a reliable speaker) · Probability N2.

N3 The six darshanas

Six systems, each with a distinct central concern.

WHAT IT IS. Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, Mimamsa and Vedanta — the six orthodox systems, traditionally paired.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because each addresses a different problem: logic and epistemology, physics and categories, the analysis of reality into consciousness and matter, disciplined practice, ritual and textual interpretation, and the nature of ultimate reality. They are complementary specialisations as much as rivals.

DEPENDENCIES. N1, N2.

SUBTOPICS. Nyaya's logic and epistemology · Vaisheshika's atomism and category theory · Samkhya's purusha-prakriti dualism and its atheism · Yoga's practical discipline built on Samkhya metaphysics · Mimamsa's hermeneutics and philosophy of language · Vedanta's concern with ultimate reality · why they are conventionally paired.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free reference · Title: Stanford Encyclopedia entries for each of the six schools — free, one at a time
Why this one: free, and reading them individually rather than as a survey preserves the distinctiveness. Surveys of "the six schools" flatten them into a list; the individual entries show six different research programmes.
HANDS-ON · Match each school to the modern discipline it resembles For each of the six, identify what modern field it most closely corresponds to. Nyaya maps onto logic and epistemology. Vaisheshika onto natural philosophy and ontology — it proposed atomism and a systematic category scheme. Samkhya onto a dualist metaphysics of mind and matter. Mimamsa onto hermeneutics and philosophy of language, with a sophisticated theory of how words refer and how injunctions work.

Vaisheshika's atomism is worth dwelling on: a systematic theory of indivisible constituents combining into compounds, developed independently and long before the corresponding European tradition. Compare its categories to those in Greek atomism and note both the parallels and the differences.

Then examine Mimamsa's problem, which is unexpectedly technical: how do you correctly interpret an imperative text? It developed rules for resolving conflicting injunctions and for determining scope and applicability. That is statutory interpretation, and the parallels to how courts read constitutional text are real.

Then note the atheism of Samkhya explicitly, and ask why an atheistic system counts as orthodox. The answer is N1: orthodoxy is about Vedic authority, not about God.

The realisation to reach: these are six specialised research programmes with distinct subject matters, and treating them as six flavours of the same mysticism discards nearly all their content.

CONNECTS TO. World Philosophy (parallel developments) · Physics (atomism) · Mathematics N3 · Indian Politics N1 (textual interpretation).

N4 Vedanta's split

Three incompatible readings of the same texts — and a model for how traditions disagree.

WHAT IT IS. Advaita, Vishishtadvaita and Dvaita — three systematic positions on the relationship between the individual self, the world and ultimate reality, all claiming to interpret the same source texts.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because the Upanishads contain statements that pull in different directions, and each school constructed a rigorous reading that made the whole corpus consistent. Reading the same texts and reaching incompatible systematic conclusions is precisely what makes this philosophically interesting.

DEPENDENCIES. N1–N3.

SUBTOPICS. Advaita's non-dualism and the status of the world · maya and its careful meaning · the two-truths structure · Vishishtadvaita's qualified non-dualism and its account of relation · Dvaita's dualism and its arguments · how each handles the same key Upanishadic statements · the devotional consequences of each.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free reference plus free primary texts · Title: Stanford Encyclopedia entries on Advaita and Vedanta, read alongside the principal Upanishads in free translation
Why this one: free, and reading the source statements first and then seeing how each school accommodates them shows you philosophy being done rather than reported.
HANDS-ON · Take one statement and see three systems built on it Take a single Upanishadic statement identifying the individual self with ultimate reality. Then find how each Vedanta school reads it.

Advaita reads it literally — the identity is real and the appearance of separateness is the error to be dissolved. Vishishtadvaita reads it as a relation — the self is real and distinct but exists as an inseparable attribute of the whole, as a body relates to a person. Dvaita reads it as similarity or dependence, maintaining a real and permanent distinction.

Three rigorous systems, one text, incompatible conclusions — and each can explain why the others read it wrongly. This is the best available demonstration that interpretation is constrained by text but not determined by it, and it transfers directly to how you read any authoritative document.

Then handle maya carefully, because it is the most misused term in this domain. It does not mean the world is an illusion in the sense of being nothing. It concerns the status of the world's reality relative to the ultimate, within a structure of levels of truth. Find the precise formulation before using the word.

Then note the practical consequence: Advaita's path emphasises knowledge, while Vishishtadvaita and Dvaita make devotion central because a real distinction is required for a relationship. Metaphysics produced practice, traceably.

The realisation to reach: these are systematic philosophical positions with arguments and objections, not competing sentiments — and the disagreements are as precise as any in philosophy anywhere.

CONNECTS TO. World Philosophy (idealism, monism, personal identity) · Religion N2 · Communication N3 (interpretation and warrant).

N5 Buddhist analysis

A systematic argument, whose load-bearing claim is easy to state and hard to accept.

WHAT IT IS. The Buddhist analysis of experience: the diagnosis of dissatisfaction, the doctrine of dependent origination, impermanence, and the denial of a permanent self.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because it presents a structured argument rather than a revelation: a diagnosis, a causal account, a claim that the cause can be removed, and a method. The load-bearing claim is anatta — no permanent self — and the entire system depends on it.

DEPENDENCIES. N2 · Religion N2.

SUBTOPICS. The four truths as a diagnostic structure · dukkha and why "suffering" is a poor translation · dependent origination as a causal thesis · impermanence · anatta and the bundle analysis · the middle way between eternalism and annihilationism · Madhyamaka emptiness · Yogachara · how rebirth is reconciled with no-self.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free primary texts · Title: the Pali Canon suttas — freely available in translation online, including the discourse on non-self
Why this one: free, and the early discourses are structured as arguments and dialogues. Reading the non-self discourse directly shows the argument being made step by step, which no summary conveys.
HANDS-ON · Try to locate the self, using the argument's own method Follow the argument as given: examine each component of experience — body, sensations, perceptions, mental formations, consciousness — and for each ask whether it is permanent and whether it is under your complete control.

The argument runs that no component satisfies both, and therefore nothing found in experience is a permanent controlling self. Whether you accept the conclusion or not, the argument has a structure you can examine.

Then compare it directly to Hume's bundle theory of the self, reached independently in eighteenth-century Scotland by introspection. Two traditions with no contact arrived at closely similar analyses, which is either evidence about the self or evidence about introspection — and deciding which is itself a philosophical question.

Then confront the hard problem the tradition set itself: if there is no permanent self, what is reborn? The answer — causal continuity without an underlying substance, as a flame passes from candle to candle — is precise and worth understanding whether or not you accept the framework. It is also structurally similar to how you might think about the identity of a process, a river, or a long-running system whose components have all been replaced.

Then note the practical claim: this analysis is offered as therapeutic, on the grounds that grasping at a permanent self is the source of dissatisfaction.

The realisation to reach: this is an argument with premises and a conclusion, offered for examination, and its central claim connects directly to problems in Western philosophy of personal identity.

CONNECTS TO. World Philosophy (personal identity, Hume) · Psychology N1 (the constructed self) · Software Engineering N1 (identity versus composition — the parallel is genuine).

N6 Jain analysis

A theory of partial truth, and one of the most underrated ideas in this Atlas.

WHAT IT IS. Jain metaphysics and, more importantly for you, anekantavada — the doctrine that reality is many-sided and any single statement captures only one aspect of it.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because the Jain tradition took seriously the observation that competing philosophical schools each seemed to capture something real. Its response was a formal theory of conditional predication rather than a claim to have found the one correct view.

DEPENDENCIES. N2 · Religion N2.

SUBTOPICS. Anekantavada — many-sidedness · syadvada — conditional predication and its seven-fold scheme · nayavada — standpoints · the blind men and the elephant as the tradition's own illustration · jiva and ajiva · karma as a physical substance in this system · why ahimsa follows from the metaphysics rather than being merely commanded · the Jain contribution to logic.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free reference · Title: the Stanford Encyclopedia entry on Jain philosophy — free
Why this one: free, and it treats anekantavada as the serious logical proposal it is rather than as a platitude about tolerance. The seven-fold predication scheme is formal machinery, not a sentiment.
HANDS-ON · Apply conditional predication to a real dispute Take an argument you have witnessed where both sides seemed partly right. Now apply the Jain move formally: instead of asserting "X is true", assert "in a certain respect, X is true" and specify the respect. Do the same for the opposing claim.

Frequently both survive, because they were describing different aspects or operating from different standpoints. This is not vagueness — it is a demand that every claim carry its standpoint explicitly, which is a genuine logical discipline and is remarkably close to Systems Thinking N7's boundary problem.

Then trace the ethical consequence, which is the elegant part. If no single perspective captures reality completely, then certainty about your own view is unwarranted, and violence conducted in the name of a partial truth is unjustifiable. Ahimsa follows from epistemology rather than from command — which makes it an argument rather than a rule, and arguments can be examined.

Then note the metaphysical basis of the extended non-violence: karma is conceived as a physical substance accumulating through action, and living beings extend further down than in neighbouring traditions. The practice follows from the metaphysics with unusual directness.

Then find the objection: does anekantavada apply to itself? If every claim is only conditionally true, what is the status of that claim? The tradition addresses this, and following the response is worthwhile.

The realisation to reach: this is a formal theory of perspectival truth with an ethical consequence derived from it — and it is directly usable in evaluating disagreements today.

CONNECTS TO. Systems Thinking N7 (model boundaries) · Communication N3 (types of disagreement) · World Philosophy (relativism and its careful versions) · Human Behavior N4.

N7 Ethics and action

Dharma, karma, and the specific problem the Gita poses.

WHAT IT IS. The ethical frameworks of these traditions — duty as role-relative, action and consequence, and the question of how to act under moral uncertainty.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because these traditions produced a distinctive ethical structure: duty attached to role and situation rather than to universal rules, which is closer to virtue ethics and role ethics than to the rule-based frameworks that dominate Western moral philosophy.

DEPENDENCIES. N1–N6.

SUBTOPICS. Dharma as role-relative and situation-relative duty · svadharma · the four purusharthas and their ordering · karma as consequence and as accumulated disposition · nishkama karma — action without attachment to fruit · the Gita's problem of acting when all options cause harm · moksha as the final aim · the tension between renunciation and engagement.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free primary text · Title: the Bhagavad Gita in a scholarly free translation, read after N4 rather than before
Why this one: free, short, and reading it after the Vedanta schools lets you see which school's reading you are being given — every translation carries an interpretive commitment, and noticing that is part of the exercise.
HANDS-ON · Compare three ethical frameworks on one real dilemma Take a genuine dilemma from your own life or work — a case where telling the truth harms someone, or where a commitment conflicts with a better outcome.

Now analyse it three ways. Consequentialist: which action produces the best outcome? Deontological: which action follows the rule that could be universalised? Dharmic: what does your specific role in this specific situation require, given who you are and what you have undertaken?

The third frequently gives a different answer, and its distinctive feature is that it is explicitly role-relative — what is right for you here may differ from what is right for someone else in the same situation. Note that this is a real philosophical position with real costs, including the historical use of role-relative duty to justify hierarchy.

Then examine nishkama karma precisely, because it is widely misread. It is not indifference to outcomes — you must still choose the right action, which requires caring about consequences. It is non-attachment to the fruits accruing to you, which is a psychological claim about the source of paralysis. Read it as an answer to the specific problem of being unable to act.

Then note the criticism honestly: Ambedkar's critique of role-relative duty as it operated in practice is serious, documented and free to read. Holding both the philosophical structure and its historical use is the mature position.

The realisation to reach: this is a distinct ethical framework with genuine advantages — situational sensitivity, an account of moral development — and genuine problems, and it deserves to be compared rather than either dismissed or exempted.

CONNECTS TO. World Philosophy (virtue and role ethics) · Indian History N5 (Ambedkar's critique) · Human Behavior N2 · Etiquette (duty and obligation).
