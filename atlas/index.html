The Atlas — how 36 domains actually connect

This is the navigation layer. It does not teach anything. It answers four questions that otherwise cost you more time than the learning does: what exists, what genuinely gates what, where to enter, and what a thing connects to once you have it.

The claim this map makes. Most of the perceived difficulty of learning broadly is not difficulty at all — it is bad ordering and invented prerequisites. People believe they need calculus before probability, physics before electronics, and economics before accounting. All three are false, and each one costs months. The dependency arrows below are the real ones.
#dependency-graph #free-resources-only #hands-on-per-node #no-schedule

00 What this is, and what it is not

A territory to travel, not a course to complete.

Each of the 36 domains gets its own document. This page is the index that makes them navigable — it holds the arrows between domains, which is the part that would otherwise be lost.

This map doesThis map does not
Say what exists inside a fieldTest whether you learned it
Show which arrows are realSchedule when you travel them
Name one good free resource per nodeOffer ten alternatives
Attach a hands-on experience that makes the concept necessaryAttach exercises that merely demonstrate it
Point out where the domain touches anotherTrack your progress through it
The target is commander-level, not specialist-level Enough to know what a thing is, why it exists, roughly how it works, its components, its trade-offs, and what it touches. Enough to follow a conversation, ask a real question, spot a weak argument, and know what you would need in order to go deeper. Two domains break this rule and go much deeper: Software Engineering and Data Engineering.

01 How to read a node

Every significant node in every domain document carries this shape.

TOPIC
WHAT IT IS          one paragraph, no jargon
WHY IT EXISTS       the problem that forced it into being
DEPENDENCIES        what must be roughly understood first — and nothing more
SUBTOPICS           the branches beneath it
PRIMARY RESOURCE    type · title · creator · why this one
DEEPER (optional)   only if you decide to descend
HANDS-ON            the phenomenon, investigation, build, or observation
CONNECTS TO         the arrows out, into other domains
The hands-on rule A hands-on experience qualifies only if it makes you arrive at “so that is why this exists”. It is a vehicle, never a portfolio piece. For a physical domain it may be taking something apart; for geopolitics, reconstructing a real decision from incentives; for psychology, a structured observation. The form follows the subject, not the habit of writing code.

02 The two lenses

These two have no prerequisites and multiply the value of every other domain. They are not a cluster — they sit above all six.

Systems Thinking

Stocks, flows, feedback, delays, leverage points. Turns every other domain from a list of facts into a machine with behaviour. You already have a working note on this.

Why first: once you can see a feedback loop, you see it in central banking, congestion control, hormones, arms races, and hiring — five domains for the price of one.

Probability & Decision Making

Uncertainty, base rates, expectation, tails. The grammar of every claim about the future.

Why first: without it you cannot evaluate a medical statistic, a benchmark, a forecast, a risk, or a p99 — and you will confidently believe things that are wrong.

03 Real gates vs fake gates

Invented prerequisites are the single largest cost in self-directed learning. Here are the ones worth deleting.

The beliefThe reality
Calculus before probabilityFalse. Discrete probability plus simulation covers most of what you need. Calculus enters only for continuous distributions, and later than you think.
Physics before electronicsMostly false. Ohm's law, circuits and components need almost no formal physics. Electromagnetic theory matters only at antennas and transmission lines.
Mathematics before computingMostly false. You need binary and logic. Most working software touches no calculus at all.
Finance before accountingBackwards. Accounting is upstream. This inversion is the most expensive one on the list.
Macroeconomics before businessFalse. Unit economics needs arithmetic and a real filing, not GDP.
History in chronological orderFalse and counterproductive. Start from a present event you care about and walk backwards until it stops surprising you.
OS before networkingFalse. Siblings, not parent and child. Either order works.
Linear algebra before machine learningTrue for understanding, false for using. Decide which you want before spending the months.
Biology before psychologyLight dependency only. Neuroscience basics suffice; you do not need the Krebs cycle.
Scripture in the original languageFalse for commander-level. Good translations plus commentary get you the argument. Language study is a different project.
You must finish a domain before movingFalse, and the most damaging belief here. None of these are finishable. You leave when the marginal return drops, and you return when another domain drags you back.

Why this section exists Breadth is only affordable because of what it refuses. Every one of these is a well people fall into and do not come out of for a year. Knowing they are optional is worth more than any single resource on this page.

06 Entry routes

Three honest ways in. None is a schedule; each is a direction of travel.

Route A · Professional gravity

Start where you already have traction and let it pull the rest in.

Software Engineering → Operating Systems → Networking → Databases
    → Distributed Systems → Infrastructure → Data Engineering
         ↑ pulling in Probability exactly when a tail or a queue demands it
         ↑ pulling in Business when cost models start to matter

Best if: you want depth to compound and breadth to arrive as a by-product.

Route B · Curiosity, through a real object

Pick a physical thing and follow one signal all the way through it.

Everyday Technology (one device)
    → Electronics (what the components do)
    → Computing (what the processor does)
    → Networking (how it talks)
    → Physics (only where you get stuck)

Best if: you want the physical cluster and would abandon a textbook. This is the highest completion-rate route in the Atlas.

Route C · Present event, walked backwards

For the entire Place & Power cluster, chronological order is the worst possible order.

One real event you care about
    → the incentives of each actor
    → the geography and resources that constrained them
    → the history that produced those constraints
    → the economics that priced them
    → the philosophy or religion that framed them

Best if: you want geopolitics, history and economics to stick. They only stick when they explain something you already wanted to understand.

The honest recommendation Run Route A as the spine, because it is where your existing traction is and depth there is worth the most. Enter every other cluster through Route B or C — never by starting at chapter one of a field you have no relationship with.
