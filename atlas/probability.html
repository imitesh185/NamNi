Probability & Decision Making

This is one of two lenses in the Atlas — a domain with almost no prerequisites that multiplies the value of every other one. Without it you cannot evaluate a medical statistic, a benchmark, a forecast, a risk, a p99, or a claim about the future — and you will believe wrong things with total confidence.

The single idea. Human intuition about uncertainty is not merely imperfect, it is systematically and predictably wrong in specific directions. Probability is not a refinement of intuition; it is a replacement for it. Every node below is organised around one place where your intuition will produce a confident wrong answer.
#simulate-first #base-rates #heavy-tails #expected-value

00 Why this is a lens, and how to learn it

Starting from scratch is not a disadvantage here. Starting from formulas is.

Most probability instruction fails because it presents closed-form solutions to problems the learner has never felt. The order that works is the reverse:

PHENOMENON  →  wrong intuition
     ↓
SIMULATION  →  10,000 trials, count the outcomes, believe the number
     ↓
FORMULA     →  now derive it, and check it matches your simulation
     ↓
TRANSFER    →  find the same structure in another domain
The rule for this entire domain If you cannot simulate it, you do not understand it yet. A simulation you wrote is evidence. A formula you copied is not. This also means you need almost no mathematics to begin — a loop and a counter is enough for the first four nodes.

N1 Sample spaces and counting

Enumerating what could happen — the boring node that prevents most errors.

WHAT IT IS. The discipline of writing down the complete set of possible outcomes before reasoning about any of them, and counting those outcomes correctly.

WHY IT EXISTS. Almost every classic probability paradox dissolves the moment the sample space is written out honestly. The paradoxes are not about probability — they are about people quietly using the wrong sample space.

DEPENDENCIES. None beyond arithmetic.

SUBTOPICS. Outcomes and events · equally likely outcomes and when that assumption fails · permutations and combinations · complementary counting · inclusion–exclusion.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free interactive site · Title: Seeing Theory (Brown University), chapter 1
Why this one: free, visual, and it shows outcome spaces as things you manipulate rather than describe. Twenty minutes here saves a great deal of confusion later.

DEEPER (optional) — Harvard Stat 110 (Blitzstein), lectures 1–3, free on YouTube.
HANDS-ON · Monty Hall, and refusing to believe it Three doors, one prize. You pick one. The host — who knows where the prize is — opens a different door revealing no prize, and offers you the switch. Should you?

Commit your answer in writing first. Then simulate it ten thousand times and count. Switching wins two-thirds of the time. Most people, including mathematicians historically, refuse to accept this until they see their own counter.

Then the part that actually teaches the node: change the rules so the host opens a door at random and it happens to contain no prize. Simulate again. Now switching gains you nothing. The visible situation is identical; only the host's knowledge differs.

The realisation to reach: the probability was never in the doors. It was in the procedure that generated the information. This is the most transferable idea in the domain — it applies to survey design, medical testing, A/B tests, and every statistic you will ever be quoted.

CONNECTS TO. Psychology (why the intuition fails) · Software Engineering (test case enumeration) · Mathematics (combinatorics).

N2 Conditioning and Bayes depth

How evidence should change belief — and the single most expensive error in human reasoning.

WHAT IT IS. Conditional probability is the probability of something given that you know something else. Bayes' theorem is the rule for updating a prior belief when evidence arrives.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because evidence is almost never conclusive, and the correct amount to update depends on how common the thing was before you saw the evidence. Ignoring that prior — base rate neglect — is the most consequential reasoning error there is, and it is committed daily by doctors, journalists, hiring managers and engineers reading dashboards.

DEPENDENCIES. N1.

SUBTOPICS. Conditional probability · independence, and why it is rarer than assumed · the law of total probability · Bayes' theorem · prior, likelihood, posterior · base rate neglect · the prosecutor's fallacy · P(A|B) is not P(B|A).

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free book · Title: Think Bayes, Allen Downey (free at Green Tea Press), chapters 1–3
Why this one: it teaches Bayes computationally — you write code that counts, and the theorem emerges from the counting. This matches how you learn, and it means you never have to trust a formula you cannot check.

DEEPER (optional) — 3Blue1Brown's Bayes theorem video for the geometric intuition, then Stat 110 lectures 4–6 for the formal treatment.
HANDS-ON · The test that is right 99% of the time and usually wrong A disease affects 1 in 10,000 people. A test correctly identifies 99% of those who have it, and gives a false positive for only 1% of those who do not. You test positive.

Write down your estimate of the probability you have the disease before computing anything. Most people say around 99%. Now simulate: generate a million people, assign the disease at the base rate, run the test, and count how many positive results actually have it. The answer is under 1%.

The simulation makes the reason unavoidable: there are 100 true positives and roughly 10,000 false ones, because the healthy group is enormous. Then vary the base rate upward and find the point where a positive result becomes more likely true than false.

Then apply it to your own field: an alert that fires on 1% of healthy deploys, in a system where 1 in 500 deploys is genuinely bad. Compute the fraction of pages that are noise. This is why on-call engineers stop trusting alerts — the mathematics guarantees it.

The realisation to reach: accuracy is not a property of a test. It is a property of a test applied to a population, and the population usually matters more than the test.

CONNECTS TO. Psychology (base rate neglect as cognitive bias) · Distributed Systems (alerting and failure detection) · AI (classifiers, precision and recall) · Business (screening and hiring) · World Philosophy (evidence and induction).

N3 Expectation and variance

The long-run average, and the far more important question of how far from it you land.

WHAT IT IS. Expectation is the probability-weighted average outcome. Variance measures spread around it. Together they are the minimum vocabulary for describing an uncertain quantity.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because decisions need a single number to compare — and because that single number is dangerous on its own. An expectation without a spread is one of the most misleading objects in quantitative reasoning.

DEPENDENCIES. N1, N2.

SUBTOPICS. Random variables · expectation and its linearity · variance and standard deviation · covariance and correlation · why linearity of expectation works even under dependence.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free book · Title: Think Stats, Allen Downey (free at Green Tea Press), chapters 1–4
Why this one: it works from real datasets rather than urns and dice, so summary statistics stay attached to something you can inspect and question.
HANDS-ON · The bet with infinite value that nobody wants A coin is flipped until it comes up heads. If that takes n flips, you are paid 2n rupees. Compute the expected payout — it is infinite. Now ask yourself honestly what you would pay to play. Almost nobody says more than a few hundred rupees.

Simulate a million games and look at the distribution of actual payouts. The median is tiny; the mean is dragged upward by outcomes that essentially never occur. Then plot the running average across increasing numbers of games — it does not settle. It jumps upward at unpredictable moments and drifts down in between.

The realisation to reach: an expected value is only a useful summary when the distribution is well-behaved enough for it to be typical — and “typical” is exactly what the mean stops being when the tail is heavy. This is the doorway into N4, and it is also, precisely, the reason your p99 latency matters more than your average.

CONNECTS TO. Finance (risk and return) · N4 · Distributed Systems (latency) · Economics (utility, and why it was invented to solve this exact paradox).

N4 Distributions and tails depth

The shapes uncertainty takes — and the one shape that breaks everything.

WHAT IT IS. A distribution describes how probability is spread across possible values. A small number of named shapes cover most real phenomena, and the differences between them determine which summary statistics mean anything.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because “random” is not one behaviour. Waiting times, counts, sums, extremes and multiplicative processes each produce a characteristic shape, and using the wrong mental default — usually the bell curve — produces confident, catastrophic errors.

DEPENDENCIES. N3.

SUBTOPICS. Uniform · Bernoulli and binomial · Poisson and what it counts · exponential and memorylessness · normal and where it legitimately arises · lognormal · power laws and Pareto · percentiles · heavy tails and infinite variance.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free book · Title: Think Stats, chapters 5–6 (distributions and analytic distributions)
Why this one: it teaches you to fit and test a distribution against real data rather than assume one, which is the actual working skill.

DEEPER (optional) — Taleb's technical notes on fat tails are free online; read them only after the hands-on below, and treat the rhetoric as separable from the mathematics.
HANDS-ON · Two worlds, same mean, opposite consequences Generate two datasets of ten thousand values with the same mean: one normal, one lognormal with high variance. Compute mean, median, p90, p99 and max for each.

In the normal world, all five numbers are close and the mean describes almost everyone. In the lognormal world the mean sits above the 70th percentile, the p99 is many multiples of it, and the max is absurd. Same mean. Completely different reality.

Now run the crucial experiment: take one sample of 1,000 from each, and compute the mean. Repeat a hundred times. In the normal world your estimate is stable. In the heavy-tailed world it lurches — because whether your sample happened to contain one extreme value dominates the answer. You have just discovered why sample means from heavy-tailed data are nearly worthless.

Finally, connect it: pull the latency distribution from your own service, or income data from Our World in Data, and identify which world you are in. Both are lognormal-ish. Both are routinely reported as means.

The realisation to reach: “average” is a claim that the distribution is well-behaved, and that claim is usually false and never checked.

CONNECTS TO. Distributed Systems (p99 and tail latency) · Finance (fat tails, risk models that failed in 2008) · Economics (income and wealth distribution) · Geopolitics (war and disaster severity follow power laws) · Business (venture returns).

N5 Law of large numbers and the central limit theorem

Why averages become predictable — and the fine print that everyone omits.

WHAT IT IS. Two results. The law of large numbers says sample averages converge to the true mean. The central limit theorem says the distribution of those sample averages becomes normal, regardless of what you sampled from.

WHY IT EXISTS. These two theorems are why statistics is possible at all — why a poll of two thousand people can describe a nation. They are also routinely applied where their conditions do not hold, which is why so many confident statistical claims are wrong.

DEPENDENCIES. N3, N4.

SUBTOPICS. Convergence and its rate · the √n rule · standard error · confidence intervals · the conditions the CLT actually requires · why heavy tails break it · sampling bias, which no sample size can fix.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free interactive site · Title: Seeing Theory, chapters 3–4
Why this one: the CLT is one of the few things genuinely better understood by watching an animation than by reading a proof. Then re-derive it by simulation yourself.
HANDS-ON · Make the bell curve appear out of nothing Pick the least bell-shaped distribution you can construct — a coin flip, or a bimodal mixture with a hole in the middle. Draw samples of size 30, take the mean, repeat ten thousand times, and plot the histogram of those means. A bell curve appears, from a source that looks nothing like one.

Then break it. Do exactly the same thing sampling from a Pareto distribution with a heavy enough tail. No bell curve appears. The histogram of sample means stays skewed no matter how large the samples get, because the theorem's conditions are not met.

Third experiment, and the most practically useful: take a poll of 1,000 from a population where you deliberately make responders differ from non-responders. Increase the sample size to 100,000. The confidence interval shrinks beautifully around the wrong answer.

The realisation to reach: sample size fixes noise; it does nothing whatsoever about bias. A tight confidence interval is a statement about precision, never about correctness — and conflating the two is how most published statistics mislead.

CONNECTS TO. Software Engineering (benchmark confidence intervals) · Psychology (the replication crisis) · Economics (survey data) · Politics (polling) · World Philosophy (induction).

N6 Processes and Markov chains

Uncertainty that unfolds over time, where the next step depends on where you are now.

WHAT IT IS. A stochastic process is a random quantity indexed by time. A Markov chain is the most useful special case: the next state depends only on the current state, not on the path taken to reach it.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because most real systems have memory of their state but not of their history, and that assumption — memorylessness — makes otherwise intractable problems solvable with arithmetic.

DEPENDENCIES. N4 · a little linear algebra for steady states, though simulation avoids it entirely.

SUBTOPICS. Random walks · gambler's ruin · Markov property · transition matrices · steady state · absorbing states · queues, arrivals and Little's Law · birth–death processes.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free course · Title: Harvard Stat 110 (Blitzstein), the Markov chain lectures, free on YouTube
Why this one: Blitzstein builds the chain from a story rather than a matrix, which is what makes steady state feel inevitable rather than algebraic.
HANDS-ON · The gambler who cannot win, and the queue that explodes First: simulate a fair coin-flip betting game where you start with ₹100, bet ₹1 per round, and stop at zero or at ₹200. It is a completely fair game — expected value zero every round. Now run it against an opponent with effectively unlimited money and no upper stopping point. You go broke with probability approaching one. Fairness at each step does not survive an asymmetry in reserves.

Second, and directly relevant to your work: simulate a single-server queue. Jobs arrive randomly, service takes a random time. Set utilisation to 50% and measure average wait. Then 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, 99%. Plot it. The curve does not rise gently — it goes vertical.

The realisation to reach: a queue at 99% utilisation is not 10% busier than one at 90% — it is a different regime. This single curve explains hospital waiting times, motorway congestion, server latency and why running any system at full capacity is a decision to make it unpredictable. It is also the reason capacity planning exists as a discipline.

CONNECTS TO. Distributed Systems (queueing, backpressure, Little's Law) · Operating Systems (scheduling) · Economics (business cycles) · Finance (random walks and option pricing) · AI (reinforcement learning, diffusion models) · Cars & Transportation (traffic flow).

N7 Decisions under uncertainty

Converting a probability into an action — where mathematics stops and judgment starts.

WHAT IT IS. The frameworks for choosing when outcomes are uncertain: expected value, expected utility, risk of ruin, and the recognition that some decisions are reversible and some are not.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because maximising expected value is provably wrong for any decision you cannot repeat, or any decision where one outcome ends the game. Most consequential decisions are exactly those.

DEPENDENCIES. N3, N4, N6.

SUBTOPICS. Expected value and its limits · utility and diminishing marginal value · risk of ruin and ergodicity · repeated versus one-shot decisions · reversible versus irreversible · the value of information · calibration.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free book · Title: Think Bayes, the decision-analysis chapters
Why this one: it connects posterior distributions to actual choices, which is the step almost every probability resource omits — leaving people able to compute probabilities and unable to use them.

DEEPER (optional) — Kelly's original 1956 paper on optimal bet sizing, freely available and surprisingly readable.
HANDS-ON · The favourable bet that bankrupts you You are offered a repeated bet with a genuine edge: 60% chance to win what you stake, 40% to lose it. Positive expected value on every single round. Simulate a thousand players who each bet their entire bankroll every time, for a hundred rounds.

Almost all of them end with nothing. The average across players is enormous, carried by a handful of survivors — but the median player is ruined. Now simulate again with players betting a fixed fraction, and sweep that fraction from 1% to 100% to find where median wealth peaks.

The realisation to reach: the average outcome across many parallel players is not the outcome you should expect from playing many times yourself. When losses compound and zero is absorbing, expected value is the wrong objective — and this single distinction separates people who survive volatility from people who are eventually destroyed by it, in markets, in careers, and in engineering risk.

CONNECTS TO. Finance (position sizing, leverage, ruin) · Business (bet sizing, optionality) · Psychology (loss aversion, prospect theory) · Geopolitics (escalation and deterrence) · Systems Thinking (irreversibility as a leverage point).
