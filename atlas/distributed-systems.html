Distributed Systems

A distributed system is one in which the failure of a computer you did not know existed can render your own computer unusable. Everything in this domain follows from a single irreducible fact: you cannot tell the difference between a machine that is slow and a machine that is dead.

The single idea. In a single process, failure is total — the program lives or dies. Across a network, failure is partial, asymmetric and undetectable. Every mechanism below exists to make useful guarantees on top of a world where you cannot know what happened, only what you have been told, and possibly not even that.
#partial-failure #consistency #consensus #idempotency #backpressure

00 Why this domain exists

Nobody wants a distributed system. They are accepted as the price of something else.

Distribution is never the goal. It is the cost of wanting one of four things: more capacity than one machine has, survival when a machine dies, physical proximity to users in other continents, or independent teams shipping independently. Every one of those is a real need, and every one of them buys you this entire domain's worth of problems.

Gated byGates

N1 Partial failure depth

The one node that, if truly absorbed, makes the rest of the domain feel inevitable.

WHAT IT IS. The condition in which some components of a system have failed, others have not, and no component can reliably determine which is which.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because a message that receives no reply has three indistinguishable explanations: it never arrived, it arrived and the work was done but the reply was lost, or the other side is merely slow. No timeout can separate these. Every retry, every consensus protocol, and every idempotency key exists because of this ambiguity.

DEPENDENCIES. Networking N4.

SUBTOPICS. The eight fallacies of distributed computing · failure models: crash-stop, crash-recovery, omission, Byzantine · failure detectors, completeness versus accuracy · the Two Generals problem and its impossibility · why "is it up?" is not a well-formed question.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free course · Title: MIT 6.824 Distributed Systems, lectures 1–2 — full videos, notes and labs are free online
Why this one: the best free distributed systems course available, built around implementing real protocols rather than describing them. Lecture 1 establishes partial failure as the organising problem, which is exactly the right framing.

DEEPER (optional) — Martin Kleppmann's Cambridge distributed systems lecture series, free on YouTube, for a more formal treatment of failure models.
HANDS-ON · The request whose outcome you cannot know Build the smallest possible client and server. The server increments a counter and replies. Now make the server sleep for ten seconds after incrementing but before replying.

From the client, set a five-second timeout. The call fails. Now answer the question honestly: did the increment happen? You cannot tell from the client. Retry, and the counter is now 2 when the user asked for 1.

Then run the experiment that makes it visceral. Have the server randomly choose one of three behaviours: crash before the work, crash after the work but before replying, or be slow. From the client, log what you observed. Across a hundred trials, produce a table of what you saw versus what actually happened. They do not correlate.

Finally, make it real: pull the network cable or run iptables -A INPUT -j DROP on the server mid-request. The client hangs — not fails, hangs — because a dropped packet produces silence, and silence is indistinguishable from thinking.

The realisation to reach: a timeout is not a way of detecting failure. It is a way of giving up, and the decision of what to do next must be made without knowing what happened. Everything else in this domain is a strategy for acting under that ignorance.

CONNECTS TO. Probability N2 (a failure detector is a test with false positives, and base rates apply) · Human Behavior (acting under irreducible uncertainty) · Geopolitics (signalling when you cannot verify intent).

N2 Time and ordering

There is no global "now", and clocks lie in ways that produce silent data loss.

WHAT IT IS. The study of how to order events across machines when no shared clock exists, and the distinction between physical time and causal ordering.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because "which write happened last?" is the central question of replication, and physical timestamps answer it incorrectly. Clocks drift, get corrected backwards, and differ between machines by more than the duration of the operations you are ordering.

DEPENDENCIES. N1 · Networking N8.

SUBTOPICS. Clock drift and skew · NTP and its accuracy limits · monotonic versus wall-clock time · happens-before · Lamport clocks · vector clocks · last-write-wins and the data it silently discards · hybrid logical clocks · Spanner's TrueTime and buying certainty with hardware.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free paper · Title: Lamport, "Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events in a Distributed System" (1978), freely available
Why this one: it is short, it is the origin of the idea, and it is one of the few genuinely foundational papers that a non-specialist can read directly and understand. Reading the original is faster than reading explanations of it.

DEEPER (optional) — Kleppmann's "Please stop calling databases CP or AP" and his clock-related posts, free, for how this bites in production.
HANDS-ON · Lose a write with a correct clock Run two processes writing to a shared store using last-write-wins with wall-clock timestamps. Now skew one machine's clock forward by two seconds — on a VM this is one command, and in reality it happens through NTP correction, virtualisation pauses and leap seconds.

The machine with the fast clock now wins every conflict, permanently, regardless of what actually happened first. Writes from the other machine vanish with no error and no log entry. Count how many you lose over a thousand operations.

Now implement a Lamport clock instead — a counter incremented on every event and carried in every message — and re-run. Causally related events are now correctly ordered. Then find the limitation: Lamport clocks cannot tell you whether two events were concurrent or merely unordered. Implement vector clocks and watch concurrency become detectable.

The realisation to reach: time is not for ordering; causality is. A timestamp tells you what a machine believed, and machines believe different things. Every "last write wins" system is silently discarding data, and the only question is how much.

CONNECTS TO. Databases N5 (snapshot isolation needs an ordering) · Physics (simultaneity is not absolute — the analogy is closer than it appears) · Data Engineering (event time vs processing time).

N3 Replication

Keeping more than one copy, and paying for it.

WHAT IT IS. Maintaining copies of data on multiple machines, and the protocols that decide where writes go, how copies converge, and what a reader may see in the meantime.

WHY IT EXISTS. Three distinct reasons that are often conflated: surviving machine failure, serving reads at higher volume, and serving reads closer to users. The right architecture depends on which of the three you actually want.

DEPENDENCIES. N1, N2 · Databases N4.

SUBTOPICS. Synchronous versus asynchronous replication · leader-follower · failover and split brain · multi-leader and conflict resolution · leaderless replication and quorums · the R + W > N condition · read repair and anti-entropy · replication lag.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free paper · Title: Amazon's Dynamo paper (2007), sections on replication and quorums
Why this one: free, and it is unusually explicit about what was traded away and which problems were pushed up into the application. That honesty is rare and instructive.
HANDS-ON · Read your own write and fail Set up PostgreSQL with a streaming replica — two containers, fifteen minutes. Write a row to the primary and immediately read it from the replica in the same script. Sometimes it is there. Sometimes it is not. Run it a thousand times and measure the distribution of the lag window.

That distribution is not symmetric. Plot it and recognise the shape from Probability N4 — a heavy tail, where the p99 lag is many multiples of the median. Now add load to the primary and watch the tail stretch.

Then build the fix and feel its cost: route reads for a user to the primary for N seconds after they write. You have implemented read-your-writes consistency, and you have also just made your replicas useless for the users who are most active.

Finally, simulate failover: kill the primary and promote the replica while writes are in flight. Count the acknowledged writes that no longer exist. Asynchronous replication means "acknowledged" and "durable" are different words.

The realisation to reach: replication does not give you a copy. It gives you a copy of the past, and the distance into the past is a distribution, not a number.

CONNECTS TO. Databases N4 · Probability N4 (lag is heavy-tailed) · Infrastructure (multi-region architecture) · Networking N8 (the lag floor is the speed of light).

N4 Consistency models

A precise vocabulary for what a reader is permitted to observe.

WHAT IT IS. A hierarchy of guarantees — from linearizable, which behaves as if there were one copy and one timeline, down to eventual, which promises only that copies stop diverging if writes stop.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because stronger guarantees cost round trips, and round trips are bounded by physics. A consistency model is a purchase decision: how much latency will you pay for how much simplicity in your application code.

DEPENDENCIES. N2, N3 · Databases N5.

SUBTOPICS. Linearizability · sequential and causal consistency · eventual consistency · the session guarantees: read-your-writes, monotonic reads, monotonic writes · why "strong" and "weak" are marketing terms · CRDTs as convergence without coordination.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free reference · Title: Jepsen's "Consistency Models" page (jepsen.io/consistency)
Why this one: free, and it presents the models as a lattice with the implication arrows drawn explicitly — which turns a confusing pile of adjectives into a structure you can reason about. Then read the Jepsen analyses of databases you use.
HANDS-ON · Break an application with a legal database Take a two-node replicated store with eventual consistency and build a trivial application on it: a user updates their profile photo, then loads their profile page.

With reads load-balanced across replicas, the user sometimes sees their old photo. They will refresh, see the new one, refresh again, see the old one. The database is behaving exactly as specified. The bug is that you assumed a guarantee you never purchased.

Then work up the lattice and measure the cost of each step. Sticky routing gives read-your-writes almost free. Quorum reads with R + W > N give strong reads for the cost of extra round trips — measure them. Full linearizability requires consensus on every read, and the latency will be startling.

Finally, implement a CRDT — a grow-only counter or an observed-remove set — and watch two replicas converge after partition without any coordination at all. Then find its limitation: try to express "bank balance must never go negative" as a CRDT. You cannot.

The realisation to reach: consistency is a price list, not a quality rating. Eventual consistency is not a broken version of strong consistency; it is a different purchase, and some invariants simply cannot be bought at that price.

CONNECTS TO. Databases N5 · Networking N8 (round trips are the currency) · Economics (a genuine cost-benefit decision) · Data Engineering (eventual consistency in pipelines).

N5 Consensus

Getting a group of unreliable machines to agree on one value, forever.

WHAT IT IS. A protocol by which a set of nodes agrees on a single value or an ordered log of values, such that the decision survives failures and no two nodes ever disagree about a committed entry.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because almost every hard distributed problem — leader election, lock services, configuration, atomic commit, membership — reduces to agreement. Solve consensus once and you can build the rest on top.

DEPENDENCIES. N1, N2, N3.

SUBTOPICS. The safety and liveness properties · why majorities work — any two majorities intersect · Raft: leader election, log replication, commitment, safety argument · terms as logical time · Paxos and why Raft was written · FLP impossibility and what it does and does not forbid · Byzantine fault tolerance.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free paper + free labs · Title: "In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm" (the Raft paper), implemented via MIT 6.824's labs 2A–2C
Why this one: the paper was explicitly written to be understandable, and the 6.824 labs are free, self-checking, and brutal in the right way. Consensus cannot be learned by reading.

DEEPER (optional) — raft.github.io's interactive visualisation, free, to watch elections happen before you implement them.
HANDS-ON · Implement leader election, then try to break it Implement Raft leader election alone — no log replication yet. Three nodes, randomised election timeouts, request-vote messages. Get it electing a leader, then kill the leader and watch a new one appear.

Now attack your own implementation. Make elections deterministic by removing the randomised timeout — you will get split votes forever, and you have discovered why the randomisation is not an implementation detail but a core part of the algorithm.

Then partition the cluster 2–1 and verify the minority cannot elect anyone. Then partition 1–1–1 and watch nothing get elected at all, which is correct behaviour — the system chooses unavailability over disagreement.

Finally, the experiment that teaches the safety argument: allow a node to vote twice in one term. Elect two leaders. Watch them both accept writes. You have manufactured split brain, and the single line preventing it was "vote once per term".

The realisation to reach: consensus is not about being clever. It is about the intersection property of majorities, and every rule in the protocol exists to preserve one invariant. Removing any of them produces divergence within seconds.

CONNECTS TO. Indian Politics (quorum, majority rule, anti-defection) · Human Behavior (coordination and commitment) · Databases N4 (atomic commit) · Infrastructure (etcd, ZooKeeper).

N6 Partitioning

Splitting data across machines, and the hot spot you did not anticipate.

WHAT IT IS. Dividing a dataset so each machine holds a subset, plus the scheme for mapping a key to a machine and rebalancing when membership changes.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because replication solves availability but not capacity — every replica holds everything. Growing beyond one machine's storage or write throughput requires splitting.

DEPENDENCIES. N3 · Software Engineering N1 (hashing).

SUBTOPICS. Hash versus range partitioning · consistent hashing and virtual nodes · rebalancing without full reshuffle · hot keys and skew · request routing · secondary indexes: local versus global · why cross-partition transactions are expensive.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free paper · Title: The Dynamo paper's consistent hashing section, alongside the original Karger et al. consistent hashing paper (both free)
Why this one: consistent hashing is one of the few ideas where the original paper is shorter and clearer than the tutorials, and Dynamo shows the production adaptations.
HANDS-ON · Add one node and move the whole dataset Distribute a million keys across four nodes using hash(key) % 4. Now add a fifth node and count how many keys must move. The answer is roughly 80% — nearly the entire dataset, for a 25% capacity increase.

Now implement consistent hashing and repeat. Roughly 20% move — only the keys that should. Plot both against node count and see the difference in shape, not just magnitude.

Then find consistent hashing's own problem: with only a few nodes on the ring, the load is badly uneven. Measure the ratio between the busiest and least busy node. Add virtual nodes — 100 ring positions per physical node — and watch it flatten. You have just discovered why every real implementation uses them.

Finally, create a hot key deliberately: make one key receive 30% of all traffic, as happens with a celebrity account or a popular product. No partitioning scheme helps, because the unit of partitioning is the key, and one key cannot be split. Then solve it with key salting and observe what you gave up: you can no longer read that key with a single request.

The realisation to reach: partitioning distributes data, not load. Skew is the normal case, not the exception, and it follows the same power-law shape as everything else in Probability N4.

CONNECTS TO. Probability N4 (skew is a power law) · Data Engineering (partitioning strategy, the small-files problem) · Geography (data locality and residency).

N7 Flow control and idempotency

The node that prevents your system from destroying itself.

WHAT IT IS. The mechanisms that keep a system stable under load and correct under retry: timeouts, backoff, jitter, idempotency keys, bounded queues, load shedding and circuit breakers.

WHY IT EXISTS. Because N1 makes retries necessary, and retries make overload self-amplifying. A struggling system that is retried becomes a dead system. Most large outages are this loop, not the original fault.

DEPENDENCIES. N1 · Probability N6 (queueing) · Networking N4.

SUBTOPICS. Timeouts and budget propagation · exponential backoff and why jitter is required · retry storms and metastable failure · idempotency keys · exactly-once as an illusion, and effectively-once as the achievable version · bounded queues · load shedding · circuit breakers · backpressure as a feedback loop.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free article · Title: Amazon Builders' Library — "Timeouts, retries, and backoff with jitter" and "Using load shedding to avoid overload" (both free)
Why this one: written by engineers who caused and then fixed these outages at scale, free, short, and specific about the numbers rather than the principles.
HANDS-ON · Build a retry storm, then survive it Run a service that handles 100 requests per second comfortably. Send 120. Latency rises. Now add a client that retries three times on timeout — offered load becomes 360 requests per second, latency rises further, more requests time out, more retries fire. The system does not recover even when you reduce the original load back to 80.

That last part is the important one. You have produced a metastable failure: a system stuck in a bad equilibrium sustained entirely by its own retries. Removing the original cause does not fix it.

Now fix it in three stages and measure each. Add exponential backoff — better, but clients still synchronise into waves. Add jitter — the waves disappear, and the plot is dramatic. Add a bounded queue with load shedding, and watch the system serve 100 requests per second successfully while rejecting the rest immediately, instead of failing all 360 slowly.

Finally, the correctness half: with retries enabled, verify your counter is wrong. Add an idempotency key and verify it is right. Retries and correctness are not separate concerns.

The realisation to reach: rejecting work quickly is a feature. A system that accepts everything and serves none of it has chosen the worst possible outcome, and choosing it is the default if nobody designs otherwise.

CONNECTS TO. Systems Thinking (this is a positive feedback loop, and jitter is desynchronisation) · Probability N6 (queue explosion near saturation) · Economics (congestion pricing) · Networking N4 (TCP congestion control solves the same problem).

N8 The trade-off laws

CAP, PACELC, and how to use them honestly.

WHAT IT IS. A small set of results stating what cannot be had simultaneously: CAP (under partition, choose consistency or availability) and PACELC (and even without partition, choose latency or consistency).

WHY IT EXISTS. To stop engineers from looking for a system that has everything. The honest version of these results is narrower and more useful than the popular version, and PACELC is the more practically relevant of the two because partitions are rare while latency is constant.

DEPENDENCIES. N4, N5.

SUBTOPICS. CAP stated precisely, and the three common misstatements · why "CA" is not a real category · PACELC · availability as a spectrum rather than a boolean · what real systems choose and why · reading a Jepsen report.

PRIMARY RESOURCE
Type: Free article · Title: Martin Kleppmann, "Please stop calling databases CP or AP" (free)
Why this one: it dismantles the popular version of CAP carefully and replaces it with something you can actually use, which is more valuable than learning the folk version first and unlearning it later.
HANDS-ON · Make the choice yourself, with a real partition Take your three-node Raft cluster from N5. Partition it 2–1 with firewall rules. On the majority side, writes succeed. On the minority side, they hang or fail. You have observed a CP system choosing consistency and sacrificing availability — for the minority.

Now do the same with an eventually consistent store configured for W=1. Both sides accept writes. Nothing fails. Heal the partition and inspect the damage: conflicting values, and some resolution rule silently choosing a winner. Count what was lost.

Then write down, for three real applications you can name — a bank ledger, a shopping cart, a like-count — which choice each should make, and defend it. The answers differ, and the fact that they differ is the entire content of this node.

The realisation to reach: CAP is not a classification of databases. It is a decision you make per operation, and mature systems make different choices for different operations within the same application.

CONNECTS TO. Economics (constrained optimisation) · Business (SLAs are this made contractual) · Systems Thinking (no free lunch in a constrained system).
